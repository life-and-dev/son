# Known by Jesus: Must We Hear His Voice?

Jesus’ words in Matthew 7 can be frightening:

> “I never **knew** you; depart from me, you workers of **lawlessness**.” — Matthew 7:23 (ESV)

If Jesus is truly human and we cannot sit with him as his first disciples did, how can he know us personally? Must we hear him answer before we can know that we belong to him?

The warning deserves its full force. It does not, however, name the absence of a private conversation as the reason Jesus rejects these people. Nor does Jesus’ humanity establish that communication with the risen Lord is impossible. Scripture describes a real relationship with him while leaving some questions about how he receives appeals unanswered.

## What Matthew 7 Actually Warns About

Matthew 7:15–20 first warns against false prophets and says their fruit reveals them. Jesus then says that calling him Lord does not, by itself, mean entering the kingdom of heaven; he contrasts it with doing the will of his Father in heaven (Matthew 7:21). In verse 22, the people facing rejection claim to have prophesied, cast out demons, and done mighty works in Jesus’ name. The passage does not say whether each claimed work happened. Their claims do not settle whether Jesus acknowledges them.

Jesus’ reply names **lawlessness**, not failure to hear an audible reply from him. He closes the teaching with two builders: one hears his words and does them; the other hears but does not do them (Matthew 7:24–27). That contrast helps explain the warning. Saying the right title and claiming striking deeds cannot replace a life responsive to what Jesus teaches.

The word *knew* need not mean that Jesus lacked information about the people speaking to him. In the LORD’s address to Israel, “You only have I **known** of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your **iniquities**” (Amos 3:2, ESV). The LORD was not ignorant of other nations. In that setting, knowing Israel describes a particular relationship, and even that relationship does not excuse wrongdoing. Amos does not supply a fixed definition for every use of *know*, but it shows why relational recognition makes sense in Matthew 7.

Matthew 7 therefore gives no test requiring a private voice, a Nicene confession, or a record of extraordinary works. This observation limits what the passage says; it does not, by itself, settle every question about who Jesus is.

## Knowing and Being Known by Jesus

Jesus describes more than knowledge *about* an absent teacher. “My sheep **hear** my voice, and I **know** them, and they **follow** me” (John 10:27, ESV). He says that his own know him, that he gives his sheep eternal life, and that the Father has given them to him (John 10:14, 27–29). Their relationship with Jesus is real and mutual, under the Father’s authority.

John 10 also cautions against equating hearing with receiving a private audible message. People questioning Jesus heard him speak in person, yet he said they did not believe (John 10:24–26). In the description of his sheep, hearing, being known, and following belong together. The point is not that the sound of his voice, by itself, makes someone his follower.

Jesus anticipated people who would believe in him through his disciples’ word (John 17:20). Those later believers would not need to repeat the first disciples’ face-to-face experience for their faith to be genuine. Peter likewise addresses believers who love Jesus though they have not seen him (1 Peter 1:8–9). Apostolic testimony brings us Jesus’ words; trusting him, loving him, and responding to those words are not merely collecting facts about a dead teacher. First John 2:3–6 also tests claims to know him against keeping his commandments and walking as Jesus walked. None of these passages makes a privately heard voice the certificate of belonging.

This does not turn Jesus into a name for the church or make fellowship with other believers a substitute for him. It identifies ways Scripture itself speaks of a relationship with the living Lord. For the related question of how the Father’s Spirit and fellow believers sustain that fellowship, see [Fellowship with Jesus](fellowship.draft.md).

## Can a Human Jesus Hear Us?

Jesus’ real humanity does not prove that nobody can address him now. Trinitarians also affirm his humanity, so the fact that he is human does not, on its own, decide the wider dispute about his nature. The question here is narrower: does Scripture treat an appeal to the risen Jesus as impossible?

Stephen, seeing Jesus at God’s right hand, says, “Lord **Jesus**, receive my spirit” (Acts 7:55–60, ESV). This is an actual appeal to Jesus, not merely a report that Stephen thought about him. Stephen had a vision, however, and Acts records no spoken reply from Jesus. The scene establishes neither that everyone will have such a vision nor that every appeal receives a private answer.

Jesus also says, “If you ask **me** anything in my name, I will do it” (John 14:14, ESV). The ESV notes that some manuscripts omit *me*. On the reading printed in the ESV, Jesus can be the one asked and the one who acts; the surrounding passage says that the Father is glorified in the Son (John 14:13). Paul’s repeated plea to the Lord, followed by an answer about grace and the power of Christ, offers further support for addressing Jesus, although the addressee of the plea is not explicitly named in those words (2 Corinthians 12:8–10).

These passages do not explain how the risen human Jesus receives requests, how many he receives, or whether every request reaches him. His resurrection and exaltation by the Father do not automatically make him independently omniscient or present in every place. Equally, an unexplained mechanism is not evidence that speaking to him is impossible. Addressing Jesus and hearing him answer aloud are different matters. [Prayer to Jesus](../shema/prayer-to-jesus.draft.md) examines that evidence more fully.

## Faithfulness without a Performance Quota

Matthew 7 calls for doing the Father’s will and putting Jesus’ words into practice. It does not set a number of good deeds by which someone earns recognition. Ephesians 2:8–10 holds together salvation by grace through faith, not by works, and a life made for good works. Obedience matters; it is not a payment that forces Jesus to accept us.

Nor does every failure mean that Jesus’ words in Matthew 7 are addressed to that person. First John warns against claiming to be without sin and points those who sin to confession and to Jesus Christ as advocate with the Father (1 John 1:8–2:2). This is not permission to dismiss lawlessness. It is a reason not to confuse repentance with sinless performance.

Fear itself cannot certify that someone belongs to Jesus, and an absence of an audible answer cannot certify rejection. The question Matthew 7 presses is whether a claim to follow Jesus is joined to receiving and doing his teaching, rather than resting on a title or impressive claims. ## Conclusion

[Matthew’s warning](#what-matthew-7-actually-warns-about) concerns lawlessness despite a claim to serve Jesus, not failure to secure a private conversation. [Jesus’ account of his sheep](#knowing-and-being-known-by-jesus) joins being known to hearing and following him, including among later believers reached through apostolic witness. [Appeals to the risen Jesus](#can-a-human-jesus-hear-us) challenge the claim that his humanity makes communication impossible, without revealing how every request is received or promising an audible answer. [Grace and obedience](#faithfulness-without-a-performance-quota) belong together: neither claimed achievements nor anxious efforts to be perfect earn his recognition.
