# Could Refusing Jesus Worship Be Idolatry?

If Jesus is God, refusing him worship would be a grave mistake. If he is not God, treating him as a second Almighty God would also be a grave mistake. Fear of idolatry therefore deserves more than an argument over the word *worship*. The question is what allegiance God requires and what honour Jesus himself receives in Scripture.

The answer here is not to withhold honour from Jesus. The Father appointed him Lord, raised him from the dead and requires people to honour him. Nor should concern about idolatry become an accusation that Trinitarians worship three gods. Trinitarians affirm Jesus’ humanity and distinguish the Father from the Son. The disagreement concerns whether the Son also shares the identity of the one God.

## God Requires Exclusive Allegiance

God forbids other gods and the worship and service of their images (Exodus 20:3–5). Israel must fear and serve the LORD rather than follow the gods of surrounding peoples (Deuteronomy 6:13–15). When tempted to redirect worship, Jesus appeals to this command: worship and service belong to God (Matthew 4:10). His reply does not make devotion to God optional once the Messiah arrives.

Paul addresses a world with “many gods and many lords” before saying that, for believers, there is “**one God, the Father**” and “**one Lord, Jesus Christ**” (1 Corinthians 8:4–6). Those phrases must be read together, not detached from the contrast with rival gods and lords. *On the reading advanced here, Paul names the Father as the one God and Jesus as the one Lord through whom God works.* *A Trinitarian reading understands Paul’s description of Jesus as including him within the one God’s identity.* Neither reading should turn Jesus into an imaginary substitute for the person Scripture describes.

## The Father Requires Honour for His Son

Jesus says the Father entrusted judgment to the Son so “**that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father**” (John 5:19–23). He goes on to say that failing to honour the Son fails to honour the Father who sent him. This is not permission to acknowledge Jesus politely while disregarding his authority. The Father gives the Son life and authority to execute judgment (John 5:24–27).

Peter likewise announces that God made the crucified Jesus both Lord and Christ (Acts 2:36). Paul says God highly exalted him, so that every knee bows and every tongue confesses Jesus Christ as Lord “**to the glory of God the Father**” (Philippians 2:9–11). Jesus’ lordship demands a response; it is not a rival lordship chosen in place of God’s command.

*The distinction between the Father who appoints and the Son who receives authority fits the reading that Jesus is God’s real human Messiah.* That distinction alone does not settle the Trinity: Trinitarians also affirm it, and receiving authority does not, by itself, disprove their account of Jesus’ divinity. The claim made here is narrower. Honouring Jesus as the Father’s appointed Lord is obedience to the Father, not allegiance to an unauthorised god.

## Bowing to a King Does Not Settle His Identity

At the close of David’s reign, the assembly “**paid homage to the LORD and to the king**” (1 Chronicles 29:20). The LORD and the human king are not the same being, even though both receive homage in the account. Thus an act of bowing does not, by itself, identify its recipient as Almighty God.

That example does **not** prove every instance of worship directed towards Jesus is merely civil respect. The risen Jesus is not an ordinary king. The women bow before him, as do the disciples, and he declares, “**All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me**” (Matthew 28:9, 17–20). These scenes give Trinitarians reason to see more than conventional homage. Equally, Jesus’ stated reception of authority matters to the reading that sees him as God’s uniquely authorised Son. Neither a single English translation of *worship* nor a single ancient word can decide his identity apart from its context.

## The Strongest Worship Texts Need Hearing

In Isaiah, the LORD calls the ends of the earth to turn to him because there is no other God, and declares that every knee will bow to him (Isaiah 45:22–23). Philippians applies the language of every knee bowing to Jesus (Philippians 2:9–11). This is serious evidence for a Trinitarian reading, not a resemblance to pass over. *The reading advanced here understands the universal confession of God’s exalted Messiah as honour that reaches its stated end in the Father’s glory.* It does not reduce the confession to ordinary respect or claim that the echo alone proves this reading over its rival.

Thomas provides another serious challenge. He directly addresses the risen Jesus: “**My Lord and my God!**” (John 20:28). The words cannot be dismissed as a remark addressed to someone else. Earlier in the chapter Jesus calls the Father “**my God and your God**” (John 20:17); John then states his purpose so readers may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God (John 20:31). *A representative or royal use of “God” is one possible reading of Thomas’s address,* but appealing to such a use in Psalm 45:6–7 is not proof that Thomas meant it. These verses deserve the fuller treatment in [Thomas’s witness](../son-as-god/witnesses/thomas-s-witness.draft.md); related questions about [worship of the Son](../son-as-god/worship.md) and [the Word](../word.md) also require their own contexts.

## Honour the Son Without Replacing the Father

Paul describes Christ reigning until his enemies are subdued, then handing the kingdom to God the Father, so that God may be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:24–28). Jesus teaches his disciples to pray to their Father (Matthew 6:9) and speaks of asking the Father in his name (John 16:23–27). Access to the Father is through the Son (Ephesians 2:18), not around him.

The Father was not absent or unknowable until Jesus appeared. Israel heard God’s voice without seeing a form (Deuteronomy 4:12, 15). Moses spoke with the LORD, though he could not see God’s face without dying (Exodus 33:9–11, 20). These accounts do not remove the need for the Son; they prevent a false choice between Jesus and a Father who could not be known at all. ## Conclusion

The risk of idolatry is real, but fear must not make honouring Jesus optional. [God’s exclusive claim](#god-requires-exclusive-allegiance) rules out a rival Almighty God; [the Father’s command](#the-father-requires-honour-for-his-son) requires honour for Jesus as Lord and Son. Bowing before Jesus and confessing his lordship must be taken seriously, especially where [Scripture echoes God’s own claims](#the-strongest-worship-texts-need-hearing). *On the reading advanced here, honouring the Father’s appointed Son while giving undivided divine allegiance to the Father is obedience, not idolatry.* This answer does not by itself settle every text about Jesus’ identity, nor does it pronounce Trinitarians idolaters. The governing question is what God commands and what Jesus claims, not which side can use the word *worship* most forcefully.
