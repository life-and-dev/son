# Did God Appear as Jesus?

God truly makes himself known in Jesus. The disputed step is whether this means that God Almighty personally appeared *as* Jesus. Scripture often reveals God through someone who bears his authority without becoming identical to him. At the burning bush, “the angel of the LORD” appears in the flame, yet the encounter is narrated as God speaking (Exodus 3:2–6); Stephen likewise says that an angel appeared and that God sent Moses through that angel (Acts 7:30–35). Agency can make God genuinely present without collapsing God and his agent into one person.

The New Testament presents Jesus as the supreme case: the human Messiah is God’s image, God’s commissioned Savior, and the bearer of God’s glory. *That affirmative agency pattern gives good reason to distinguish Jesus from God Almighty even where Jesus receives extraordinarily high language.*

## The image and firstborn

> “He is the **image** of the invisible God, the **firstborn** of all creation.” — Colossians 1:15 (ESV)

An **image** makes someone else known. Genesis says that humans are made in God’s image (Genesis 1:26–27), and Paul can call man “the image and glory of God” (1 Corinthians 11:7, ESV). Christ is not merely one image among many: he is the flawless, authoritative image in whom God’s character and saving purpose are displayed. Yet “image **of** the invisible God” still distinguishes the representative from the God represented.

Nor should **firstborn** be reduced to “first thing created.” The title can express rank, inheritance, and royal supremacy. The LORD says of David, “I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth” (Psalm 89:27, ESV), although David was not Jesse’s first child. Colossians itself explains the goal: Christ is “the firstborn from the dead, that in everything he might be preeminent” (Colossians 1:18, ESV). His resurrection inaugurates his rule over the reconciled creation.

The strongest opposing point is the hymn’s creation language: “by him all things were created,” all things were created “through him and for him,” and “he is before all things” (Colossians 1:16–17, ESV). A Trinitarian naturally reads this as the eternal Son personally creating before becoming human. That reading must not be weakened into a claim that “firstborn” itself means uncreated; its real force lies in the whole passage.

A Unitarian answer begins with Paul’s language of agency. Elsewhere Paul names “one God, the Father, **from whom** are all things” and “one Lord, Jesus Christ, **through whom** are all things” (1 Corinthians 8:6, ESV). The Father remains the ultimate source; Christ is the Lord through whom God’s purpose is realized. Colossians also says that God was pleased for fullness to dwell in Christ and that God reconciles through the blood of Christ’s cross (Colossians 1:19–22). Indwelling, commission, and mediation are positive relations between God and his Messiah.

Some readers understand Colossians 1 primarily as new-creation language; others understand Christ as the foreordained goal and agent of the original creation. Neither option can be proved merely by the word **firstborn**. The secure point is narrower: even personal preexistence or participation in creation would not by itself identify Jesus as God Almighty. Paul calls him God’s image, places him in a relation of agency to God, and roots his supremacy in God’s reconciling work through his death and resurrection.

## Titus 2:13 and “our great God”

> “Waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great **God and Savior Jesus Christ**.” — Titus 2:13 (ESV)

This is one of the strongest verses for directly calling Jesus God. The [NET Bible note on Titus 2:13](https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Titus&chapter=2&verse=13) applies the Granville Sharp rule and takes “our great God and Savior” as referring to the one person Jesus Christ. Because the two singular personal titles share one article and are joined by “and,” the single-referent reading has substantial grammatical force. An honest Unitarian interpretation cannot pretend that the Greek plainly requires two persons.

Two replies remain possible. First, a minority reading understands the verse as the appearing of God’s glory in Jesus, or as coordinated references to God and Christ. That fits biblical manifestation language, but it is grammatically less straightforward and must not be called certain.

Second, even if Titus here calls Jesus “God and Savior,” that usage must be read within the letter and the wider scriptural category of representation. Titus repeatedly distinguishes “God our Savior” from “Christ Jesus our Savior” (Titus 1:3–4; 3:4–6). God pours out the Spirit “through Jesus Christ our Savior” (Titus 3:6, ESV). The same saving title applies to God as source and to Jesus as the one through whom God saves.

Scripture can also address God’s royal agent with divine language while distinguishing him from his God. Psalm 45 says to the king, “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever,” and immediately adds, “God, your God, has anointed you” (Psalm 45:6–7, ESV). Hebrews applies both lines to the Son (Hebrews 1:8–9). Therefore, even the strong single-referent reading of Titus 2:13 need not mean “Jesus is God Almighty.” It may be an exceptional honorific title for the uniquely authorized bearer of God’s saving rule.

## Appearance, glory, and agency

The Old Testament says that the LORD alone is Savior (Isaiah 43:11), yet the New Testament says that God exalted Jesus “as Leader and Savior” (Acts 5:30–31, ESV). This is not competition between two independent saviors. God saves through the Messiah he appointed.

The same pattern explains appearance language. Jesus can say that whoever has seen him has seen the Father because his words and works come from the Father dwelling in him (John 14:9–10). He does not say that he is the Father; in the same discourse he says he is going to the Father and that the Father is greater than he is (John 14:12, 28). Hebrews calls the Son the radiance of God’s glory and exact imprint of his nature (Hebrews 1:3). Revelation calls the Lamb worthy because he was slain and by his blood ransomed people **for God** (Revelation 5:9). In each case, Jesus reveals God perfectly while remaining related to God as Son, image, servant, and agent.

## A bounded conclusion

Colossians gives Jesus cosmic supremacy but calls him the image of the invisible God and describes God’s fullness dwelling in him. Titus 2:13 probably refers both “God” and “Saviour” to Jesus under its strongest grammatical reading; that evidence should be admitted rather than evaded. Yet Titus itself distinguishes God the saving source from Christ the saving mediator, and Scripture allows God’s anointed king to bear divine language while still having a God over him.

*The best cumulative reading is that God appeared, reconciled, and saved through Jesus, his supreme human image and Messiah, not that God Almighty appeared as Jesus.* This conclusion remains bounded: Titus 2:13 is genuinely disputed, and no single grammatical observation universally resolves the New Testament’s ontological question.
