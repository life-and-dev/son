# Did Nebuchadnezzar See Jesus Before His Human Birth?

Daniel 3 records an extraordinary deliverance: four men appear alive in a furnace meant to kill three. Some readers see the fourth figure as Jesus before his human birth. The passage deserves a careful reading in its Aramaic wording, its royal setting, and its own explanation of how God rescued faithful servants.

This article argues a Unitarian case. Daniel 3 does not identify the fourth figure as Jesus, and it cannot identify Jesus as God Almighty or God the Father. The chapter instead presents the God of Israel acting for his servants through an angelic messenger. That conclusion does not depend on claiming that an angel can never be Christ, nor does it deny that Trinitarian theology distinguishes Father and Son.

## Daniel’s Fourth Figure

Before the furnace is heated, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego confess that their God is able to deliver them. They also refuse to make their obedience conditional on rescue: “But if not,” they will not serve Nebuchadnezzar’s gods or worship his image (Daniel 3:16-18). The story therefore centres on the God of Israel, the servants’ faithfulness, and God’s power to save.

After the men are thrown in, Nebuchadnezzar sees four men walking unharmed. He says, “the appearance of the fourth is like **a son of the gods**” (Daniel 3:25, ESV). Daniel 2:4b-7:28 is written in Aramaic. The phrase *lebar-’elahin* means “like a son of the gods” or “like a divine being” in this pagan king’s speech, not “the Son of God” as a narrator’s Christian identification. The [Daniel 3:25 interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/daniel/3-25.htm) displays the wording. It names neither Jesus nor Messiah.

Nebuchadnezzar is the only speaker who describes the fourth figure. His comparison reflects Babylonian religious language and should not be made to say more than the king says.

## The Chapter’s Identification

Nebuchadnezzar later says that God “sent his **angel** and delivered his servants” (Daniel 3:28, ESV). This is again the king’s speech, not a separate narrator identification. The [Daniel 3:28 interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/daniel/3-28.htm) displays *mal’akeh*, “his angel.” The same speaker who compared the fourth figure to a divine being now credits Israel’s God with sending his angel.

The verse does not name Jesus, Messiah, God Almighty, or God the Father. Nor does **angel** by itself make a later Christophany inference impossible. A Trinitarian reader may infer that the pre-incarnate Son appeared as God’s messenger, but Daniel 3 neither makes that textual identification nor excludes it as a later theological inference.

The wider Old Testament pattern makes angelic deliverance intelligible. The LORD sent an angel ahead of Israel (Exodus 23:20-23), and Daniel says that God sent his angel in the lions’ den (Daniel 6:22). God remains the one who saves and sends.

## The Strongest Case for a Pre-Incarnate Son

The strongest opposing argument does not rest only on the word **angel**. It joins the KJV’s **the Son of God** in Daniel 3:25 with a wider Christian conviction that the Son existed before his birth from Mary. On that reading, the fourth figure visibly shares in God’s saving presence. The later New Testament speaks of the Son’s glory before the world existed and of creation in relation to him (John 17:5; Colossians 1:15-17). For readers who already hold that the Son personally existed before his human birth, Daniel 3 can seem a fitting earlier appearance.

This is a coherent Trinitarian interpretation. The distinction it draws between Father and Son is real within Trinitarian theology. Therefore, a Unitarian case must not argue merely that the figure is distinct from God or that Jesus is distinct from the Father. Trinitarians affirm that distinction. Nor does pre-existence, if established from other passages, automatically make the Son the Father.

The question here is more limited: what does Daniel 3 itself establish? Its speaker calls the figure **a son of the gods** in the ESV, then identifies God’s deliverance through “his angel.” The account neither says that this angel is the eternal Son nor supplies a basis for calling him God Almighty. A later theological conclusion may be brought to the passage, but it is not the passage’s stated conclusion.

## The Human Messiah and God’s Agent

The Old Testament’s main messianic expectation is a human ruler and servant whom God appoints. God promises David, “I will raise up your offspring after you,” and says of that descendant, “I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son” (2 Samuel 7:12,14). Isaiah describes the LORD’s servant as one upheld and chosen by the LORD (Isaiah 42:1). Daniel sees “one like a son of man” receive dominion from the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:13-14). These texts distinguish the LORD from his chosen king, servant, or human-like representative.

Psalm 110:1 is especially direct: “The LORD says to my Lord: ‘Sit at my right hand’” (ESV). The enthroned lord receives authority from the LORD. *In a Unitarian reading, this is the pattern fulfilled in Jesus: God exalts his Messiah rather than the Messiah being the God who exalts him.* Jesus himself cites this psalm when discussing the Messiah (Mark 12:35-37).

The New Testament repeatedly speaks in this agency pattern. Peter calls Jesus “a man attested to you by God” and says God raised him up (Acts 2:22,24). Paul writes, “there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). After the resurrection, Jesus says, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (John 20:17). These passages do not describe Jesus as God Almighty or as God the Father. They describe the Messiah in relation to the one God who sends, raises, gives authority, and is Jesus’ God.

This does not require denying every claim of pre-existence before considering it. It establishes a separate point: pre-existence is not identical to being God Almighty. Even if someone concludes that the Son existed before Mary, Daniel 3 would still need to identify the fourth figure as that Son, and other texts would still need to identify him as the Father or as Almighty God. Daniel 3 does neither.

## A Substantive Reply

A reader may answer that biblical angels sometimes speak and act with God’s authority, so Daniel 3:28 could still refer to Christ. That possibility is why **angel** alone is not decisive. The only speaker who describes the fourth figure first uses Babylonian divine-being language and later says that God sent “his angel.” The narrative does not add Jesus’ name or a messianic title.

Nor does the rescue establish that the rescuer is God Almighty. In Daniel, God’s servants receive help through agents: an angel shuts lions’ mouths (Daniel 6:22), while God receives credit for the rescue. A later Christophany reading is possible, but it remains an inference beyond Daniel’s explicit identification. The text neither proves it nor rules it out.

## Conclusion

Daniel’s own account supports reading the fourth figure as [God’s angelic agent](#the-chapters-identification), not as an explicit appearance of Jesus. The KJV reading supports a serious alternative interpretation, but [Nebuchadnezzar’s setting and Daniel 3:28](#daniels-fourth-figure) do not show that the king knew the Christian Son. The Old Testament’s appointed human Messiah and the New Testament’s God-and-Messiah distinction support the conclusion that Jesus is [not God Almighty or God the Father](#the-human-messiah-and-gods-agent).

*The Aramaic **bar elahin** is naturally “a son of the gods” in this pagan speaker’s words; Daniel 3:28 identifies the rescuer as an angel. Neither wording establishes a speculative identity.* See [limits on Eden Christophany](was-jesus-in-the-garden-of-eden.draft.md#what-can-be-concluded).
