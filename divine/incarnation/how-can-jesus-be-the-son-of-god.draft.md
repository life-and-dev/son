# How Can Jesus Be the Son of God?

Christians sometimes hear **Son of God** as if it could mean only “God the Son,” an eternally generated divine person. Scripture gives the title a broader history. Angels, Israel, and Israel’s king can be called God’s son, and Luke expressly explains why Jesus will bear the title. *Jesus is God’s Son because God caused his human conception, appointed him as David’s heir, and uniquely revealed himself through him, not because God physically fathered a child or because Jesus is God Almighty.* Luke 1:35 establishes birth-related sonship; it does not alone decide whether Jesus existed before his conception.

## Sonship in Israel’s Scriptures

The Old Testament prepares readers to understand divine sonship as a relationship established by God. God calls Israel “my firstborn son” (Exodus 4:22). He promises David concerning the coming royal heir, “I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son” (2 Samuel 7:14, ESV). Psalm 2 addresses God’s anointed king: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you” (Psalm 2:7, ESV). In each setting, **son** communicates election, covenant, resemblance, inheritance, and royal authority. It does not make the son identical to the God who appoints him.

This background matters because Gabriel presents Jesus as that promised Davidic king. Mary will bear a son who “will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High,” and God will give him “the throne of his father David” (Luke 1:31–33, ESV). The Most High is God; Jesus is the Son who receives David’s throne from him.

## Luke’s explanation of Jesus’ sonship

Mary asks how she can conceive while a virgin. Gabriel answers:

> “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; **therefore** the child to be born will be called holy—the Son of God.” — Luke 1:35 (ESV)

The word **therefore** gives Luke’s stated reason for the title in this scene. God’s Spirit and power cause the human conception; consequently, the child is holy and is called God’s Son. Matthew confirms that the child conceived in Mary is “from the Holy Spirit” (Matthew 1:18,20). Neither account describes sexual generation.

Luke 1:35 establishes birth-related sonship. It does not, by itself, say whether Jesus had a personal existence before conception. Trinitarian theology may read the verse as the eternal Son’s human conception; a Unitarian reading takes the stated conception as the basis of Sonship in Luke’s narrative. The verse itself settles neither wider pre-existence question.

This reading does not reduce Jesus to an ordinary man. He is uniquely conceived, holy from birth, David’s promised heir, and the Saviour whom God provides. He is truly Mary’s son, descended from David “according to the flesh” (Romans 1:3, ESV), and truly God’s Son by divine origin and appointment. Conception, anointing, kingship, and resurrection together identify the human Messiah as God’s unique Son.

## What “only Son” does—and does not—mean

John describes Jesus with the Greek word *monogenēs*, translated “only Son” by the ESV in John 1:14, 1:18, 3:16, and 3:18. Bill Mounce’s [Greek dictionary entry for *monogenēs*](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/monogenes) lists “only, unique” within its range, while also recording the traditional “only-begotten” wording.

Hebrews 11:17 calls Isaac Abraham’s *monogenēs*, even though Abraham had also fathered Ishmael. Isaac was the unique son of the promise through whom Abraham’s covenant line would be named (Genesis 21:12). This usage shows why the word can stress a son’s unique status and relationship, rather than answer every question about how that son began to exist.

The lexical point must remain modest. *Monogenēs* does not prove a Unitarian view, and “only begotten” must not be made to mean “created.” Nor does the word by itself establish the later doctrine of eternal generation. John’s own emphasis is that the unique Son comes from, is sent by, reveals, and obeys the Father (John 3:16–17, 5:19, 17:3).

## The strongest eternal-Son reading

Mainstream Trinitarian theology does not teach biological fatherhood. It distinguishes Father and Son and says that the Son is eternally generated, not created. On this reading, Luke 1:35 explains why the eternally existing Son will be recognized as God’s Son in human life, not when his personal existence begins. John 1:1–18, John 17:5, and Hebrews 1:2–3 are then read as evidence that the Son personally existed before Mary conceived him. A two-natures account adds that the eternal Son assumed a complete human nature while remaining divine.

That is a serious cumulative argument; simply showing that Jesus is not the Father does not answer it. Yet Luke never mentions an eternal Son entering Mary or a prehuman person assuming flesh. He describes God’s Spirit acting upon Mary and makes that action the explicit ground introduced by **therefore**. The Old Testament royal promises supply Luke’s public meaning for **Son of God**, while later preaching repeatedly identifies Jesus as the man whom God anointed, raised, and exalted (Acts 2:22–24, 36; 10:38).

The Unitarian case therefore rests on positive narrative and covenantal evidence, not on bare silence. The one God acts; Jesus receives life, status, power, revelation, and kingship from him. Even Hebrews, while exalting the Son above angels, says that God speaks “by his Son” and addresses the Son alongside “God, your God,” who anointed him (Hebrews 1:1–2, 8–9).

## A bounded conclusion

Luke 1:35 provides Scripture’s clearest direct explanation of why the child born to Mary is called God’s Son: God’s Holy Spirit and power cause his conception, **therefore** he is called the Son of God. Israel’s royal promises, Jesus’ anointing, and his resurrection reinforce that messianic sonship. John’s *monogenēs* language adds uniqueness and does not prove that Jesus was created or establish eternal generation by itself.

*These texts favour Jesus as God’s unique human Messiah, not God Almighty or the Father.* Luke’s birth-related explanation does not alone decide prior existence. Compare [the Word becoming flesh](incarnation-of-jesus.draft.md#the-word-became-flesh).
