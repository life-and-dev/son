# 1 John 5:20 (The True God)

> And we know **that the Son of God has come** and has given us understanding, **so that we may know him who is true**; and **we are in him who is true**, in his Son Jesus Christ. **He is the true God** and eternal life. — 1 John 5:20 (ESV)

Out of context and the way it is translated, it seems like "the Son of God" is "the true God".

This raises an immediate **paradox within the same sentence**, showing that *God is His own Son*. [Trinitarians](../../trinity.md) would explain this fits their doctrine because of their shared essence, meaning *"the Son of God" is also "the God the Father"*, yet distinct persons with a Father-son relationship. This is all based on [philosophy developed many centuries](../../trinity/development.md) later. Therefore no Bible scripture will support this reasoning. If Jesus and God the Father had this special relationship, then the Bible authors should not have used common "Father" and "Son" terminology to describe it. No human language allow a "father" and "son" to be the same individual.

## Textual Variation

The manuscript [A(02) reads “so that we may know the true **God**”](https://corebible.app/2PE/1?verseNumber=%2228%22&translation=AICNT) which clarify that "who is true" is not "the Son of God" in this context.

Who is "the true God" of 1 John 5:20?

> The question that has been hotly debated about this sentence is who is “This one” who is the true God? Is it the Father or Jesus Christ? **The grammar can go either way**, and each side of the argument has had its noteworthy proponents, so gathering scholars to support one’s position can be done for both positions. The argument is not settled by the nearest antecedent noun because reference to the nearest noun is not a hard-and-fast rule of Greek grammar, and **there are times that John, like other writers of the New Testament, does not follow it** (cf. 1 John 2:22).
>
> The evidence from Scripture is that the “true God” is the Father. **The Father is called the “true God” in both the Old and New Testaments** (cf. Jeremiah 10:10; 1 Thessalonians 1:9), and **Jesus himself referred to the Father as the “true God”** (John 17:3). Even 1 John 5:20 itself calls the Father “the True One.” In contrast, **Jesus is never called the true God.** There does not seem to be any good reason that the Father would be called “the True One” in contrast to the Son, but then in the very next sentence the Son be called the “True God.” That would only seem to **introduce a new idea into the Epistle and be confusing**. Furthermore, **1 John consistently makes a distinction between “God” and the Son**, Jesus Christ (cf. 1 John 1:3, 7; 2:1; 4:2,3,9,10,15; 5:1,9,10,11,12; 5:18). Jesus Christ is never referred to as God in 1 John. Thus, the evidence of Scripture is that “This one” refers to the Father. — [REV Bible Commentary](https://revbible.com/comm/1Jo/5/20)

## Context

In John's life-time there was an ancient belief that [Christ was divine](../../divine.md) but *not genuinely human*, called **Docetism**. The term comes from the Greek word dokeō (δοκέω), meaning “to seem” or “to appear.” Docetism taught that *Jesus only appeared to have a physical human body, suffer, and die*, rather than experiencing these things as a genuine human being. This belief appeared in various forms in early Christianity, particularly during the second century, although related ideas may have existed earlier. This is evident from scriptures like:

> What was from the beginning, what **we have heard**, what **we have seen** with our eyes, what **we beheld and touched** with our hands, concerning [the Word of Life](../../name/word.md)—and **the life was manifested**, and we have seen and bear witness and proclaim to you [the eternal life](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/life), which was with the Father and was manifested to us— what we have **seen** and **heard** we proclaim to you also, so that you may also have fellowship with us; and indeed our fellowship is with the Father, and with His Son Jesus Christ. — 1 John 1:1-3 (LSB)

John repeatedly mention that they physically heard, seen and touched Jesus Christ. John want to make sure his audience clearly understood that Jesus was [genuinely human](../../human.md).

During the book John identifies all sorts of deceptions and show [how to identify the spirit the Antichrist](../../shema/concerns/antichrist.md). For example:

> By this you know the Spirit of God: every spirit that **confesses that Jesus Christ** has **come in the flesh** is from God, and every spirit that does **not confess Jesus** is not from God. This is the spirit of the **antichrist**, which you heard was coming and now is in the world already. — 1 John 4:2-3 (ESV)

> [!TIP]
> ["Christ" means *anointed*](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/christ), so "*anti*christ" would mean *not annointed*.

> [!TIP]
> "Come in flesh" means to be born as a human (John 1:14).

This scripture refutes Docetism and Christology (Jesus is God), because it shows Christ was anointed by God and that he was born as a human.

John even concludes his book with the warning:

> Little children, **guard yourselves from idols**. — 1 John 5:21 (LSB)

The common interpretation is that John referred to idol images. However, John was addressing his "little chiren" (John 2:1) and not pagans. John never mention any pagan or idol worship in his book. The theme of the book is to address deceptions regarding the nature of Jesus Christ. Although ubiquitous, John likely meant that his audience should not idolize Jesus, by confusing him with the true God (the Father).
