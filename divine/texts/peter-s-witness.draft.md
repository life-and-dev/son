## Peter’s Confession and Gospel

Peter gives Jesus extraordinary honour. He calls him the Christ, God’s Son, Lord, and Saviour, and he presents him as the one through whom God saves. Yet Peter repeatedly distinguishes God from Jesus and identifies God as Jesus’ Father. This pattern supports a Unitarian reading in which Jesus is God’s exalted Messiah and authorised agent, without making the title evidence disappear.

> “You are the **Christ**, the **Son of the living God**.”  
> Matthew 16:16 (ESV)

Peter’s foundational confession identifies Jesus as the Messiah and as the Son *of* God. “Christ” means God’s anointed king. The wording honours Jesus while distinguishing him from the living God whose Son he is.

> “Blessed be the **God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ**! According to his great mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead.”  
> 1 Peter 1:3 (ESV)

God is the source whom Peter blesses. Jesus is Lord, and God brings new birth through Jesus’ resurrection. This is positive source-and-agent language, although it does not by itself resolve Peter’s direct “God and Savior” title in 2 Peter 1:1.

#### Messiah Under God’s Authority

Peter says that Christ suffered “that he might bring us to God” (1 Peter 3:18). The goal is God; Christ is the one who brings believers to him. Peter likewise calls Jesus “a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious” (1 Peter 2:4). God chooses and honours the Messiah.

The Hebrew Scriptures give this royal framework:

> “I will be to him a **father**, and he shall be to me a **son**.”  
> 2 Samuel 7:14 (ESV)

> “The **LORD** says to my **Lord**: ‘Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.’”  
> Psalm 110:1 (ESV)

A son promised to David and a lord seated at God’s right hand receive great honour without becoming the LORD who appoints and exalts them. *Peter’s titles for Jesus fit this messianic pattern of delegated rule.*

### God and Jesus in Peter’s Letters

Peter does not present “God” and “Jesus” as interchangeable names. He commonly coordinates them while retaining their distinction.

> “May grace and peace be multiplied to you in the knowledge of **God and of Jesus our Lord**.”  
> 2 Peter 1:2 (ESV)

> “For when he received honor and glory from **God the Father**, and the voice was borne to him by the Majestic Glory, ‘This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased,’”  
> 2 Peter 1:17 (ESV)

The transfiguration scene is decisive for Peter’s own framing. The Father gives honour and glory; Jesus receives them and is named the beloved Son. The relation is not one of two persons identified as the same God.

Peter also writes that Christ “was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times for the sake of you” (1 Peter 1:20). Foreknowledge and manifestation describe God’s saving purpose fulfilled in Christ. *They do not require that Christ be the God who foreknows and sends.*

### The Difficult Title in 2 Peter 1:1

Second Peter 1:1 must be read without evasion:

> “To those who have obtained a faith of equal standing with ours by the righteousness of **our God and Savior Jesus Christ**:”  
> 2 Peter 1:1 (ESV)

The strongest Trinitarian reading takes “God and Savior Jesus Christ” as one referent and therefore understands Peter to call Jesus God. Many interpreters support that reading from the Greek article before “God” and the two singular personal nouns joined by “and.” This observation is commonly called Granville Sharp’s rule. It is a serious reading and should not be dismissed by an English punctuation choice.

#### What the Grammar Establishes

The Greek construction strongly connects the two titles to Jesus in this verse. Second Peter 1:11 uses the same article-and-coordination pattern for “our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ”, where both titles plainly refer to Jesus:

> “for in this way there will be richly provided for you an entrance into the eternal kingdom of **our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ**.”  
> 2 Peter 1:11 (ESV)

The same one-article construction occurs in Titus 2:13, commonly rendered “our great God and Savior Jesus Christ”. Daniel B. Wallace, *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics*, discusses the construction commonly called Granville Sharp’s rule and its application to these texts. Its strict scope concerns singular, personal, non-proper nouns joined by *kai* under one article. It does not apply mechanically to plural, impersonal, or proper-noun constructions, and grammar alone does not define a title’s full theological implications.

*Within that scope, the grammar gives a strong single-referent case for “God and Savior” in 2 Peter 1:1. It does not rest on English punctuation or a manuscript variant, and it does not by itself settle the relation of Jesus to the Father.*

#### Direct Unitarian Reply

A Unitarian reading may therefore grant that 2 Peter 1:1 calls Jesus “God and Savior”. *The question is what Peter means by the title in its scriptural and literary setting, not whether the phrase is weighty.* Israel’s Scriptures sometimes give divine language to authorised representatives without confusing them with the LORD.

> “And the LORD said to Moses, ‘See, I have made you like **God** to Pharaoh, and your brother Aaron shall be your prophet.’”  
> Exodus 7:1 (ESV)

That precedent does not make every use of “God” figurative, nor does it make Peter’s direct title unimportant. It shows why a title must be read with context rather than treated as an automatic definition of shared identity or a complete doctrine of the Trinity.

In 2 Peter, Jesus remains “Lord”, “Savior”, and the beloved Son who receives honour from the Father; 2 Peter 1:2 immediately distinguishes “God” and “Jesus our Lord”. *A Unitarian reading can therefore affirm the single-referent grammar while arguing that Peter’s wider Father-Son pattern still matters. The Trinitarian reply is that the direct application of “God” to Jesus is nonetheless substantial positive evidence, not an ambiguity resolved merely by agency texts.*

### Conclusion

Peter’s confession identifies Jesus as the [Christ and Son of the living God](#peters-confession-and-gospel), and his letters distinguish the [Father who gives Jesus honour](#god-and-jesus-in-peters-letters). Second Peter 1:1 has a [strong same-referent grammatical reading](#what-the-grammar-establishes), also reflected in Titus 2:13. *Peter’s wider pattern supports a Unitarian reading, but his direct “God and Savior” title remains serious evidence for the Trinitarian case.* Compare [Paul’s Witness](paul-s-witness.draft.md#creation-and-pre-existence).
