# John 20:28 (Thomas’s Confession)

> Thomas answered him *[Jesus]*, “My **Lord** and my **God**!” — John 20:28 (ESV)

Jesus does not rebuke Thomas for speaking to him this way. Instead, he connects Thomas’s belief with what Thomas has seen:

> Jesus said to him, “Have you **believed** because you have **seen** me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed.” — John 20:29 (ESV)

Does Thomas identify Jesus as Almighty God?

## Thomas before the resurrection

When Jesus decides to return towards Judea, Thomas says, “Let us also go, that we may **die** with him” (John 11:16). His words anticipate defeat and disbelief that Jesus would be able to protect them.

Later Thomas asks, “Lord, we do not know where you are going. **How can we know** the way?” Jesus answers by identifying himself as the way to the Father (John 14:5–7). This may also imply that Thomas thought that Jesus was making an unfair statement.

**Thomas is absent** when the risen Jesus first appears to the gathered disciples. The first disciples told that they have seen the Lord, but Thomas demands evidence from Jesus’ wounds (John 20:24–25). Jesus subsequently invites him to inspect them and calls him to believe. Then Thomas responds with his confession. Jesus then blesses those who believe without seeing him (John 20:26–29).

## Does ὁ θεός Mean Almighty God?

Thomas’s confession reads:

> ἀπεκρίθη Θωμᾶς καὶ εἶπεν αὐτῷ, **Ὁ κύριός** μου καὶ **ὁ θεός μου** — John 20:28 (Greek)

> My **lord** and my **god**! — John 20:28 (English)

Thomas speaks directly to Jesus. The question concerns the meaning of his confession, not whether Jesus is its addressee.

The expression **ὁ θεός** (*ho theos*) contains the Greek definite article, roughly corresponding to English “the”. However, the article does not mean “highest” or “Almighty”. Compare these expressions:

| Passage           | Greek Expression             | ESV Rendering               |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| John 20:28        | **ὁ θεός** μου               | “my **God**”                |
| 2 Corinthians 4:4 | **ὁ θεὸς** τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου | “the **god** of this world” |

In 2 Corinthians 4:4, “the god of this world” is normally understood to refer to the devil. Paul nevertheless uses the same article and noun found in Thomas’s confession. The different accent on θεὸς reflects its position within the phrase, not a different word.

**Therefore, ὁ θεός does not, by itself, identify someone as Almighty God.** The accompanying words and context determine whom the expression describes.

Early surviving New Testament book manuscripts generally use [majuscule writing](https://www.csntm.org/2022/04/22/manuscripts-101-a-brief-history-of-greek-handwriting/), broadly comparable to capital letters. They do not present the modern English distinction between “God” and “god”. Consequently, the capital **G** in an English translation of John 20:28 is **an interpretative judgement**, not a capitalisation distinction reproduced from the earliest Greek manuscripts. Its justification must come from meaning and context, not from the English capital letter itself.

### God-Language for Human Representatives

Scripture also applies God-language to someone exercising delegated authority. The LORD tells Moses:

> And the LORD said to Moses, “See, I have made you *like* **God** to Pharaoh, and your brother Aaron shall be your prophet.” — Exodus 7:1 (ESV)

The Greek Old Testament renders the relevant words "δέδωκά σε **θεὸν** Φαραώ", approximately “I have made you **god** to Pharaoh”. Here θεόν is another grammatical form of θεός.

Neither the Hebrew nor the Greek explicitly includes a word meaning “like”. The ESV makes the representative comparison explicit by adding the word "like".

Moses does not become Almighty God. He receives authority to represent [the LORD (YHWH)](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/name) before Pharaoh. This illustrates that [Moses represented God](../../shema/agency.md) as an agent like Jesus was representing God in the book of John.

Jesus likewise quotes Psalm 82:

> “I said, you are **gods**” — John 10:34 (ESV, excerpt)

Under the human-judges interpretation of this psalm, the designation concerns people exercising delegated authority. [Thayer’s lexical entry](https://www.bibletools.org/index.cfm/fuseaction/lexicon.show/id/G2316/theos.htm) includes the usage *“of magistrates and judges”*.

### Reading Thomas in Context

Thomas’s words are the climax of a scene about testimony and belief. He has rejected the other disciples’ report, sees the risen Jesus, and confesses him. Jesus’ blessing then turns from this eyewitness towards people who will believe without seeing. Immediately afterwards, the narrator explains why selected signs have been written down:

> But these are written so that you may **believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God**, and that by believing you may have life in [his name](../../name.md). — John 20:31 (ESV)

John repeatedly [distinguishes the Father](../../human/distinct.md) Who [sends from Jesus who reveals Him](../../shema/agency.md). In prayer, Jesus addresses the Father as “the **only true God**” and identifies himself as “Jesus Christ whom you have **sent**” (John 17:3).

Some would defend Jesus divinity by claiming these scriptures only applied to Jesus *human form* before he had risen. **After his resurrection**, he sends Mary to the disciples with this message:

> I am ascending to **my Father and your Father**, to **my God and your God**. — John 20:17 (ESV)

He still calls the Father “my God”.

## Conclusion

John 20:28 does not, by itself, prove that Jesus is **Almighty God**. Neither [the expression ὁ θεός](#does-ὁ-θεός-mean-almighty-god) nor English capitalisation establishes that identity. John’s stated purpose identifies Jesus as the Christ and Son of God (John 20:31), while the risen Jesus still calls the Father “my God” (John 20:17).

Thomas honours God the Father’s risen Messiah and authorised representative.
