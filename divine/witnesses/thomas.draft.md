# Thomas’s Confession

> Thomas answered him *[Jesus]*, “My **Lord** and my **God**!” — John 20:28 (ESV)

Jesus does not rebuke Thomas for speaking to him this way. Instead, he connects Thomas’s belief with what Thomas has seen:

> Jesus said to him, “Have you **believed** because you have **seen** me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed.” — John 20:29 (ESV)

Does Thomas identify Jesus as Almighty God?

## Thomas before the resurrection

When Jesus decides to return towards Judea, Thomas says, “Let us also go, that we may **die** with him” (John 11:16). His words anticipate defeat and disbelief that Jesus would be able to protect them.

Later Thomas asks, “Lord, we do not know where you are going. **How can we know** the way?” Jesus answers by identifying himself as the way to the Father (John 14:5–7). This may also imply that Thomas thought that Jesus was making an unfair statement.

**Thomas is absent** when the risen Jesus first appears to the gathered disciples. The first disciples told that they have seen the Lord, but Thomas demands evidence from Jesus’ wounds (John 20:24–25). Jesus subsequently invites him to inspect them and calls him to believe. Then Thomas responds with his confession. Jesus then blesses those who believe without seeing him (John 20:26–29). Thomas also appears among the disciples in the fishing scene after these events (John 21:2). That final mention establishes his presence, not a further explanation of his confession.

## The Narrator’s Purpose

Thomas’s words are the climax of a scene about testimony and belief. He has rejected the other disciples’ report, sees the risen Jesus, and confesses him. Jesus’ blessing then turns from this eyewitness towards people who will believe without seeing. Immediately afterwards, the narrator explains why selected signs have been written down:

> But these are written so that you may **believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God**, and that by believing you may have life in [his name](../../name.md). — John 20:31 (ESV)

## Reading the confession within John

John repeatedly distinguishes the Father who sends from Jesus who reveals him. In prayer, Jesus addresses the Father as “the **only true God**” and identifies himself as “Jesus Christ whom you have **sent**” (John 17:3). After his resurrection, he sends Mary to the disciples with this message:

> I am ascending to **my Father and your Father**, to **my God and your God**. — John 20:17 (ESV)

This is the risen Jesus speaking shortly before Thomas’s confession. He still calls the Father “my God”. Any account of the passage must take that statement seriously **and** preserve Thomas’s direct address to Jesus. Neither verse should be silently rewritten to remove the tension.

Jesus describes how the Father can be encountered through the one he sends:

> Whoever believes in me, believes not in me but in **him who sent me**. And whoever sees me sees **him who sent me**. — John 12:44–45 (ESV)

He also says that receiving his messenger is receiving him, and receiving him is receiving the one who sent him (John 13:20). This chain of reception does not make messenger, Jesus, and Father identical. It shows how receiving an authorised representative reaches the sender. In the exchange begun by Thomas’s question, Jesus’ answer to **Philip** similarly joins seeing Jesus with seeing the Father, while speaking of the Father’s works in him (John 14:8–11).

## How confessing the Son includes the Father

The relationship between confession of Jesus and allegiance to the Father is stated plainly in 1 John:

> No one who **denies the Son** has the **Father**. Whoever **confesses the Son** has the **Father also**. — 1 John 2:23 (ESV)

“Has” here is not “is”. The verse concerns having the Father while confessing the Son, not an equation in which the Son becomes the Father. Its immediate context identifies the denial at issue as denying that Jesus is the Christ and urges readers to abide in what they heard from the beginning (1 John 2:22–24). Later, the letter says the Father sent the Son as the world’s Saviour and links confessing Jesus as Son of God with abiding in God (1 John 4:14–15). The text does not require us to import a later doctrinal definition into the word “denies”.

This distinction is inseparable from the relationship John’s Gospel describes. Rejecting the Father’s sent Son rejects the Father’s testimony; receiving him reaches the one who sent him (John 12:44–45; 13:20). Confessing Jesus as the Christ and Son of God is likewise the purpose stated in John 20:31. The letter and Gospel can be read together on this point without first claiming to prove identical authorship.

Thus, recognising the Father through Jesus is not a switch of addressee in Thomas’s sentence. Thomas speaks **to Jesus**. The proposed connection is *“Confessing the risen Son also acknowledges the Father revealed through him, without making Father and Son the same person.”* This neither treats them as interchangeable nor assumes that their distinction alone settles the debate with Trinitarian interpretation. Nor does “has the Father also” (1 John 2:23) itself define the Son’s nature.
