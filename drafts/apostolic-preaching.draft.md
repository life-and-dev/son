# Apostolic Preaching

This page gathers recorded preaching and gospel summaries without turning every narrative summary into a verbatim sermon. Its project view is that the Father alone is God, and Jesus is God's raised and exalted human Messiah. For the broader claims, see [The Son Of God](../index.md), [The Word With God](../word.md), and [Unitarian Views](../unitarianism.md).

## How This Evidence Is Read

Three claims must remain separate.

| Category | What it can establish | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Recorded statement** | Words actually reported in a speech, prayer, confession, or letter. | More than the words say. |
| **Inference** | A conclusion drawn by joining statements, titles, allusions, or a larger theology. | A statement that the speaker expressly made. |
| **Omission** | A limit on claims about the recorded content. | What hearers privately believed, understood, or later learned. |

No recorded Acts sermon states that Jesus is God.

That is a statement about the available recorded sermon content, not about every hearer's internal belief. Omission only limits claims about recorded content; it never proves that hearers lacked an internal belief.

Acts also contains travel reports, trial scenes, visions, and narrative summaries such as “preaching Jesus” or “reasoning from the Scriptures.” Those are evidence of proclamation, but they are not treated here as full verbatim sermons. This page does not claim completeness beyond recorded Acts material.

## Peter's Recorded Witness in Acts

### First Community and Pentecost: Acts 1–2

Acts 1 records Jesus' commission and the apostles' prayer, not a public sermon by Peter. Its explicit frame is God, the promised Spirit, witness to Jesus, and Jesus' ascension. In Acts 2 Peter's sermon identifies Jesus as “a man attested to you by God,” says God did works through him, says his hearers killed him, and repeatedly says **God raised him** (Acts 2:22–24, 32). Peter concludes that “God has made him both Lord and Christ” (2:36), then calls for repentance and baptism in Jesus Christ's name for forgiveness (2:38).

| Recorded content | Project reading | Strongest Nicene reading |
|---|---|---|
| God worked through Jesus; God raised Jesus; God made Jesus Lord and Christ. | God publicly vindicated and exalted his human Messiah. See [Jesus serves God](../son-of-man/serve-god.md). | The exalted Lord shares divine status; Peter's use of Psalm 110 and Joel may place Jesus within God's identity. |
| “Everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved” (2:21), followed by preaching Jesus. | Peter can apply the saving Lord-text through God's appointed Messiah without calling Jesus God. | The Joel allusion is read as identifying Jesus with Israel's Lord. This is an inference from allusion and referent, not an explicit “Jesus is God” sentence. |

### Temple, Opposition, and Prayer: Acts 3–5

Peter calls Jesus God's “servant” in Acts 3:13, calls him the Holy and Righteous One and Author or Prince of life, and says **God raised him** (3:14–15). He presents Jesus as the prophet like Moses and the Messiah sent by God (3:20–26). In Acts 4, Peter says salvation is in Jesus' name (4:10–12); the community prays to God about “your holy servant Jesus, whom you anointed” (4:27–30). In Acts 5, Peter says “the God of our fathers raised Jesus,” and “God exalted him at his right hand as Leader and Savior, to give repentance to Israel and forgiveness of sins” (5:30–31).

The strongest Nicene reading sees the unique saving name, “Author of life,” and right-hand exaltation as more than human status. The project response is that these are high messianic titles within a speech that repeatedly distinguishes God from the one God raised, anointed, sent, and exalted. For the creation-related title, see [The Creator](../son-as-god/creator.md); for this distinction, see [Jesus Interacted With God](../son-of-man/distinct.md).

### Gentiles and Council: Acts 10–11 and 15

To Cornelius, Peter says “God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power,” that God was with him, that people killed him, and that God raised him (Acts 10:38–40). Jesus is appointed by God as judge of living and dead; forgiveness comes through his name (10:42–43). Acts 11 repeats this Gentile mission in Peter's report. At the Jerusalem council in Acts 15, Peter speaks of God giving the Spirit and cleansing Gentile hearts by faith (15:7–11); James gives the council's judgment. These are council speeches and summaries, not a new complete evangelistic sermon.

## Paul's Recorded Witness in Acts

### Call, Synagogue, and Public Ministry: Acts 9, 13–20

| Acts material | Form | Recorded focus |
|---|---|---|
| **9** | Call narrative and summary | Paul is chosen to carry Jesus' name; he immediately proclaims that Jesus is the Son of God and proves Jesus is the Christ (9:20–22). The summary does not preserve a full sermon. |
| **13** | Sermon at Pisidian Antioch | “The God of this people Israel” chose the fathers; from David's offspring God brought a Savior, Jesus; God fulfilled promise by raising Jesus; forgiveness and justification are proclaimed through this man (13:16–41). |
| **14** | Brief address at Lystra | Turn from vain things to the living God, creator and benefactor (14:15–17). Jesus is not named in this surviving address. |
| **16** | Reports and short responses | Lydia receives Paul's message; the jailer asks how to be saved and receives “Believe in the Lord Jesus” with fuller teaching to his household (16:14, 31–32). These are not complete transcripts. |
| **17** | Thessalonica summary and Areopagus speech | The Thessalonica summary says Paul reasoned that Jesus is the Christ (17:2–3). At Athens he proclaims the one Creator God, repentance, coming judgment, and assurance by raising the man God appointed (17:24–31). |
| **18–19** | Ministry summaries, synagogue reasoning, and Ephesus events | Paul reasons that Jesus is the Christ (18:5, 28), teaches the word, and disciples are baptized in the Lord Jesus' name (19:1–7). Summaries do not supply whole sermons. |
| **20** | Farewell to Ephesian elders | Paul recalls repentance toward God and faith in the Lord Jesus (20:21), God's grace, and his gospel ministry. The address is to elders, not a first-hearing sermon. |

Acts 20:28 is often used for direct deity: “church of God … with his own blood.” The exact dispute is a **manuscript/textual variant** (“church of God” or “church of the Lord”) and the **Greek grammar** of “blood of his own,” which may refer to God's own Son. See [Acts 20:28](../essence/as-god/incarnation/does-god-have-blood.md). The strongest Nicene reading takes “God” and “his own blood” together; the project response takes the variants and grammar seriously and keeps God's identity distinct from Jesus' shed blood.

### Testimony, Trials, and Rome: Acts 22–28

| Acts material | Form | Recorded focus |
|---|---|---|
| **22** | Paul's testimony | The God of the fathers appointed Paul to see the Righteous One; Paul is told to be baptized and call on Jesus' name (22:14–16). |
| **23** | Hearing and narrative | Paul confesses hope in resurrection; no complete evangelistic sermon is recorded. |
| **24** | Defense before Felix | Paul serves the God of his fathers, believes Scripture, and has hope in resurrection and judgment (24:14–15, 24–25). |
| **26** | Defense before Agrippa | Paul tells of the risen Jesus' commission and preaches repentance, turning to God, and deeds fitting repentance; the Messiah suffers and proclaims light (26:15–23). |
| **28** | Rome ministry summary | Paul explains the kingdom of God and persuades about Jesus from Moses and the Prophets (28:23, 30–31). The summary does not preserve every word. |

## Other Witnesses in Acts

Stephen's speech in Acts 6–7 retells Israel's history, calls Jesus the Righteous One, and sees “the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God” (7:52, 55–56). Philip preaches Christ in Samaria and announces good news about the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ (Acts 8:5, 12); the Ethiopian is taught from Isaiah and baptized (8:35–38). These reports establish focus, but only Stephen's long speech is substantially recorded.

Acts 7:59–60 records Stephen calling, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit,” and asking that sin not be held against his killers. The strongest Nicene reading sees prayer to Jesus as divine worship. The exact dispute is the **referent** and function of “Lord Jesus”: an address to the visibly exalted Son of Man, not an explicit identification of him as God. Compare [Worshipping Jesus](../son-as-god/worship.md) and [Jesus Interacted With God](../son-of-man/distinct.md).

The Jerusalem-council witness is also useful: Peter credits God with giving the Spirit and cleansing hearts (Acts 15:7–9); James speaks of God's action among Gentiles (15:14–19). It records agreement about Gentile inclusion, not a formal definition of God's essence.

| Philippian jailer (16) | Believe in the Lord Jesus; baptism follows. | Salvation through faith; fuller household teaching is mentioned but not quoted. |

This consistent baptism language concerns Jesus' name and authority. It does not by itself settle whether Acts uses a short form for Matthew 28:19 or states a different spoken formula. See [The Baptism Formula](../trinity/baptism-formula.md).

| Passage | Concise summary |

These formulas repeatedly join God, Jesus, death, resurrection, lordship, forgiveness, reconciliation, and salvation. They do not use a formula saying that Jesus is God. The project reads the repeated pattern as Father-God and raised human Messiah; Nicene readers read it within a wider account of the Son's eternal identity.

## Confession Formulas and First-Hearing Content

### Repeated Formulas

* **Jesus is the Christ / Messiah** — Acts 2:36; 9:22; 17:3; 18:5, 28.
* **Jesus is the Son of God** — Acts 9:20; Romans 1:3–4.
* **Jesus is Lord** — Acts 2:36; Romans 10:9; Philippians 2:11.
* **God raised him** — Acts 2:24, 32; 3:15; 5:30; 10:40; 13:30; Romans 10:9; Galatians 1:1.
* **One God, one Father; one Lord, Jesus Christ** — 1 Corinthians 8:6; compare 1 Timothy 2:5–6.
* **Mediator** — “the man Christ Jesus” mediates between the one God and humanity (1 Timothy 2:5–6).
* **Baptism in Jesus' name** — Acts 2:38; 8:16; 10:48; 19:5; 22:16.

## Strongest Nicene Case and Project Response

The following are the strongest commonly offered readings from Acts and the letters. A fair reading grants their force before stating the project response.

| Hebrews 1:8–12 | The Son is addressed as God and receives creation language from Psalm 102. | **Referent**: the application and scope of the quoted Psalm language to the Son is disputed; Hebrews also says God appoints, anoints, and calls the Son's God (1:2, 9). | 1 Corinthians 10:4, 9 | Christ was the wilderness rock and was tested by Israel. | **Referent**: whether “Christ” identifies the prehuman Jesus as Israel's God, or identifies God's promised Messiah in typological reading, is disputed. The Nicene case gains force by combining these texts: divine titles, creation language, preexistence, Scripture allusions, and worship. The project response is not that these readings are impossible; it is that each requires a disputed grammatical, textual, scope, or referent decision, while the direct preaching pattern repeatedly names God as the one who sends, anoints, raises, exalts, and judges through Jesus. On this project view, Jesus receives unique God-given lordship and honour as God's human Messiah, not identity as the Father who alone is God.

## Result

Recorded apostolic preaching makes God, Jesus, resurrection, lordship, repentance, forgiveness, judgment, and salvation central. It explicitly proclaims Jesus as God's Messiah, Son, servant, Savior, Lord, mediator, and appointed judge, and explicitly says God raised and exalted him. Strong Nicene inferences remain important to assess, but they must be labeled as inferences rather than substituted for what the recorded sermons say.

## What the Apostles Did and Did Not Make Explicit

Trinitarians rightly observe that apostolic preaching includes exalted claims about Jesus, not merely a human teacher. The apostles call him Lord, proclaim his resurrection, and announce that all must answer to him.

The recorded proclamations nevertheless identify the acting God as the Father. Peter calls Jesus “a man attested to you by God,” says that God raised him, and says that God made him Lord and Christ (**Acts 2:22–24, 36**). Paul tells the Athenians that God will judge the world through “a man whom he has appointed,” giving assurance by raising him from the dead (**Acts 17:30–31**). The preaching gives Jesus supreme messianic authority while retaining the Father alone as God.

## Apostolic Pattern and Its Strongest Challenge

The strongest Trinitarian reading does not depend on a sermon containing later creedal formulae. In **Acts 2**, Peter gives Jesus the Davidic Lordship of **Psalm 110:1**; in **Acts 7:59–60**, Stephen addresses the exalted Jesus; and Paul can apply the confession “Jesus is Lord” to salvation in **Romans 10:9–13**. Together with high passages in the letters, these are reasonably read as apostolic worship and divine Christology.

The direct project reply concerns the message actually proclaimed. Peter calls Jesus “a man attested to you by God,” says “God raised him,” and says “God has made him both Lord and Christ” (**Acts 2:22, 24, 32, 36**). The church prays to God concerning “your holy servant Jesus, whom you anointed” (**Acts 4:24, 27, 30**). Paul identifies God as judge and Jesus as “a man whom he has appointed,” whose resurrection supplies assurance (**Acts 17:30–31**). These are not minor omissions. They identify the God who acts and the human Messiah through whom he acts.

The Spirit fits the same preaching pattern: God gives the promised Holy Spirit (**Acts 2:33**), and God anointed Jesus “with the Holy Spirit and with power” (**Acts 10:38**). Thus the Spirit is presented as God's promised gift and power in Father–Son interaction, not as a separately defined coequal person. The recorded gospel therefore supports Jesus’ unique, exalted authority while retaining the Father alone as God.
