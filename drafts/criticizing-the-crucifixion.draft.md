## Was Jesus a fragrant offering?

Paul directly says that **“Christ loved us and gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God”** (Ephesians 5:2, ESV). The phrase modifies Christ’s self-giving. It is not a claim that the congregation itself is the fragrant offering.

The language echoes Old Testament sacrifices described as a pleasing aroma (Genesis 8:21; Exodus 29:18,25). Paul uses it to portray Christ’s voluntary, Godward self-giving and to ground the command that believers “walk in love” (Ephesians 5:1-2). The analogy does not require Golgotha to reproduce temple ritual, nor does it say that God required continuing sacrifices after Christ’s death.

A careful Unitarian reading can affirm the text’s sacrificial language without constructing a transaction in which God is paid. A classical Trinitarian reading can affirm the same text as the incarnate Son’s self-offering to the Father. Both must preserve Paul’s direct claim about Christ and the ethical conclusion he draws. See [The Crucifixion and Reconciliation](the-crucifixion.draft.md#the-purpose-of-the-new-covenant).

## Conclusion

Sacrificial objections should test claims, not deny the New Testament’s own typology. Christ’s death is best read through several images, including [ransom](the-crucifixion.draft.md#the-purpose-of-ransom) and Passover deliverance.

## Sacrifice, Purification, and Repentance

Levitical sacrifice, ritual purification, and repentance must not be collapsed into one mechanism. Leviticus 4:20 says that the priest’s sin offering makes atonement and the people are forgiven. Leviticus 16:29-34 joins the Day of Atonement’s priestly rites with Israel’s self-affliction and rest. The passage does not call this self-affliction a sacrifice or an expiatory rite.

Joel calls the people to **“return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning”** (Joel 2:12, ESV), and grounds the appeal in the LORD’s gracious character (Joel 2:13-14). Isaiah 58 rebukes fasting that leaves oppression untouched and describes the fast God chooses as justice, mercy, and care for the needy. In both prophets, fasting expresses repentance and petition; neither text equates it with sacrificial propitiation.

The strongest warranted atonement claim is therefore narrower: Scripture presents sacrificial rites as making atonement in their appointed setting, and calls sinners to repent, seek mercy, and practise justice. Jesus’ death can be read through sacrificial and covenant imagery, but the images should not make fasting, purification, repentance, and expiation interchangeable.
