# Jesus meant to be spiritually present

Being "spiritually present" is a vague statement. Even Paul said that:

> **When you are assembled** in the name of the Lord Jesus and **my spirit is present**, with the power of our Lord Jesus, you are to deliver this man to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord. -- 1 Corinthians 5:4-5 (ESV)

Surely Paul is not divine and neither was he omnipresent.

Therefore, some suggest that, like Paul, Jesus’ words should not be considered literally as if he would be physically "among" or "with" all Christians always.

So what was the intent for Jesus’ and Paul’s statements?

* This could mean that, when an assembly agrees with God’s Holy Spirit, its members act in the same spirit as the Lord Jesus and Paul, as if Jesus or Paul were present among them. They are united as "one".
* It could also mean that Jesus and Paul passed on their authority or approval to their disciples, enabling decisions as if they were present to give approval.

## Jesus meant it literally

Some reject the argument that Jesus words should be considered "spiritually" as there are no evidence that he was speaking in idioms, neither did he mention anything about the spiritual realm.

### Matthew 18:20

> For where two or three are gathered together in My name, **I am there *in the midst* of them.**” -- Matthew 18:20 (NKJV)

Out of context, it seems like Jesus magically appear "in the midst" everytime Christians gather. The context here is that Jesus was addressing how they should deal with the "brother that sins against you". One solution would be to gather two or three witnesses to confront him in Jesus' authority (name). In such a case, Jesus would be "among" (on the side of) those witnesses.

> “If your brother sins against you:
> 1. go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone. If he listens to you, you have  gained your brother.
> 2. But if he does not listen, take **one or two** others along with you, that every charge may be established by the evidence of **two or three witnesses**.
> 3. If he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the church.
>
> And if he refuses to listen even to the church, let him be to you as a Gentile and a tax collector.
>
> Truly, I say to you, whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven. Again I say to you, **if two of you** agree on earth about anything they ask, it will be done for them by my Father in heaven. For where **two or three are gathered** in my *name ([authority](/bible/concepts/name))*, there am I ***among*** them.”
>
> -- Matthew 18:15-20 (ESV)

Some would argue that the topic has changed from gathers to confront sin to gatharing to claim blessings from God. The reason they say so, it that most modern bibles translations separte this verse from the next verse with a paragraph break and often separate it even further by including an unrelated title between them which was not part of the original text. This is a common practice by bible translators to make their copies "more readable", but ironically it adds confusion to the intended message.

Jesus did not switch to prosperity preaching. Instead he meant with "bind" and "loose" **to intercede** for those who sin. Intercession is to plead with "the Father in heaven". When the first 3 steps failed, then the last alternative is to gather to intercede for the sinner. This is the strategy that Jesus authorized and support.

The topic is not finish yet. In the next verse Peter asked a valid question relating the same topic:

> Then Peter came up and said to him, “Lord, how often will my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? As many as seven times?”
> Jesus said to him, “I do not say to you seven times, but seventy-seven times. Therefore the kingdom of heaven may be compared to...”
>
> -- Matthew 18:21-23 (ESV)

Not only did Jesus answer his question, but he also explained his answer with a parable which is still related to the original topic "how to confront sin".

### Matthew 28:20

> Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them [in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit](/eternal/saved/baptize/formula), teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age. -- Matthew 28:19-20 (ESV)

Jesus closes the commission with an assurance for his disciples as they make disciples of all nations. The promise can mean that he remains with them in support of that mission, rather than that he states an attribute of unlimited presence. The Father sends the Holy Spirit in Jesus’ name (John 14:26), which gives a contextual means by which Jesus can be with his followers without Matthew 28:20 identifying him as omnipresent.

Acts records the same commission and Jesus’ visible ascension:

> So when they had come together, they asked him *(Jesus)*, “Lord, will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?”  
> He said to them, “It is not for you to know times or seasons that [the Father](/god/son/essence/not-god/distinct) has fixed by [His own authority](/god/son/essence/not-god/serve-god). But you will receive power when [the Holy Spirit](/god/spirit) has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth.”
>
> And when he had said these things, as they were looking on, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight. And while they were gazing into heaven as he went, behold, two men stood by them in white robes, and said,
>
> “Men of Galilee, why do you stand **looking into heaven**?  
> This Jesus, who **was taken up from you** into heaven, **will come in the same way** as you saw him go into heaven.”
>
> -- Acts 1:6-11 (ESV)

The ascension shows that the risen Jesus is visibly located and will return in the same manner. By itself, however, this does not disprove the Trinitarian claim. Trinitarians commonly distinguish Jesus’ ascended human nature from his divine nature, which they hold to be omnipresent.

The question is therefore textual. Neither Matthew 28:20 nor Acts 1:6-11 makes that two-natures distinction or says that Jesus has unlimited presence. In their context, Matthew promises continuing help for the mission, while Acts points to the Father’s authority, the Holy Spirit’s coming, Jesus’ departure, and his future return. A Unitarian reading may therefore understand Jesus’ presence as his support and authority exercised through God’s Spirit, without treating the assurance as a claim that Jesus shares YHVH’s omnipresence.

### Acts 18:9-10

> And the Lord said to Paul one night in a vision, “Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am ***with*** you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for **I have many in this city** who are my people.” -- Acts 18:9-10 (ESV)

This scripture does not proof Jesus omnipresence. Jesus did not magically appear on Paul's trial. Jesus was simply assuring Paul that he was not alone and that he would protect him with his people in the city.

### Context, Objection, and Response

Matthew 18:20 appears in Jesus’ instructions about confronting sin and establishing a matter through two or three witnesses (Matthew 18:15-20). Matthew 28:20 closes the commission. Acts then records Jesus’ visible ascension and promises his return from heaven (Acts 1:9-11).

The strongest Trinitarian reading understands Jesus to be personally present with believers in every place. It also distinguishes his ascended human nature, which is visibly located in heaven, from his divine nature, which is said to remain omnipresent. On that view, the ascension does not conflict with Jesus’ presence among disciples.

That distinction prevents the ascension from serving as a standalone disproof, but it is not stated in these passages. Matthew 18 describes Jesus’ authority in communal judgement, and Matthew 28 assures his continuing help in the mission. The Father sends the Holy Spirit in Jesus’ name (John 14:26), while Acts distinguishes the Father’s authority, the Spirit’s work, and Jesus’ future return (Acts 1:7-11). Within this context, Jesus’ real presence through God’s Spirit and his delegated authority is sufficient to explain the promises without concluding that he possesses YHVH’s unlimited presence.
