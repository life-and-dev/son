## Was Jesus God Incarnate?

> “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us.” — John 1:14 (ESV)

The strongest Trinitarian reading takes the Word in John 1:1–18 to be an eternal, personal divine Son. On that reading, “became flesh” means that this Son assumed a complete human nature while remaining God. It accounts for the Word’s pre-existence, the Word’s role in creation, and the glory John says the disciples saw in Jesus (John 1:1–3, 14–18).

Yet John’s context continues to distinguish God from Jesus Christ: “No one has ever seen God”; it is Jesus Christ who makes him known (John 1:18). Jesus later calls the Father “the only true God” and himself the one whom the Father sent (John 17:3). Thus the prologue need not identify Jesus as YHVH himself. It can present God’s own life-giving word, purpose, and self-revelation becoming flesh in the man through whom God is finally made known; this is not an impersonal abstraction, but neither does the passage expressly name the Word an eternal second divine person.

Matthew and Luke locate Jesus’ beginning as God’s Son in his conception in Mary by God’s Holy Spirit—God’s own Spirit and power—not in a descent from heaven (Matthew 1:18, 20; Luke 1:35). The same Jesus grows in wisdom (Luke 2:52), says there are things the Son does not know (Mark 13:32), dies, and is raised by God (Acts 2:24). A two-natures reading supplies a divine nature to explain these contrasts; the narratives themselves instead emphasise God acting in and through his genuinely human Messiah. God gives him authority because he is the Son of Man (John 5:27), rather than Jesus possessing authority as God by nature.
