# Was a worthy price required?

Some belief that [a single human being is not worthy enough](/god/son/essence/of-man/crucifixion) to pay the price that Jesus did. This is based on all sorts of reasoning like:

## Did God bargain with the devil?

Some believe that the devil owns everyone who sins and that Jesus had to buy his disciples back by paying with his life. Hebrews 2:14-15 does not make that claim. It says that the devil held the power of death and that people were enslaved by their fear of death. It presents Jesus’ death as breaking that power and freeing those people, not as a payment negotiated with the devil.

The passage therefore gives no basis for treating the crucifixion as a transaction in which the devil owned sinners, set a price, or received Jesus’ life. Sin, death, and the devil’s opposition are real, but Hebrews directs the saving work to God’s purpose and Christ’s faithful offering to God (Hebrews 2:14-18; 9:14).

### Quantity versus quality

The strongest Trinitarian argument says that only a divine person could offer a sacrifice sufficient for the sins of many. Scripture instead presents Jesus’ saving work through his obedient human identity as the last Adam and God’s appointed servant.

Paul deliberately makes the comparison human to human: “For as by the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous” (Romans 5:19). Likewise, “since death came through a man, the resurrection of the dead comes also through a man” (1 Corinthians 15:21). The force of the argument is not an infinite divine value. It is God’s gracious remedy through one faithful man.

Isaiah describes the servant as bearing the sins of many because “the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all” (Isaiah 53:6,11-12). Jesus gives his life willingly, but God is the one who provides, vindicates, and raises him (Acts 2:23-24; Acts 3:13-15). His sacrifice is sufficient because God accepted His sinless Messiah, not because the Messiah is God Himself.

#### Contextual Distinction

The strongest Trinitarian case says salvation needs an infinite sacrifice because sin is against an infinite God. The apostles instead ground the saving value of Jesus’ death in God’s purpose and Jesus’ faithful obedience. God “gave his only Son” (John 3:16); Christ “gave himself as a ransom for all” (1 Timothy 2:5-6); and by one man’s obedience many are made righteous (Romans 5:18-19). The sacrifice is sufficient because God accepted and appointed His sinless human Messiah, not because the victim is God.

### God had always been the owner

Scripture identifies the LORD as Creator and owner of the earth and its inhabitants (Psalms 24:1-2). Human sin does not transfer ownership of people to the devil. It brings guilt, death, and alienation from God.

This does not mean that God saves everyone. God retains the right to judge all people, while the gospel proclaims reconciliation through His Son. Hebrews describes that reconciliation through Christ’s priestly work before God, not through any transfer of ownership from the devil.

## A high penalty was required for breaking the old covenant?

Some reason that because the Israelites broke [the Mosaic covenant](/god/covenants/mosaic) (also known as the "Old Covenant" or "Old Testament"), that one of their descendants had to pay the penalty. While there are many scriptures that states that Jesus died for their sin, there is none that specifically said that Jesus had to die because they broke their covenant.

Instead, we read that the Assyrian (2 Kings 17:7-23) and Babylonian (2 Kings 24:1-4; Jeremiah 25:8-11; Ezekiel 39:23-24; Daniel 9:11-14; Amos 5:25-27) exiles were the results for breaking the Old Covenant.

# Was special authority required?

## Did Jesus cancel the Old covenants?

Hebrews 7-10 addresses the covenantal system centred on the Levitical priesthood, sacrifices, and sanctuary. It does not treat that system as permanently unchanged. A change of priesthood entails a change of law (Hebrews 7:12), and the former command concerning the priestly order is set aside because it could not bring perfection (Hebrews 7:18-19).

Hebrews calls Jesus the mediator of a better covenant, enacted on better promises (Hebrews 8:6). By quoting Jeremiah, it says that the new covenant makes the first covenant obsolete and ready to disappear (Hebrews 8:7-13). It also says that Christ takes away the first in order to establish the second (Hebrews 10:9). These statements rule out the claim that the old covenantal order lasts forever in the same binding form.

Jesus’ statement that he came not to abolish but to fulfil the Law and the Prophets (Matthew 5:17) should not be used to deny Hebrews’ argument. Fulfilment is compatible with the completion and replacement of the former sacrificial and priestly order by the new covenant.

This conclusion concerns the old covenantal administration discussed in Hebrews. It does not require treating every promise made to Abraham or every use of the word “covenant” elsewhere in Scripture as identical to that administration. The question remains how Jesus serves as its decisive high priest.

## Did Jesus offer a blood sacrifice as a high priest?

Hebrews, not Matthew 1, supplies the relevant argument about Jesus’ ancestry and priesthood. It says that Jesus came from Judah, a tribe about which Moses gave no instruction concerning altar service (Hebrews 7:13-14). His priesthood is therefore not Levitical. Hebrews identifies him instead as a priest for ever according to the order of Melchizedek, appointed by God and possessing an indestructible life (Hebrews 7:15-17,20-25).

This does not mean that Jesus performed a Levitical sacrifice at an earthly altar or that the crucifixion followed the ritual requirements for Aaron’s sons. Hebrews contrasts the repeated Levitical offerings with Christ’s single offering. As high priest of the greater and more perfect tent, he entered once for all with his own blood and obtained eternal redemption (Hebrews 9:11-14). Through the offering of his body, those being sanctified are made perfect for all time (Hebrews 10:10-14).

The strongest Trinitarian reading holds that Hebrews’ language about the eternal Son and his unique priesthood supports a divine person offering a sacrifice of unlimited worth. Hebrews does present the Son as unique and exalted. Yet its stated argument for the sufficiency and finality of the offering is God’s appointment, Jesus’ indestructible life, his sinlessness, and his once-for-all self-offering. It does not say that the sacrifice is accepted because Jesus is God Himself.

# Did God have to proof Himself?

## Did God have to proof that He loves us?

> For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly. For one will scarcely die for a righteous person (though perhaps for a good person one would dare even to die) but **God shows his love for us** in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. -- Romans 5:6-8 (ESV)

Often people confuse God and Jesus with each other and by quoting John 3:16 and Romans 5:6-8 to proof that Jesus loved the world so much that he was willing to gave himself up (sacrifice himself) to save us.  The argument assume that unless God sacrificed Himself, he cannot proof his love to us.

 God also proves his love:
    * by providing a way for us to be [saved](/eternal/saved)
    * by His ministering [Spirit](/god/spirit)
    * by His [gifts](/god/spirit/gifts)
    * by [the body of Christ](/kingdom/body)
    * by [His creation](/creation)
    * by His [blessing](/life/blessings)
    * by testimonies:
        * from Moses (Exodus 34:6-7)
        * from Solomon (1 Kings 8:23)
        * from David (Psalm 86:5; 103:8-17)
        * from Isaiah (Isaiah 54:10)
        * from Jeremiah (Jeremiah 31:3)
        * from Nehemiah (Nehemiah 9:17)
        * from Jesus' (especially John 14-17)
        * from John (1 John 4:7-8)
        * from Paul (Romans 5:8)
        * and so forth...

While it is true that Jesus did gave himself up as a hero to save those whom he loves,

## Did God have to proof that He can defeat the devil as a human?

Some belief that the purpose of the crucifixion was that God had to prove that He could limit Himself as a human and still be able to conquer the devil without falling for the temptation to use any of His god-powers to defeat the devil.

> Since the children have flesh and blood, he too shared in their humanity so that **by his death he might break the power of him who holds the power of death (that is, the devil)** and free those who all their lives were held in slavery by their fear of death. -- Hebrews 2:14-15 (NIV)

Note that only [the unknown author of the book of Hebrews](/bible/authors/hebrews) support this view. No other bible authors made any such claims.

The questions that arise are:

1. Why would God have to proof Himself?
2. How did the invincible God got killed?
3. How did God got separated from Himself?

# Did God establish a new covenant?

[God the Father](/god/father) established a [new covenant](/god/son/crucifixion#the-purpose-of-the-new-covenant), with the blood of [His son](/god/son), to restore [righteousness](/bible/concepts/righteousness) to us. Reconciliation would not have been possible without removing the division (cleansing of [sin](/bible/concepts/sin)) between God and us. Therefore, the Son of God voluntarily offered himself as hero to provide [the way to eternal life with the Father](/god/son/purpose) Who loves to [adopt us as His children](/god/sons-of-god) so that we can inherit as fellow heirs with [His Son](/god/son), Jesus [Christ](/god/son/christ), whom He had resurrected like [He would also resurrect us one day](/eternal/death) into [eternal life](/eternal/new-earth) as sealed by God's new covenant.
