## Bible translations

The strongest Trinitarian reading of Mark 2:5-12 is that Jesus forgave a sin against God, so he must be God. The scribes state the issue clearly: “Who can forgive sins but God alone?” Jesus then says that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins (Mark 2:7,10).

The decisive word is authority, not identity. Matthew’s parallel records the crowd’s conclusion after the healing: they “glorified God, who had given such authority to men” (Matthew 9:8). The crowd did not infer that Jesus was God. They praised God for granting authority to a human representative.

Jesus’ authority is extraordinary, but the Gospel repeatedly locates it in the Father’s commission. The Father gave the Son authority to judge (John 5:27), and the risen Jesus says that all authority “has been given to me” (Matthew 28:18). Forgiveness announced by God’s authorised Messiah is not a denial that God alone is the ultimate forgiver. It distinguishes God, who grants and confirms forgiveness, from His Son, who carries out the commission.

### Contextual Distinction

The strongest objection remains serious: sins are ultimately against God, and Jesus speaks forgiveness before God. Yet prophets could announce God’s forgiveness as His authorised servants. Nathan told David, “The LORD also has put away your sin” (2 Samuel 12:13), without being YHVH. Mark’s point is that the Son of Man has authority on earth, and Matthew states that God gave such authority to men (Mark 2:10; Matthew 9:8). Forgiveness through the Messiah is divine mercy exercised by God’s authorised human agent, not an identity claim.

## Crowd's reaction

Mark 2:1-12 ends when the healed man rises, takes up his bed, and goes out before everyone. Mark 2:13 begins a new scene by the sea. The response in the forgiveness scene is therefore the crowd’s amazement and praise to God: “We never saw anything like this!” (Mark 2:12).

Matthew’s parallel adds that the crowd “glorified God, who had given such authority to men” (Matthew 9:8). The strongest Trinitarian reading can answer that the crowd’s response does not by itself define Jesus’ identity. Mark’s narrative may present Jesus’ authority as unique, especially because he pronounces forgiveness and then confirms it by healing.

Nevertheless, Matthew’s wording supplies contextual support for a delegated-authority reading. The crowd praises God as the giver of this authority and describes it as authority given to men. This fits the claim that Jesus, the human Son of Man, acts as God’s authorised Messiah. It does not require that Jesus be God Himself.

## Defining forgiveness

In Mark 2:5, the Greek verb translated “forgiven” is *aphiéntai*, a form of *aphiēmi*. In this context, the word refers to the remission of sins. Its meaning must be taken from the sentence and scene, not from a proposed breakdown of the word into parts. [Bible Hub’s entry for *aphiēmi*](https://biblehub.com/greek/863.htm) includes “forgive” and “release” among its contextual glosses.

The scribes’ objection shows why the claim is significant: they understand the forgiveness to concern sins for which God alone is the ultimate judge (Mark 2:7). The strongest Trinitarian reading is that Jesus’ pronouncement, followed by his claim that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, identifies him with God’s unique prerogative (Mark 2:10).

The contextual counter is not that forgiveness is merely interpersonal. Jesus claims real authority to pronounce forgiveness. The question is whether that authority must be inherent to God or can be granted to God’s Messiah. Matthew’s parallel says that God gave such authority to men (Matthew 9:8), and Jesus later commissions his disciples to forgive or retain sins (John 20:21-23). These passages support the Unitarian reading that God remains the ultimate forgiver while He authorises His human representatives to declare and administer His forgiveness.
