## Contextual Distinction

The strongest Trinitarian reading of Revelation 5 is that every creature gives the Lamb the worship due only to God. John’s vision certainly gives the Lamb extraordinary honour. Revelation 22:16 also identifies Jesus as the speaker who says, “I, Jesus, have sent My angel to testify to you these things in the churches.” This supplies important speaker evidence for reading the preceding first-person statements, including “I am the Alpha and the Omega” in Revelation 22:13, as Jesus' words. It therefore strengthens the Trinitarian case that Revelation applies divine language to Jesus.

A Unitarian reading should not overlook that evidence. Yet Revelation 5 still distinguishes the Lamb from “Him who sits on the throne”: the Lamb receives the scroll from God's hand because he was slain and redeemed people for God (Revelation 5:6-10). Revelation 22:16 identifies the speaker in its immediate closing context; it does not by itself identify the Lamb as God Almighty in Revelation 5. Revelation can honour God and His Lamb together while maintaining their distinction.

The falling down before the Lamb deserves the same care. In Revelation 5:8 the living creatures and elders fall before the Lamb, and in 5:14 the elders again fall down after all creation blesses both “Him who sits on the throne” and the Lamb. The Greek *proskyneō* can describe bowing or prostration in homage, and in a religious vision it may express worship; its meaning should not be reduced in advance either to ordinary respect or to proof that its recipient is God. The immediate setting—who receives the act, why, and how John elsewhere contrasts God, the Lamb, angels, and creatures—must guide the reading.

The Trinitarian counter is substantial: Revelation repeatedly uses falling down and *proskyneō* for God (for example, Revelation 4:10; 7:11), and it sharply refuses such devotion to an angel: “Worship God” (Revelation 19:10; 22:8-9). In that context, the Lamb's reception of universal praise and the elders' prostration can reasonably be read as sharing the worship due to God, not as mere courtly honour. A Unitarian reading can acknowledge that high devotional force while observing that Revelation 5 still names two recipients and explains the Lamb's exaltation through his faithful death and God's grant of authority. Falling before the Lamb therefore shows his uniquely God-authorized, heavenly honour; it does not, by itself, settle whether he is identical with the one seated on the throne.
