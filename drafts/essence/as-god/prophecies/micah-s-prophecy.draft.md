Matthew recorded that the wise men told Herod:

> “In Bethlehem of Judea, for thus it is written by the prophet:  
> ‘But you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah,  
> Are not the least among the rulers of Judah;  
> For out of you shall come a **ruler**  
> Who will shepherd My people Israel.’ ”
>
> -- Matthew 2:5-6 (NKJV)

They quoted Micah who wrote this about Jesus:

> “But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah,  
> Though you are little among the thousands of Judah,   
> Yet out of you shall come forth to Me  
> The one to be **ruler** in Israel,  
> **Whose goings forth are from of old, from everlasting**.”
>
> -- Micah 5:2 (NKJV)

The strongest Trinitarian reading is that "whose goings forth are from of old, from everlasting" means that Jesus personally existed before Bethlehem, even from eternity. Since Matthew identifies Bethlehem's ruler with Jesus, the phrase can appear to identify him as God.

The immediate context identifies the ruler differently. He is born from a woman, rules Israel in YHVH's strength, and acts in the name of "YHVH his God":

> Therefore **he** shall give them up,    
> Until the time that she who is in labor has given birth;    
> Then the remnant of **his** brethren shall return to the children of Israel.    
> And **he** shall stand and feed His flock in the strength of **the LORD**,  
> In the majesty of the name of **the LORD ([YHVH](/god/father/name.md)) his God**;  
> And they shall abide,  
> For now **he** shall be great to the ends of the earth;  
> And **this one** shall be peace.
>
> -- Micah 5:2-5 (NKJV)

This ruler, whom Matthew identifies as Jesus, serves under the authority of YHVH, who is "his God." The canonical pattern agrees: Jesus calls the Father "my God" (John 20:17; Revelation 3:12). The prophecy therefore presents God's Messiah and shepherd, not YHVH himself.

Matthew's abbreviated quotation emphasizes the ruler and shepherd of Israel (Matthew 2:5-6). It does not require that every phrase in Micah 5:2 describe personal pre-existence, nor does it identify the ruler as YHVH.

What did Micah mean by "from of old, from days of *olam*"?

> There are three phrases at the end of Micah 5:2 that may, or may not refer to eternality, and understanding these three phrases is essential:
>
> 1. *motsa'otav* (מוֹצָאֹתָיו), "his goings forth" or "origins".
> 2. *miqedem* (מִקֶּדֶם), "from before," "from old," or "from long ago."
> 3. *mimei olam* (מִימֵי עוֹלָם), literally "from days of old"; translations may render *olam* "everlasting" where context warrants.
>
> Scripture also uses *olam* and *qedem* for Israel's historical past, not necessarily eternity:
>
>> Micah 7:14 recalls grazing "as in the days of old" (*yemei olam*).
>
>> Micah 7:20 recalls the promises sworn to the fathers "from the days of old" (*mimei qedem*), the patriarchal past.
>
>> Psalm 44:1 remembers God's deeds "in the days of old" (*bimei qedem*).
>
>> Psalm 77:5, 11 recalls "days of old" and YHVH's ancient deeds for Israel.
>    
>> Isaiah 63:11 speaks of "days of old," specifically Moses and his people.
>
> These uses show that the phrases can refer to a remote historical age; they do not by themselves prove eternity past.
>
> In Micah 5:2, "days of *olam*" can naturally point to God's ancient Davidic purpose. Bethlehem is David's town, and the ruler fulfills the promise of David's enduring dynasty (2 Samuel 7:12-16; Psalm 89:3-4). This reading explains the elevated language without making the Messiah identical with the God in whose strength he shepherds.

The wording may invite discussion of pre-existence, but it cannot establish that Jesus is YHVH. Micah's own context, Matthew's presentation of Jesus' birth, and Jesus' confession of the Father as his God keep identity, agency, and Messiahship distinct (Micah 5:4; Matthew 1:18-25; John 20:17).
