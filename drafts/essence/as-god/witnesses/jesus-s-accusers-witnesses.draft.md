# Jewish mob

John records an accusation, not a direct quotation of Jesus claiming to be God: “you, being a man, make yourself God” (John 10:33). The charge follows Jesus’ claim that he and the Father are one (John 10:30). The accusers’ interpretation matters, but John lets Jesus answer it.

Jesus cites Psalm 82:6 and then states his own claim: “I am the Son of God” (John 10:34-36). The psalm’s referent is debated, but its argument is clear enough for Jesus’ purpose: people who receive God’s word may bear elevated language without becoming the one God. Jesus does not deny his unique mission. He describes himself as the one whom the Father consecrated and sent.

The strongest Trinitarian reading takes the crowd’s charge, Jesus’ unity with the Father, and his unique sonship together as an implicit claim to deity. The Unitarian reply is that Jesus redirects the issue to authorised agency. His works show that the Father is in him and he is in the Father, while the Father remains the sender whose works Jesus does (John 10:32,36-38). Unity, title, and agency do not erase the distinction between God and His human Messiah.

# Jesus’ Trial

At Jesus’ trial, the high priest asks whether he is “the Christ, the Son of God” (Matthew 26:63; Mark 14:61). Jesus’ reply invokes Psalm 110:1 and Daniel 7:13-14: the Son of Man will sit at the right hand of Power and come with the clouds of heaven (Matthew 26:64; Mark 14:62). Luke likewise records the council asking whether he is the Messiah and then the Son of God (Luke 22:67-71).

The strongest Trinitarian reading sees Jesus’ cloud-coming Son of Man language as a claim to YHVH’s divine prerogatives. It also notes that the council calls his answer blasphemy. The text must therefore not be reduced to an ordinary political claim.

The Unitarian reply follows the passages Jesus cites. Daniel’s Son of Man receives dominion, glory, and a kingdom from the Ancient of Days; Psalm 110 places David’s lord at the LORD’s right hand. Jesus claims the exalted Messiah’s role and future authority, but both texts distinguish the recipient of authority from God who grants it. The trial charge concerns his messianic claim and its exalted implications, not a recorded statement that he is God in flesh.
