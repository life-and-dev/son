# Thomas’ Witness

Thomas’ words to the risen Jesus deserve their full grammatical force. The immediate context, Jesus’ statement about “my God,” and John’s stated purpose guide a fair assessment of what the confession establishes.

## Text and Grammar

John 20:17 records Jesus saying, “my Father and your Father, my God and your God.” John 20:28 then records Thomas answering Jesus: “My Lord and my God!” The phrase is addressed to Jesus in the scene; its grammar permits direct address. A fair Unitarian reading must not turn “my God” into an address to someone else.

The two titles do not invoke Granville Sharp’s rule, because each has its own article in Greek. That observation neither proves two referents nor settles ontology. Grammar establishes Thomas’ direct and emphatic confession; the question is what divine title means within John’s narrative and biblical agency.

## Readings of the Confession

The mainstream Trinitarian reading takes both titles as predicates of Jesus. Thomas directly confesses the risen Jesus as “my Lord and my God,” and the statement functions as the Gospel’s climactic confession of Jesus’ deity. This reading best accounts for the direct address and must be acknowledged.

The Unitarian reply accepts that Thomas addresses Jesus. It argues that the confession does not itself define later Nicene metaphysics or overturn Jesus’ words eleven verses earlier: “my God and your God” (John 20:17). In biblical agency, a representative may bear God’s name, titles, authority, and honour without becoming identical with the one God. Thomas’ confession can therefore recognise the risen Messiah as God’s supreme authorised representative, while the Father remains Jesus’ God.

## The Narrator's Purpose

John 20:31 gives the narrator’s purpose: readers may believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God, and by believing have life in his name. The Trinitarian reading can take this purpose alongside Thomas’ confession. The Unitarian reading takes Messiah and Son of God as the Gospel’s stated category while retaining the direct force of John 20:28. The verse does not settle every later ontological question; it identifies the faith John seeks to produce.
