## The Unitarian Reply

The two-natures distinction is a later theological explanation; the apostolic contrast itself remains simple. Jesus died, while God raised him from the dead (Acts 2:24; Acts 3:15; Romans 10:9). Jesus is the human Messiah through whom God acted and whom God vindicated. God’s Spirit empowered Jesus, but divine agency does not make the agent identical with the God who empowers him (Acts 10:38).

Acts 20:28 contains a textual question: the manuscript tradition includes “church of God,” “church of the Lord,” and combined readings. The strongest Trinitarian reading follows “church of God” and takes “which he obtained with his own blood” in its ordinary grammatical sense: God is the subject who obtained the church, and “his own blood” is God’s blood. Trinitarians therefore understand the verse through the incarnation: the one called God shed blood in the human nature of the Son. That is a serious reading of the wording; the Greek does not itself say that divine nature, considered apart from the incarnate Son, has flesh and blood.

The Unitarian reading does not deny that the verse can be translated “with his own blood.” It holds that the wording is not enough to identify Jesus with God, especially where the textual reading itself is disputed and where *tou idiou* (“his own”) can be understood substantively, “his own one,” with “Son” supplied from the context. More importantly, the New Testament consistently distinguishes God from the one who died: Romans 5:10 says believers were reconciled to God “through the death of his Son,” not through the blood of God; Hebrews 9:14 says that Christ offered himself without blemish **to God** through the eternal Spirit. Thus Acts 20:28 should be read alongside, not against, the repeated distinction between God, who raises, and his Messiah, who dies and is raised.
