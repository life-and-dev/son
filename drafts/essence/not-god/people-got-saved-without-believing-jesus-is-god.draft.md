# Peter's sermon

Peter’s Pentecost sermon identifies the God of Israel as the actor throughout. God attests Jesus as “a man” by miracles God does through him, raises him, and exalts him to God’s right hand (Acts 2:22-24,32-33). Peter uses Psalm 110:1 to distinguish “the LORD” from David’s “Lord,” then concludes that God made the crucified Jesus “both Lord and Messiah” (Acts 2:34-36).

The strongest Trinitarian reading joins Joel 2:32 in Acts 2:21 with the risen Jesus’ title “Lord,” and sees YHVH’s saving identity applied to Jesus. Peter does connect salvation with calling on the Lord and with Jesus’ name. Yet his explanation grounds Jesus’ lordship in God’s resurrection and exaltation. Jesus is God’s reigning Messiah; the one who is raised is not thereby identical with the God who raised him.

Peter calls hearers to repent, be baptised in Jesus’ name, and receive forgiveness (Acts 2:38). Jesus’ name has a necessary saving role because God made him Lord and Messiah. The sermon does not state that forgiveness requires belief that Jesus is God.

# Paul's sermon in Antioch

In the synagogue at Antioch, Paul begins with “the God of this people Israel,” who chose the fathers, gave Israel David, and fulfilled His promise (Acts 13:17,22-23). Jesus appears within that promise as David’s descendant: “from this man’s offspring God has brought to Israel a Saviour, Jesus” (Acts 13:23). Paul grounds the good news in God’s raising of Jesus and offers forgiveness and justification through him (Acts 13:30,38-39).

The strongest Trinitarian reply is that the Old Testament names YHVH the only Saviour, so Jesus as Saviour shares YHVH’s identity. Paul’s wording nevertheless preserves source and agent. God brings the Davidic Saviour and raises him; Jesus is the promised descendant through whom God grants forgiveness. “Saviour” can name God as ultimate deliverer and His Messiah as the appointed means of deliverance.

Paul calls hearers to believe God’s fulfilled promise concerning His raised Messiah (Acts 13:32-33,38-39). The sermon gives Jesus a decisive saving office, without making belief that Jesus is God its stated condition of forgiveness.

# Paul's sermon in Areopagus

At the Areopagus, Paul first identifies God as the one Creator. God made the world and all people, gives life and breath, commands all people to repent, and will judge the world in righteousness (Acts 17:24-27,30-31). Paul identifies the judge as “a man whom he has appointed,” and gives assurance by saying that God raised this man from the dead.

The strongest Trinitarian reading observes that the New Testament elsewhere gives Jesus universal judgement and calls for honour to the Son. Acts 17 itself explains the judge’s authority by appointment. God remains the universal judge, and Jesus is the human representative through whom judgement is carried out. Delegated judicial authority is not an assertion that the appointed man is God.

Paul’s first demand is that idolaters turn to the Creator. His proclamation then calls them to account before the risen man God appointed. This address presents one God and His raised human judge without stating that Gentiles must confess the judge to be God.
