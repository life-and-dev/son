# Jesus compared to Adam

Paul builds his resurrection argument by comparing Adam and Christ as human representatives:

> “For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead.” — 1 Corinthians 15:21 (ESV)

> “The first man Adam became a living being”; the last Adam became a life-giving spirit. — 1 Corinthians 15:45 (ESV)

> “The first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven.” — 1 Corinthians 15:47 (ESV)

Adam's disobedience brought death to those identified with him. Christ's obedience and resurrection bring life to those identified with him (Romans 5:12-19; 1 Corinthians 15:21-22,45-49). Paul therefore calls Jesus “the man Christ Jesus” when describing his mediating work between God and humankind (1 Timothy 2:5-6).

The strongest Trinitarian reading takes “the second man is from heaven” as evidence that the eternal Son existed in heaven before becoming human. John also says that the Word became flesh (John 1:1,14). The passage can support a belief in pre-existence, but its immediate contrast is between Adam's earthly, mortal origin and the risen Christ's heavenly, life-giving status. Paul says that believers will bear the image of “the man of heaven” (1 Corinthians 15:49). He does not call Christ God in this argument.

The comparison does not make Jesus ordinary or deny his unique origin, sinlessness, authority, and resurrection. It identifies him as God's appointed human representative. God raised the man Jesus and appointed him judge (Acts 17:31); through the obedience of this one man, many are made righteous (Romans 5:18-19).

# Jesus is not God

Scripture does not use the sentence “Jesus is not God.” Its case is made by repeated distinctions between the one God and the Messiah He sent. Jesus calls the Father “the only true God” and identifies himself as the one sent by that God (John 17:3). After his resurrection, he says, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (John 20:17). Paul likewise distinguishes “one God, the Father” from “one Lord, Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:6) and calls Jesus the human mediator between God and humankind (1 Timothy 2:5).

Trinitarians reply that these texts distinguish persons, not natures: the Son can call the Father his God in his incarnate human life while sharing the divine nature. They also point to Thomas's confession, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28), and to the Word being God before becoming flesh (John 1:1,14).

Those texts deserve their weight. Yet Thomas's confession occurs in a Gospel whose stated purpose is that readers believe Jesus is “the Christ, the Son of God” (John 20:31), not that Jesus is the Father or the only true God. John records Jesus praying to the Father as the only true God, and Paul consistently presents God as the one who raised, exalted, and gave authority to Jesus (Acts 2:32-36; Philippians 2:8-11). Taken together, these passages support confession of Jesus as God's unique human Son and Christ, with the Father alone as God.
