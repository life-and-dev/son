# Views on Jesus

Christian views of Jesus differ sharply. This article tests those claims by the Bible's repeated distinction between the one God, the Father, and Jesus Christ, God's human Son and Messiah. It also considers major Trinitarian texts fairly in their literary context.

The biblical Unitarian position is that YHVH, the Father, alone is God; Jesus is the Messiah whom God brought into being through Mary, raised from the dead, and exalted; and the Holy Spirit is God's own Spirit and power at work.

## How Jesus is identified

### How God identifies Himself

Israel's confession begins with one personal referent: “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one” (Deuteronomy 6:4). YHVH says, “I am the LORD, and there is no other, besides me there is no God” (Isaiah 45:5). He alone made the heavens and earth (Isaiah 44:24).

Jesus affirms this confession. Asked for the greatest commandment, he answers, “The Lord our God, the Lord is one” (Mark 12:29). In prayer he addresses the Father as “the only true God” and identifies himself separately as “Jesus Christ whom you have sent” (John 17:3). After the resurrection, Paul likewise writes, “for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist” (1 Corinthians 8:6).

This pattern does not diminish Jesus. It identifies God as the Father and Jesus as the Lord Messiah appointed by that God. It also gives the ordinary biblical meaning to Jesus' title “Son of God”: it marks his unique relationship and commission, not a second God alongside YHVH.

### How Jesus identifies himself

At Caesarea Philippi, Peter confesses, “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God.” Jesus calls this revelation from “my Father who is in heaven” (Matthew 16:16-17). Jesus does not correct Peter by saying that he is God. He confirms the confession that he is God's Messiah and Son.

Jesus repeatedly distinguishes himself from God. He says, “I can do nothing on my own” and seeks “the will of him who sent me” (John 5:30). He calls the Father “the only true God” (John 17:3), and after his resurrection says, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (John 20:17). In John 8:40, Jesus describes himself as “a man who has told you the truth that I heard from God.”

Jesus is more than an ordinary prophet. The Father gives him authority to raise the dead and judge (John 5:21-27), yet the text states that this authority is given. His dependence, obedience, suffering, and exaltation fit the promised human Messiah whom God commissions and vindicates.

### How Gabriel Identifies Jesus

Gabriel announces Jesus' origin and identity before his birth: “You will conceive in your womb and bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus.” He will receive “the throne of his father David,” and “the child to be born will be called holy. The Son of God” (Luke 1:31-35).

The explanation is concrete. The Holy Spirit comes upon Mary and the power of the Most High overshadows her. The text therefore calls Jesus God's Son; it does not say that God became the child. His promised Davidic throne places him within Israel's hope for a human king (2 Samuel 7:12-16; Luke 1:32-33).

### How the apostles identified Jesus

The apostolic preaching identifies Jesus as the man and Messiah whom God authenticated, raised, and exalted. Peter proclaims: “Jesus of Nazareth, a man attested to you by God with mighty works and wonders and signs that God did through him” (Acts 2:22). He concludes, “God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified” (Acts 2:36).

Paul preserves the same distinction: “there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). God raised Jesus from the dead (Romans 10:9), seated him at his right hand, and made him “both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:32-36). Jesus' lordship is real and universal, but it is received from God.

Philippians 2:5-11 also ends with this order. Because Jesus humbled himself in obedience to death, “God has highly exalted him” and every tongue confesses that “Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.” The passage presents the obedient Messiah receiving supreme honour from God, not the Father exalting himself.

### Strongest Trinitarian Response and Reply

Trinitarians commonly appeal to John 1:1-14, where the Word was with God and “was God” before becoming flesh; Thomas' confession, “My Lord and my God” (John 20:28); Philippians 2:6-11; Colossians 1:15-17; and Hebrews 1. These texts give Jesus extraordinary status, use divine language of him, and connect him with God's creative and saving work. A biblical Unitarian reading must take that evidence seriously.

The direct reply begins with each passage's context. John opens with God's word, through which God creates, becoming flesh in the man Jesus. The Prologue can therefore be read as God's self-expression and saving purpose embodied in Jesus, rather than as a second eternal person. Thomas addresses the risen Jesus with the highest honour, but John's stated purpose is that readers may believe “that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God” (John 20:31).

Philippians 2 presents Jesus' self-giving obedience and says that God then highly exalted him. Colossians calls him “the image of the invisible God” and “firstborn of all creation,” language that can describe God's supreme representative and heir. Hebrews 1 exalts the Son above angels, yet it also says of the Son, “God, your God, has anointed you” (Hebrews 1:9). None of these passages explicitly identifies Jesus as the one God whom Jesus calls his Father. Their highest claims are read alongside the repeated statements that God sent, empowered, raised, and exalted Jesus.

## How Jesus Relates to Others

### How God relates to Jesus

At Jesus' baptism, Jesus stands in the Jordan, the Spirit descends upon him, and a voice from heaven says, “You are my beloved Son; with you I am well pleased” (Mark 1:9-11). The three are distinguished, but the passage does not call them one triune God. It identifies the Father as the speaker, Jesus as the Son, and the Spirit as God's empowering presence.

Peter later summarises Jesus' ministry: “God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power. He went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him” (Acts 10:38). God works through his anointed servant. This matches Jesus' own words that the Father gives him authority and works through him (John 5:19-27).

### How Jesus relates to his Father and disciples

Jesus' prayer in John 17 states the relationship plainly. He asks the Father to glorify the Son, because the Father gave him authority over all flesh (John 17:1-2). He defines eternal life as knowing “you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent” (John 17:3).

The prayer also explains biblical unity. Jesus asks that believers “may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us” (John 17:21). The same language of being “in” one another extends to believers. In context, this is unity of love, purpose, and mission, not a statement that Jesus and believers share one divine essence.

Jesus says that the Father gave him words, people, glory, and authority (John 17:2,6-8,22). He comes from God and returns to God (John 16:28; 17:13). These are relations between God and his sent Messiah.

### Holy Spirit

The Holy Spirit is God's own Spirit and power, not another God alongside the Father. At Jesus' conception, Gabriel parallels “the Holy Spirit will come upon you” with “the power of the Most High will overshadow you” (Luke 1:35). Peter likewise says that “God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power” (Acts 10:38).

Scripture can speak of the Spirit acting, teaching, and being grieved (John 14:26; Ephesians 4:30). Those personal descriptions show God's active presence with his people. They do not require the conclusion that the Spirit is a distinct co-equal God. The Spirit proceeds from and belongs to God, who works through it (Psalm 104:30; Acts 2:17).

## Titles of Jesus

Jesus' titles should be read in their biblical settings. “Christ” means the anointed king. “Son of God” identifies God's chosen king and Son. “Son of Man” draws on Daniel 7:13-14, where a human figure receives authority from the Ancient of Days. “Lord” commonly marks the authority God grants to Jesus after his resurrection (Acts 2:36; Philippians 2:9-11).

These titles establish Jesus' unique authority without changing the Bible's confession that the Father is the one God (1 Corinthians 8:6).
