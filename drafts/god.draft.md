### Father, Son, and Spirit in Salvation

God sent His Son from love (John 3:16; 1 John 4:14). Jesus gave himself as a ransom (Mark 10:45; Ephesians 5:2), and God raised him (Acts 2:24). Believers have access to the Father through Christ in one Spirit (Ephesians 2:18). The Spirit is God’s gift, making believers new and helping them pray (Titus 3:5; Romans 8:26).

## Father, Son, and Spirit

Jesus calls the Father **“the only true God”** (John 17:3), and Paul writes **“one God, the Father”** and “one Lord, Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:6). Jesus also says “**my God and your God**” after resurrection (John 20:17). These distinctions are explicit, but distinction, agency, and genuine humanity do not by themselves prove that the Son is not God.

The strongest Trinitarian texts need passage-level treatment. In John 1:1, the Word is *with* God and the anarthrous predicate *theos* is commonly understood qualitatively: the Word has the nature of God, not that the Word is the Father. John 20:28 records Thomas addressing the risen Jesus, **“My Lord and my God!”** Classical interpretation takes this as a direct confession to the Son. Hebrews 1:8 addresses the Son as “God” in its Psalm quotation, then says, “God, your God, has anointed you” (Hebrews 1:8-9). Titus 2:13 has one article governing “great God and Saviour,” strongly favouring one referent, Jesus Christ. These texts form a serious cumulative case.

Classical Trinitarians read the distinctions through eternal Sonship and two natures: the Son shares divine nature with the Father and truly assumes human nature. Hence obedience, prayer, suffering, death, and received authority describe the incarnate Son without denying deity. First Timothy 3:16 needs different treatment: its direct “God was manifested” wording depends on a later textual reading; critical editions usually read “who was manifested in flesh.” That variant does not dissolve other high texts, but it limits this verse as direct proof.

The Unitarian case is therefore not a deduction from Jesus’ humanity. *It argues that Scripture’s repeated pattern names the Father as the one God and Jesus as the Messiah whom God sends, raises, and exalts; high designations must be interpreted within that pattern.* See [Textual Variants and Direct-Deity Texts](textual-variants.draft.md#control-texts-and-cumulative-limits).

## Conclusion

The Father, Son, and Spirit are genuinely distinguished in Scripture. Whether high texts establish shared divine nature remains a debated conclusion assessed in [Direct-Deity Texts](textual-variants.draft.md#control-texts-and-cumulative-limits).
