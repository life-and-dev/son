# Man Of God

## Stable definitions before title argument

The stable definitions are in [God](../../word/terms/god.md#stable-definitions). Titles can express agency, representation, office, or relationship. A title applied to Jesus does not alone settle his nature; a conclusion needs broader predicates and context, rather than circular proof from the title.

### Project usage

* **God** normally means the Father, the one God; **god** can denote deity, a ruler, judge, heavenly being, idol, or representative, depending on context.
* **man of God** means a human commissioned by God.
* **Son of God** can express sonship, kingship, Messiahship, agency, or representation; the title alone does not prove divine nature.
* **Messiah** means an anointed ruler or agent; the title alone does not settle nature.

### Mainstream Nicene usage

Mainstream Nicene theology confesses one God in three persons—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—and one divine being or essence. Each person is truly God, not three gods. **God** can denote the Triune God or a divine person, as context requires; **god** normally denotes deity, with contextual range.

* **man of God** remains a commissioned-human title unless context adds more.
* **Son of God**, when used of Jesus, identifies the eternal divine Son, consubstantial with the Father, incarnate.
* **Messiah** identifies Jesus as the promised Christ, while the title by itself does not establish a complete ontology.

## Definition from representative use

`Man of God` describes a person who belongs to God, is commissioned by God, or speaks for God. In its representative biblical uses, the title marks relationship and service; it does not by itself mean that the person is God. Moses is called “the man of God” (Deuteronomy 33:1; Joshua 14:6), Samuel is called “a man of God” (1 Samuel 9:6), and Elijah is called “a man of God” (1 Kings 17:18, 24).

The same pattern continues with Elisha (2 Kings 4:7, 9), an unnamed prophet (1 Kings 13:1), and David (Nehemiah 12:24, 36). The title is also used for Timothy (1 Timothy 6:11), while Scripture equips “the man of God” for good work (2 Timothy 3:16-17). These representative uses support the definition: a man of God is God's servant or representative, not a title that independently identifies someone as God.

## Jesus and the exact title

For Jesus-specific relevance, this page rests on the New Testament's explicit descriptions rather than on the exact phrase `man of God`. The New Testament instead calls him “a man attested to you by God” (Acts 2:22), a prophet (Luke 24:19; Acts 3:22-26), “the man Christ Jesus” who is mediator (1 Timothy 2:5), and the Messiah whom God anointed with holy spirit and power (Acts 10:38). These descriptions are consistent with the representative pattern above and with [Jesus Is Human](../son-of-man/human.md), [Why Jesus Had To Be Human](../son-of-man/why.md), and [Son Of Man](../son-of-man.md).

## Related titles are not synonyms

### Man of God

In project usage, a `man of God` is a human servant or representative commissioned by God, as shown by Moses, Samuel, Elijah, Elisha, David, Timothy, and the prophets (Deuteronomy 33:1; 1 Samuel 9:6; 1 Kings 17:24; 2 Kings 4:9; Nehemiah 12:36; 1 Timothy 6:11). Mainstream Nicene usage retains this commissioned-human sense unless context adds more.

### Son of God

In project usage, `Son of God` can identify sonship, kingship, Messiahship, agency, or representation: God promises concerning David's royal heir, “I will be his father, and he shall be my son” (2 Samuel 7:14); the king hears, “You are my Son” (Psalm 2:7); and Jesus is called Son of God in connection with his holy-spirit-originated birth (Luke 1:35). It does not alone prove divine nature. In mainstream Nicene usage, the title identifies Jesus as the eternal divine Son, consubstantial with the Father, incarnate. See [Son Of God](../index.md).

### Messiah or Christ

In project usage, `Messiah` or `Christ` means God's anointed ruler or agent: rulers oppose “the LORD and his anointed” (Psalm 2:2), God made Jesus “both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36), and God anointed Jesus with holy spirit and power (Acts 10:38). It does not alone settle nature. In mainstream Nicene usage, Messiah identifies Jesus as the promised Christ, while the title alone does not establish a complete ontology.

### Prophet

A `prophet` speaks God's message. God promises to put his words in the prophet's mouth (Deuteronomy 18:18-19), and Acts applies this prophetic expectation to Jesus (Acts 3:22-26).

### Agent

An `agent` is a sent person who acts under the sender's authority. Jesus says the Son does what he sees the Father doing and can do nothing from himself (John 5:19, 30); he speaks and acts as the Father taught and sent him (John 8:28-29).

### God

In this project, **God** normally means the Father, the one God, while **god** can denote deity, a ruler, judge, heavenly being, idol, or representative according to context. Jesus addresses the Father as “the only true God” (John 17:3), and Paul distinguishes “one God, the Father” from “one Lord, Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:6). Mainstream Nicene theology uses **God** for the Triune God or a divine person by context, while **god** normally denotes deity with contextual range. See [Shema](../shema.md), [Jesus Is Distinct From God](../son-of-man/distinct.md), and [Jesus Has A God](../son-of-man/has-a-god.md).

Jesus is called Messiah (Acts 2:36), Son of God (Luke 1:35), and prophet (Luke 24:19), while Acts 2:22 presents him as a man attested by God. These titles answer different questions; their overlap in Jesus does not make them synonyms. Therefore, none of these titles alone proves or disproves a claim about Jesus' nature.

## Strongest Nicene response

The formal Nicene wording says that the Son is “God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God,” “begotten, not made,” and “of the same being as the Father,” then says that he “came down and was made flesh, becoming human.” Chalcedonian wording describes one and the same eternal Son in two complete natures, divine and human, “without confusion, without change, without division, without separation.” Predicates apply to the same person according to nature; the doctrine does not teach two persons or a mixed, 50/50 nature. See [Trinity](../trinity.md). On this account, Acts 2:22 and 1 Timothy 2:5 affirm Jesus' human nature but do not deny his divine nature. Nicene readers also cite John 1:1, 14; John 20:28; and Colossians 2:9 in support of that conclusion.

## Project conclusion

The strongest project argument is that Scripture repeatedly distinguishes a commissioned man from God who commissions him: Jesus is human (Acts 2:22; 1 Timothy 2:5), approved by God (Acts 2:22), anointed by God (Acts 10:38), sent and taught by the Father (John 5:19, 30; John 8:28-29), and has one God over him (John 17:3; 1 Corinthians 8:6). See [Jesus Was Limited](../son-of-man/limitations.md), [Jesus Has A God](../son-of-man/has-a-god.md), [Jesus Serves God](../son-of-man/serve-god.md), and [Jesus Is Distinct From God](../son-of-man/distinct.md).

This project conclusion critiques the two-natures reading rather than a neutral lexical fact. The project objects that fully mortal and immortal, limited and unlimited, and similar claims appear contradictory when applied to one personal subject. It rejects the nature distinction as an insufficient resolution, while recognizing that Nicene doctrine applies predicates to one person according to nature. These texts identify Jesus as man and distinguish him from God without explicitly teaching two natures. The project reading rests on that broader context, not on `man of God`, `Son of God`, or Messiah as title-only proof (Acts 2:22; 1 Timothy 2:5; John 17:3).

## What the Title Argument Can and Cannot Prove

Trinitarians rightly caution that the absence of the exact title “man of God” for Jesus cannot disprove his deity. Biblical identity is not settled by one title, and Jesus receives titles and honours no ordinary prophet receives.

The positive human language must still carry its stated force. Paul contrasts the one God with “the man Christ Jesus,” the one mediator who gave himself as ransom (**First Timothy 2:5–6**). Peter calls Jesus “a man attested by God” (**Acts 2:22**). These are not complete Christological definitions, but together with God raising and exalting him, they support the reading of Jesus as God's human Messiah rather than God himself.

## Human Messiah Evidence Beyond a Title

The strongest Nicene objection is sound as far as it goes: Jesus is never simply classified by the exact title “man of God,” and a title's absence proves nothing. Moreover, **John 20:28**, **John 1:1–14**, and **Colossians 2:9** are read by Trinitarians as evidence that the human Jesus is the incarnate divine Son. The title argument must not evade those passages.

The direct reply does not infer Jesus’ humanity from an absent label. It follows explicit predicates: Jesus is “a man attested to you by God” (**Acts 2:22**); “the man Christ Jesus” mediates between “one God” and humankind (**First Timothy 2:5**); God anointed Jesus with Holy Spirit and power (**Acts 10:38**); and God appointed a man to judge the world and raised him from the dead (**Acts 17:31**). The Father is Jesus’ God in the risen Christ’s words, “my God and your God” (**John 20:17**), and Jesus calls the Father “the only true God” (**John 17:3**).

These facts distinguish God, his Spirit, and his Messiah without reducing Jesus to an ordinary prophet. Jesus receives authority, judgement, and universal allegiance because God grants them: “God highly exalted him” so that every tongue confesses Jesus Christ as Lord, “to the glory of God the Father” (**Philippians 2:9–11**). The project conclusion is therefore a contextual claim about the Father who commissions, anoints, raises, and exalts his human Son, not a title-only assertion.
