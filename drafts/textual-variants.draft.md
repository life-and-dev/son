# Textual Variants and Direct-Deity Texts

Manuscript readings, punctuation, grammar, translation, and theology are related but distinct questions. This study weighs major disputed texts fairly and identifies what each level of evidence can establish.

## Scope and method

This is the consolidation owner for direct-deity disputes in the `son` ledger family. It classifies the question at issue and points to the detailed project studies; it does not replace them. A manuscript reading, punctuation decision, grammatical construction, translation, and theological conclusion are different claims. A reading can be grammatically clear while its theological scope remains debated.

## New Testament passages

### Titus 2:13

**Categories:** grammar; translation; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/incarnation/did-god-appear-as-jesus.md#titus-213).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** The single article before “great God and Savior” (`τοῦ μεγάλου θεοῦ καὶ σωτῆρος`) most naturally joins both nouns to one referent, Jesus Christ. This is a strong grammatical basis for calling Jesus “our great God and Savior.”

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** The construction strongly favors one referent, but grammar identifies the referent of the paired titles; it does not define a later Nicene account of the Son’s relation to the Father. A Unitarian may therefore accept the grammatical force yet argue that the title is read within a wider New Testament pattern that distinguishes Jesus from the one God, the Father.

**Bounded conclusion:** One-referent grammar is strongly favored. The step from that designation to full Nicene doctrine is later theological inference, not a result grammar alone supplies.

### Romans 9:5

**Categories:** punctuation; grammar; subject/referent; translation; theological inference.  See detailed study.

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** Read continuously, `ὁ ὢν ἐπὶ πάντων θεὸς` describes Christ: he is “God over all, blessed forever.” The relative participle can naturally continue the description of Christ.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** Ancient Greek manuscripts did not provide the modern punctuation that decides whether this is a description of Christ or an independent doxology to God. Paul’s usual doxological patterns and his distinction between God and Christ make a break after “Christ” a live reading; the referent of “God over all” is genuinely disputed.

**Bounded conclusion:** No manuscript punctuation settles the question. The Christological reading is grammatically possible and historically important, but the doxology reading remains a defensible punctuation and referent decision.

### 1 Timothy 3:16

**Categories:** manuscript variant; grammar; translation; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/incarnation/is-jesus-god-s-body.md#1-timothy-316).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** The later reading `θεός` gives “God was manifested in flesh,” an explicit incarnation statement when the hymn is applied to Christ.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** The principal textual question is `θεός` (“God”) versus `ὃς` (“who”; with related readings). Modern critical editions favor `ὃς`, supported by early witnesses and versions; it introduces the person manifested without naming him “God.” The likely secondary `θεός` reading can arise from visual and grammatical clarification in copying, so the direct God claim depends on the disputed reading.

**Bounded conclusion:** The critical text favors `ὃς`, not `θεός`. The passage may still concern Christ, but its direct “God manifested” claim rests on a textual variant rather than the likely earliest wording.

### Acts 20:28

**Categories:** manuscript variant; grammar; lexical choice; translation; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/incarnation/does-god-have-blood.md).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** The critical reading “church of God, which he obtained through his own blood” directly joins God with redemptive blood and is read as referring to the incarnate Son.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** “God” and “Lord” are competing readings, though the critical text supports “God.” Even with “God,” `διὰ τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου` may mean “through his own blood” or use `τοῦ ἰδίου` substantivally, “through the blood of his own [one],” commonly understood as his Son. The syntax does not by itself choose decisively between those renderings.

**Bounded conclusion:** Critical-text evidence favors “church of God,” but the wording after it has a real syntactical dispute. Neither translation should be treated as settled proof by itself of how God relates to the blood.

### 2 Peter 1:1

**Categories:** grammar; translation; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/witnesses/peter-s-witness.md#peters-opening).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** “Our God and Savior Jesus Christ” (`τοῦ θεοῦ ἡμῶν καὶ σωτῆρος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ`) has one article governing coordinated personal nouns, strongly favoring a single referent, Jesus Christ.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** The single-referent construction is strong, yet its semantic and theological import is still discussed. A Unitarian can grant that the writer applies “God” and “Savior” together to Jesus while denying that this grammatical designation alone establishes coequality, eternal generation, or the full Nicene doctrine.

**Bounded conclusion:** One-referent grammar is strongly favored. It is substantial evidence for a direct designation of Jesus, while fuller doctrine remains an inference drawn with other texts and theological premises.

### 1 John 5:20

**Categories:** grammar; subject/referent; translation; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/witnesses/john-s-witness.md).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** The nearest plausible antecedent of `οὗτός` (“this one”) is Jesus Christ, so “this one is the true God and eternal life” identifies Jesus as true God.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** The Father is also a grammatically and contextually plausible antecedent: the verse says believers know “the true one” and are “in the true one, in his Son Jesus Christ.” John elsewhere calls the Father the true God, and “eternal life” can describe life given through the Son rather than identify the Son as the Father.

**Bounded conclusion:** `οὗτός` has a disputed antecedent. The Jesus reading is possible and often preferred, but grammar and context do not remove the Father reading beyond dispute.

## Hebrew Bible passages and New Testament application

### Isaiah 9:6

**Categories:** lexical choice; translation; subject/referent; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/prophecies/isaiah-s-prophecies.md#context-of-isaiah-9).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** The child’s royal titles, especially `El Gibbor` (“Mighty God”) and “Everlasting Father,” are read as divine titles fulfilled by the Messiah, making the promised child more than an ordinary Davidic king.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** Hebrew royal naming and throne language can express God’s authority, patronage, or the character of a king without making the king identical with God. Translation and division of the titles are debated, and Isaiah’s immediate royal setting must be weighed before later messianic application.

**Bounded conclusion:** Isaiah 9:6 contains exalted Hebrew royal titles and can support a high messianic reading. The New Testament does not directly quote this verse to prove Jesus’ deity, so a direct Nicene conclusion requires interpretive steps beyond the verse itself.

### Zechariah 12:10

**Categories:** manuscript variant; grammar; translation; quotation application; subject/referent; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/prophecies/zechariah-s-prophecy.md#will-god-be-pierced-in-his-flesh).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** In the Hebrew text, YHWH says “they will look to me, whom they pierced,” followed by mourning language. John 19:37 applies the piercing text to Jesus; together these are read as identifying the pierced Jesus with YHWH.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** The Hebrew’s first-person “me” stands beside third-person mourning language (“for him”), producing a difficult textual and syntactical sequence. Ancient versions and later translations differ in handling it, and John’s quotation application can present Jesus as the fulfillment of Scripture without erasing the distinction between God who speaks and the Messiah who is pierced.

**Bounded conclusion:** The Hebrew wording and John’s application create a serious intertextual argument for the Trinitarian reading, but variants, translation choices, and the relationship of application to identity remain disputed.

## Christological language

### Philippians 2:6–7

**Categories:** grammar; lexical choice; translation; subject/referent; theological inference.  See [detailed study](../essence/as-god/lord.md#philippians-29-11).

**Strongest Trinitarian reading:** Existing “in the form of God,” Christ does not treat equality with God as something to exploit, but takes “the form of a slave” and becomes human. This is read as preexistent divine Son freely humbling himself in incarnation.

**Steelmanned Unitarian response:** `μορφή` may denote visible status or condition rather than an ontological nature, and `ἁρπαγμός` may mean a prize to grasp rather than a privilege already possessed. On an Adamic or vocation reading, Christ, as the obedient human Messiah, refuses grasping at God-like status and embraces servanthood; “emptying” need not describe a prehuman divine person becoming human.

**Bounded conclusion:** The passage is central evidence for preexistence and incarnation in Trinitarian interpretation, but the meanings of `μορφή`, `ἁρπαγμός`, and “emptying” are disputed. Its theological result cannot be separated from those lexical and contextual judgments.

## Synthesis matrix

This page owns classification of direct-deity textual and grammatical disputes; the linked studies own extended argument. Textual-variant disputes are central in 1 Timothy 3:16 and Acts 20:28, and materially relevant to Zechariah 12:10. Punctuation is central in Romans 9:5. Strong one-referent grammar is central in Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1. Antecedent or referent questions are central in Romans 9:5, 1 John 5:20, Isaiah 9:6, and Zechariah 12:10. Philippians 2:6–7 chiefly turns on lexical and grammatical interpretation. Every passage also requires a distinct theological-inference judgment.

## How Disputed Texts Should Be Weighed

Trinitarians reasonably argue that several debated texts, even when grammar is contested, contribute to a cumulative case for Christ's deity. A textual variant or alternate punctuation does not erase every high claim about Jesus.

This project therefore does not make its case depend on removing difficult verses. Each passage should be translated from its best-attested text, read in its paragraph, and compared with explicit statements that distinguish God from Jesus. Jesus calls the Father “the only true God” (**John 17:3**). Paul distinguishes “one God, the Father” from “one Lord, Jesus Christ” (**First Corinthians 8:6**). Peter proclaims that God raised Jesus (**Acts 2:32**). A disputed direct-deity construction cannot overturn that repeated grammatical and narrative pattern without clear contextual warrant.

## Control Texts and Cumulative Limits

The strongest Trinitarian case is cumulative. Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1 are naturally read as calling Jesus God; Thomas addresses the risen Jesus as **“My Lord and my God”** (John 20:28); and Philippians 2:6-11 uses unusually high language. A textual variant elsewhere cannot make these passages disappear, and strong grammar should not be evaded.

The Unitarian response distinguishes questions rather than inventing a corruption motive. **1 Timothy 3:16** concerns a manuscript reading. **Romans 9:5** concerns punctuation and referent. **Acts 20:28** includes both a textual reading and a syntactical question. **Titus 2:13** and **2 Peter 1:1** are chiefly grammatical and strongly favour one referent. John 1:1 and John 1:18 must also be treated as interpretation and translation questions: John 1:1 has a qualitative anarthrous *theos* predicate, while John 1:18 has important manuscript variation between “only God” and “only Son.” Neither point permits an automatic doctrinal conclusion.

The bounded conclusion is that disputed readings require proportionate confidence, while granted designations still require contextual interpretation. The Father is “the only true God,” Jesus is the one sent by Him (John 17:3), and Paul distinguishes “one God, the Father” from “one Lord, Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:6). Those control texts do not settle every high passage, but they prevent either side from treating one construction as a complete creed. See [God, Father, Son, and Spirit](god.draft.md#father-son-and-spirit).

## Conclusion

Each disputed passage needs its own textual, grammatical, and contextual judgment. Strong texts deserve full weight, and their doctrinal implications must be read beside [apostolic preaching](apostolic-preaching.draft.md#apostolic-pattern-and-its-strongest-challenge).
