# The Spirit of Jesus Christ

New Testament expressions such as “Spirit of Christ” require close contextual reading. This study distinguishes Jesus’ human spirit, God’s Spirit, and the principal Trinitarian and Unitarian explanations.

# Jesus' Spirit

Jesus had a genuinely human spirit. He was troubled in spirit (John 11:33; 13:21), perceived in his spirit (Mark 2:8), and committed his spirit to the Father at death (Luke 23:46). These references should not be equated with the Holy Spirit.

Romans 8:9 uses “Spirit of God” and “Spirit of Christ” in the same argument about belonging to Christ; Romans 8:10-11 then distinguishes Christ being in believers from God raising the dead through His Spirit. Classical Trinitarians regard this closely coordinated language as fitting one divine Spirit related personally to Father and Son. Unitarians may read the alternate possessives as mission and possession language for God’s one Spirit, which makes believers belong to Christ and communicates his risen life; the sequence itself does not state that Christ and Spirit are identical.

Galatians 4:6 says **“God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts.”** The sender, Spirit, and Son are distinguished, while “of his Son” naturally marks the Spirit’s relation to the Son’s filial cry, “Abba! Father!” Philippians 1:19 speaks of “the supply of the Spirit of Jesus Christ.” It can denote the Spirit supplied through the exalted Christ or the Spirit identified with his ministry; possession language alone does not settle personhood.

Classical Trinitarians read the three passages with John 15:26 and other personal Spirit language as evidence for a distinct divine person. The Unitarian case is positive but bounded: God sends His Spirit, forms filial prayer, and supplies Christ-centred life to believers. *Those agency and possession claims establish coordinated saving work; whether they require shared divine nature is a further inference.* See [God, Father, Son, and Spirit](god.draft.md#father-son-and-spirit).

## Conclusion

“Spirit of Christ” language distinguishes Jesus’ human spirit from God’s Spirit and leaves a real theological debate about personhood. The wider comparison is in [God, Father, Son, and Spirit](god.draft.md#father-son-and-spirit).
