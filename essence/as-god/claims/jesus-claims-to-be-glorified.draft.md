# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The scriptures present a fascinating dialogue regarding the concept of glory. In
the Old Testament, the Creator makes a definitive statement about his unique
status and the glory that belongs to him alone. Yet, in the New Testament, Jesus
speaks frequently about his own glorification and his shared glory with the
Father. This has led many to explore how these two perspectives reconcile within
the biblical narrative.

## Isaiah Challenge

> I am [the LORD](../../../nature.md#bible-translations), that is my name;
> and **my [glory](../../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**,
> nor my [praise](../../../nature.md) to carved idols. — Isaiah 42:8 (ESV)

Yet Jesus said:

> And Jesus answered them, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

and

> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and **I am [glorified](../../../nature.md#gods-glory)**
> in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

[Out of context](../../../nature.md#concerns), it seems like Jesus contradicts
Isaiah. To solve this contradiction, some would argue this proof that Jesus is
[the LORD](../../../nature.md#bible-translations). This argument is supported by
the [confusion caused by removing God's name](../../../nature.md#bible-translations).
The divine name is replaced with "the LORD" which is also a title used for Jesus.

## Reciprocal Glorification

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the issue was that God did not want to share his
glory with foreign idols. These are referred to as carved idols. A theological
distinction exists in the original languages that clarifies this point. The
Greek Septuagint uses the word *heterō* in this passage. This term means "another
of a different kind" such as a false idol. This differs from the word *allos*
which would mean "another of the same kind."

By using *heterō*, the text indicates that God refuses to share his glory with
ontologically different entities like idols. However, as the Father of Jesus,
the Son of God, he has no problem with anyone glorifying his Son. This is because
glorifying the Son automatically glorifies the Father as well. The Son shares
the same nature as the Father.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

Parents do not compete with their children. Instead, they support them. For
example, a human father is honored when someone honors his son. It shows that
the father raised his son well. This reflects the relationship between the
Father and the Son.

> That all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. — John 5:23 (ESV)

Therefore, both Isaiah and Jesus are correct. In the context of John 17,
Jesus prayed:

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> "Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you**,
> since [you have given him authority over all flesh](../lord.draft.md#jesus-got-all-authority),
> [to give eternal life to all whom you have given him](../../../son-as-god/similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father)."
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

If someone gives glory to Jesus, it also glorifies God the Father. This
reciprocal glorification is a key theme in the New Testament. Jesus himself
explained that seeking one's own glory is not the goal.

> Jesus answered, "**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me**, of whom you say, 'He is our God.'" — John 8:54 (ESV)

## Evidence of Shared Glory

The relationship between the Father and the Son involves a unique sharing of
glory that existed before time began. Jesus explicitly asked to return to this
state after his mission on earth.

> And now, Father, glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had
> with you before the world existed. — John 17:5 (ESV)

The New Testament writers further describe Jesus as the very reflection of God's
glory. He is not a separate competitor but the means by which God's glory is
made manifest.

> He is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature,
> and he upholds the universe by the word of his power. — Hebrews 1:3 (ESV)

Therefore, [Jesus has to be distinct from God the Father](../../../son-of-man/distinct.md)
to be glorified by him.

## Conclusion

The biblical narrative reconciles the exclusive glory of God with the
glorification of Jesus through several key principles. The
[Isaiah Challenge](#isaiah-challenge) focuses on God's refusal to share glory
with ontologically different idols. This is resolved through
[Reciprocal Glorification](#reciprocal-glorification), where honoring the Son
serves to glorify the Father. Finally, the
[Evidence of Shared Glory](#evidence-of-shared-glory) confirms that Jesus
shares the Father's nature and pre-existent glory, ensuring that his
glorification does not contradict the monotheistic declaration of the
Old Testament.
