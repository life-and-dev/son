# Sons of Korah's Witnesses

When the author of Hebrews wanted to prove the superiority of Jesus, he quoted a beautiful song from the Old Testament. This song, Psalm 45, contains a striking address: "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever." For many, this is a clear declaration of the Messiah's divinity. However, a closer look at the original context reveals a royal wedding song composed by the sons of Korah for an earthly king. Does this passage identify Jesus as God, or is it a case of poetic language applied to a human ruler?

> But of the Son he says,
>
> “Your throne, O ***God***, is forever and ever,
> the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom.
> You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness;
> therefore ***God***, your God, has anointed you
> with the oil of gladness beyond your companions.”
>
> — Hebrews 1:8,9 (ESV)

## A Song for a Royal Wedding

Psalm 45 was originally written by the sons of Korah. While Hebrews 1:8,9 quotes this passage to describe Jesus, the original context was not a prophecy about the Messiah, but a love song celebrating a royal wedding.

## Linguistic Distinctions

Human kings in the ancient world were sometimes referred to using the title "god" ([Elohim](../../../son-as-god.md#the-flexible-meaning-of-god)). The original Hebrew manuscripts were all written in capital letters. The modern English [bible translators](../../../nature.md#bible-translations) added the capital letter "God" based on their understanding of scripture. The psalmists also described this "god" as a "God". It would be a paradox if God Almighty has a bigger God who is even more mighty.

## Comparing the King and Jesus

When we examine the details of Psalm 45, several descriptions appear to conflict with the life and ministry of Jesus as recorded in the Gospels.

| The King in Psalm 45 | Jesus Christ |
| ---------------------- | -------------- |
| This Psalm is a love song written for a daughter who marries the king. | Jesus did not marry an earthly wife during his ministry. |
| The king is described as the most handsome of the sons of men (Psalm 45:2). | Jesus had no physical beauty or majesty to attract people (Isaiah 53:2). |
| The king is a warrior who kills enemies with sharp arrows (Psalm 45:5). | Jesus did not kill his enemies but prayed for them. |
| The king's robes are fragrant with myrrh, aloes, and cassia (Psalm 45:8). | Jesus was anointed with myrrh and aloes only at his burial (John 19:39). |
| The king lived in ivory palaces with stringed instruments (Psalm 45:8). | Jesus had no permanent home or personal entertainers. |
| The king had a queen and daughters of kings as ladies of honor (Psalm 45:9). | Jesus had no queen and maintained no royal court of ladies. |

## The Messianic Perspective

Trinitarian scholars and the author of Hebrews view Psalm 45 as a Messianic prophecy. They argue that the earthly king served as a "type" or shadow of the true King, Jesus Christ. In this view, the "marriage" symbolizes the union between Christ and his Church (Ephesians 5:25,32).

While the king in the psalm is addressed as "god," the text also mentions "God, your God." This construction can be interpreted as a distinction between the Son (the anointed King) and the Father. For those who hold this view, the application of Psalm 45 to Jesus in the New Testament confirms his divine status. It suggests that the language of the psalm finds its ultimate and literal fulfillment only in the eternal reign of the Messiah.

Critics of this Messianic application point to the specific human details of the psalm. These details, such as the ivory palaces and multiple wives, serve as evidence that it was intended solely for a historical ruler. They suggest that the author of Hebrews used the passage midrashically to illustrate the exalted status of the Son without necessarily claiming that every detail of the psalm applied to Jesus.

## Conclusion

Psalm 45 presents a fascinating case of how the New Testament authors reinterpreted Old Testament poetry. While the sons of Korah originally composed this [song for a royal wedding](#a-song-for-a-royal-wedding), the author of Hebrews saw in it a [divine address to the Son](#the-messianic-perspective). By examining the [linguistic distinctions](#linguistic-distinctions) and [comparing the king to Jesus](#comparing-the-king-and-jesus), we can appreciate both the historical context of the psalm and its enduring theological significance.
