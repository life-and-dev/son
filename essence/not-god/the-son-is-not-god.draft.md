# The Son Is not God

The relationship between Jesus Christ and God the Father has been a subject of intense theological discussion for centuries. While traditional views often emphasize their ontological equality, a significant body of scripture and historical thought highlights the distinctions and subordination of the Son to the Father. This article explores the biblical evidence that leads many to conclude that the Son is a distinct being from the one true God whom he served.

Multiple scriptures show that [Jesus is a distinct person from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) and that [Jesus had human limitations](../../son-of-man/limitations.md).

John opens his revelation with a clear distinction between the eternal God and Jesus Christ:

> Grace to you and peace from him who is and who was and who is to come, and from the seven spirits who are before his throne, and from Jesus Christ the faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings on earth. To him who loves us and has freed us from our sins by his blood and made us a kingdom, priests to his God and Father, to him be glory and dominion forever and ever. Amen. — Revelation 1:4-6 (ESV)

Jesus also identifies his own standing before God in the same vision:

> When I saw him, I fell at his feet as though dead. But he laid his right hand on me, saying, “Fear not, I am the first and the last, and the living one. I died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades. Write therefore the things that you have seen, those that are and those that are to take place after this.” — Revelation 1:17-19 (ESV)

From this account, Jesus appears as the witness who reveals future events. This same speaker continues his revelation through the initial chapters. In chapter 3, the text records:

> The one who conquers, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God. Never shall he go out of it, and I will write on him the name of my God, and the name of the city of my God, the new Jerusalem, which comes down from my God out of heaven, and my own new name. — Revelation 3:12 (ESV)

If the identity of the speaker had shifted to John, it would not follow that John could make a believer a pillar in the temple or write the name of God upon them. The context indicates that Jesus remains the speaker, referring multiple times to the Father as "my God."

## Response to Critiques

Some readers might reference certain passages to argue that Jesus was not a man in the conventional sense. For example, Paul's opening to the Galatians is sometimes cited:

> Paul, an apostle—not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father, who raised him from the dead... — Galatians 1:1 (ESV)

The majority of scholars understand that Paul was not implying that Jesus was a non-human being. He was simply stating that his authority to write his epistles came from no man-made institution. His commission came directly from Jesus Christ and God the Father.

Other translations make this distinction clearer:

> Paul, an apostle (not commissioned and sent from men nor through the agency of man, but through Jesus Christ — the Messiah — and God the Father, who raised Him from the dead)... — Galatians 1:1 (AMP)

While these arguments attempt to bridge the gap between the Son and the Father, the weight of scriptural evidence continues to highlight their distinct identities.

### Addressing Oneness and Worship

Critiques often point to statements like John 10:30, where Jesus says, “I and the Father are one.” The Greek word for “one” used here is the neuter *hen*, rather than the masculine *heis*. This indicates a unity of purpose, will, or action. This is similar to how Jesus prays for his followers to be "one" in John 17:11. It is not a claim of being the same person or essence as God.

Furthermore, when Jesus receives what is translated as “worship,” the New Testament frequently uses the Greek word *proskuneo*. This term refers to the act of bowing down or showing reverent homage. This was a gesture that was historically given to kings, prophets, and superiors as well as to God. In contrast, the word *latreuo*, which refers to the exclusive cultic service and religious devotion reserved for the Creator, is never applied to Jesus in the New Testament. This distinction suggests that the honor given to Jesus is that of the Father's exalted representative rather than as the source of divinity itself.

## Conclusion

The biblical evidence suggests that the Son, while uniquely exalted and honored, remains distinct from and subordinate to the one true God. Through his [denial of inherent goodness or power](#jesus-denies-being-god), his [submission to the Father as his God](#jesus-has-a-god), and his [participation in human rites](#jesus-was-baptized), the scriptures consistently portray Jesus as the Father's faithful servant. The [witness of the apostles](#the-apostles-believed-jesus-had-a-god) further confirms this distinction, emphasizing a relationship that, while unified in purpose, maintains a clear ontological hierarchy.
