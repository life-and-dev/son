# The Son Is Not God

What is the true nature of the relationship between Jesus and God the Father?
While many traditions view them as one and the same being, a close examination
of the biblical text reveals a complex and often surprising distinction. This
topic has been at the heart of theological discussions since the early church,
leading to various perspectives such as
[subordinationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subordinationism). From
Jesus' own words to the observations of his closest followers, the scriptures
provide a wealth of evidence that challenges common assumptions. Exploring these
passages invites a deeper understanding of the identity of the Son and his
unique position in relation to the One he called Father.

Multiple scriptures show that [Jesus is a distinct person from God the
Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) and that [Jesus had human
limitations](../../son-of-man/limitations.md).

## Jesus Disclaims Deity

Jesus often spoke in ways that distinguished himself from the only true God.
When a ruler addressed him as "Good Teacher," Jesus redirected the focus to
God's unique goodness.

> And he said to him, "Why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one
> who is good. If you would enter life, keep the commandments." — Matthew 19:17
> (ESV)

Jesus also emphasized his complete dependence on the Father for his actions and
authority. He stated that he could do nothing of his own accord and sought only
the glory that comes from God.

> So Jesus said to them, "Truly, truly, I say to you, the Son can do nothing of
> his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing. For whatever the
> Father does, that the Son does likewise." — John 5:19 (ESV)
>
> "How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek
> the glory that comes from the only God? Do not think that I will accuse you to
> the Father. There is one who accuses you: Moses, on whom you have set your
> hope." — John 5:44-45 (ESV)

Furthermore, Jesus clarified that his mission was to honor his Father rather
than to seek his own glory. He pointed to another who would be the judge.

> Jesus answered, "I do not have a demon, but I honor my Father, and you
> dishonor me. Yet I do not seek my own glory; there is One who seeks it, and he
> is the judge." — John 8:49-50 (ESV)
>
> Jesus answered, "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who
> glorifies me, of whom you say, 'He is our God.'" — John 8:54 (ESV)

In his high priestly prayer, Jesus explicitly defined eternal life in terms of
knowing the Father as the only true God, distinct from himself as the one sent
by God.

> "And this is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God, and Jesus
> Christ whom you have sent." — John 17:3 (ESV)

Jesus also stated that his Father is greater than he is, further emphasizing
their distinct positions.

> "You heard me say to you, 'I am going away, and I will come to you.' If you
> loved me, you would have rejoiced, because I am going to the Father, for the
> Father is greater than I." — John 14:28 (ESV)

## The Relationship Between Jesus and His God

After his resurrection, Jesus continued to refer to the Father as his God. This
language indicates a persistent relationship of subordination and devotion.

> Jesus said to her, "Do not cling to me, for I have not yet ascended to the
> Father; but go to my brothers and say to them, 'I am ascending to my Father
> and your Father, to my God and your God.'" — John 20:17 (ESV)

Throughout his ministry, Jesus was characterized by a life of prayer. He
frequently sought the Father in solitude and expressed his submission to the
Father's will.

> And going a little farther he fell on his face and prayed, saying, "My Father,
> if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but
> as you will." — Matthew 26:39 (ESV)
>
> Again, for the second time, he went away and prayed, "My Father, if this
> cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done." — Matthew 26:42 (ESV)
>
> "Do you think that I cannot appeal to my Father, and he will at once send me
> more than twelve legions of angels?" — Matthew 26:53 (ESV)
>
> In these days he went out to the mountain to pray, and all night he continued
> in prayer to God. — Luke 6:12 (ESV)
>
> And Jesus lifted up his eyes and said, "Father, I thank you that you have
> heard me." — John 11:41 (ESV)
>
> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> "Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you." —
> John 17:1 (ESV)

The apostles also recognized this relationship. They frequently blessed the God
and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, acknowledging God as the source of all
blessings and as Jesus' own God.

> Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in
> Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places. — Ephesians 1:3
> (ESV)
>
> "...that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give you
> the Spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge of him." — Ephesians
> 1:17 (ESV)
>
> May the God of endurance and encouragement grant you to live in such harmony
> with one another, in accord with Christ Jesus, that together you may with one
> voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. — Romans 15:5-6
> (ESV)
>
> Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies
> and God of all comfort, who comforts us in all our affliction. — 2 Corinthians
> 1:3-4 (ESV)
>
> Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! According to his great
> mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the
> resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead. — 1 Peter 1:3 (ESV)

In the book of Revelation, the glorified Jesus still speaks of "my God."

> "The one who conquers, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God. Never
> shall he go out of it, and I will write on him the name of my God, and the
> name of the city of my God, the new Jerusalem, which comes down from my God
> out of heaven, and my own new name." — Revelation 3:12 (ESV)

The apostle Paul also describes a future where Jesus will deliver the kingdom to
God the Father and be subjected to him, so that God may be all in all.

> Then comes the end, when he delivers the kingdom to God the Father after
> destroying every rule and every authority and power... When all things are
> subjected to him, then the Son himself will also be subjected to him who put
> all things in subjection under him, that God may be all in all. — 1
> Corinthians 15:24,28 (ESV)

## The Baptism of Repentance

A significant argument for the distinction between Jesus and God involves the
baptism of Jesus. Biblical texts state that God is not a man and does not need
to repent.

> God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his
> mind. Has he said, and will he not do it? Or has he spoken, and will he not
> fulfill it? — Numbers 23:19 (ESV)

Yet, Jesus underwent a baptism specifically associated with repentance.

> "John baptized with the baptism of repentance, telling the people to believe
> in the one who was to come after him, that is, Jesus." — Acts 19:4 (ESV)
>
> In those days Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee and was baptized by John in
> the Jordan. — Mark 1:9 (ESV)

## Apostolic Belief in Jesus' Subordination

The early church leaders consistently presented Jesus as subordinate to God.
They described him as God's servant and as a witness distinct from God.

> "The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, the God of our
> fathers, glorified his servant Jesus, whom you delivered over and denied in
> the presence of Pilate, when he had decided to release him." — Acts 3:13 (ESV)

John also distinguished between God and Jesus in his greetings and descriptions
of the heavenly kingdom.

> Grace to you and peace from him who is and who was and who is to come, and
> from the seven spirits who are before his throne, and from Jesus Christ the
> faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings on earth.
> To him who loves us and has freed us from our sins by his blood and made us a
> kingdom, priests to his God and Father, to him be glory and dominion forever
> and ever. Amen. — Revelation 1:4-6 (ESV)

## Critique and Defense

Critics often point to specific verses to argue for Jesus' deity. For example,
some suggest that Paul's words in Galatians 1:1 imply Jesus is not a man.

> Paul, an apostle—not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and
> God the Father, who raised him from the dead. — Galatians 1:1 (ESV)

However, scholars generally agree that Paul is not denying Jesus' humanity.
Instead, he is defending the divine source of his apostolic authority. He
contrasts man-made institutions with the direct commission he received from the
risen Christ and God the Father.

Another common argument centers on Jesus' statement, "Why do you call me good?"
in Matthew 19:17. Trinitarian interpretations suggest Jesus was not denying his
goodness. He may have been challenging the young man to recognize the divine
nature of the one he was addressing as "good."

Another major proof text for critics is John 10:30, where Jesus says, "I and the
Father are one." Trinitarians often interpret this as oneness in essence or
deity. However, the context and similar phrasing in other parts of the New
Testament suggest a oneness of purpose or will. For instance, in John 17:21-22,
Jesus prays that his disciples may be "one" just as he and the Father are one.
Since the disciples do not become one being, it is argued that Jesus and the
Father are likewise one in spirit and mission rather than ontological identity.

Regarding John 17:3, some argue that "the only true God" is meant to contrast
the Father with false idols. They suggest this does not necessarily exclude the
Son from being the same God. However, many scholars point out that the verse
explicitly distinguishes between the "only true God" and "Jesus Christ whom you
have sent," a point explored in detail by various
[biblical commentaries](https://www.biblicalunitarian.com/verses/john-17-3).

In the book of Revelation, Jesus refers to himself with a title that some
associate with deity.

> "When I saw him, I fell at his feet as though dead. But he laid his right hand
> on me, saying, 'Fear not, I am the first and the last, and the living one. I
> died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and
> Hades.'" — Revelation 1:17-18 (ESV)

While the title "the first and the last" is also used for the LORD in the Old
Testament, context is key. Here, it is used by the one who died and was brought
back to life. This highlights his unique role as the resurrected Messiah and the
one who received authority from God.

## Conclusion

The biblical evidence presents a strong case for the distinction between Jesus
and God the Father. [Jesus' own words](#jesus-disclaims-deity) repeatedly
acknowledge the Father as the only true God and his own God. His
[life of prayer](#the-relationship-between-jesus-and-his-god) and his
[baptism](#the-baptism-of-repentance) further emphasize his human nature and
subordination. The
[apostles continued this theme](#apostolic-belief-in-jesus-subordination) by
identifying God as the God of our Lord Jesus Christ. While some verses are used
to argue for equality, they often focus on Jesus' unique authority or his
relationship with the Father rather than ontological identity. These
biblical views are central to the history of
[Unitarianism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Unitarian).
