## Sanctified and Sent

Jesus answers a charge in John 10 by describing himself as “him whom the Father **consecrated** and **sent** into the world” (John 10:36). To consecrate is to set apart for God’s purpose. The Father consecrates and sends; Jesus is the one set apart and commissioned. This supplies positive evidence for a Messiah whose mission comes from God.

### The Trinitarian Reading

Trinitarians may understand “consecrated” as the eternal Son’s setting apart for his incarnate mission, not as a denial of deity. They also read “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30) as more than agreement of purpose, given Jesus’ works and the Father’s presence in him. On this account, being sent, serving, and even being sanctified describe the Son’s saving mission; such relational language does not automatically disprove incarnation or two natures.

The context must still govern the claim. Jesus’ opponents accuse him of making himself God (John 10:33), but Jesus answers by citing Psalm 82:6 and then formulates his claim as “I am God’s Son” (John 10:34-36). Psalm 82’s precise referent is debated. Its argumentative function is clearer: a divinely authorised title need not mean identity with the one God.

### The Unitarian Reply

Jesus’ own answer identifies him as God’s Son, not with the sentence “I am God” (John 10:36). He points to works the Father gave him to do (John 10:32,36-38). This supports the Unitarian reading of commissioned agency: Jesus uniquely reveals and acts for God, while God remains the one who consecrates and sends him.

The statement that the Father is in Jesus and Jesus in the Father need not mean mere shared intention. John 14:10 grounds Jesus’ words and works in the Father dwelling in him. A Trinitarian sees mutual indwelling consistent with shared divine life; a Unitarian sees God’s effective presence in His authorised Son. The same Gospel extends a form of mutual indwelling to disciples (John 17:21-23), so the phrase requires contextual interpretation rather than a single automatic conclusion.

*The warranted conclusion is that John 10 presents Jesus as uniquely consecrated, sent, and empowered by the Father. It supplies a substantial Unitarian distinction argument while not, by itself, excluding Trinitarian incarnation.* See [Jesus serving God](serve-god.md#jesus-serves-god) and [Jesus’ distinction from God](distinct.md#jesus-interacted-with-god).
