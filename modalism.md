# Modalism (1 God = 3 Modes)

At Jesus' baptism, he stands in the water while the Spirit descends and a voice speaks from heaven. How should readers understand the Father, the Son, and the Spirit in scenes like this?

This article expands the [development overview](trinity/development.md) by explaining modalism, examining its historical reception, and testing its central claim against Scripture.

## Defining Modalism

Modalism holds that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are not three distinct personal subjects but different ways in which one divine personal subject is known or acts. It is sometimes associated with the names Sabellius and Praxeas. The word describes a *one-person* account of the three terms, not simply belief in one God.

## Relevancy Today

[The United Pentecostal Church International (UPCI)](https://upci.org/our-beliefs/) is a present-day example of an **Oneness Pentecostal** body. Its statement of belief describes one God revealed as Father, in his Son Jesus Christ, and as the Holy Spirit, and calls Jesus God manifested in flesh.

Oneness believers commonly identify the Father with the one God and regard the Holy Spirit as that God's Spirit. They identify Jesus as the one God's manifestation in flesh. On this account, “Son” refers especially to the real human life and birth of Jesus, while “Father” speaks of the divine identity. They therefore explain Jesus' prayers as the genuine humanity of Jesus addressing God, not necessarily as one actor pretending to pray. The [UPCI belief statement](https://upci.org/our-beliefs/) explicitly calls Jesus *both God and man*.

## Modalism Challenges

* Matthew 3:16-17 describe a voice from heaven **while** the Son is baptised.
* There are many examples of **interaction** between Jesus and the Father. For example the Father send Jesus and Jesus prays the Father.
* The Father and Jesus are considered **two** witnesses.

These facts make no sense if they are the same personal subject.

## Historical Rejection

### Early Christian Writers

Around the early third century, [Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*, chapters 1-2 and 9](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0317.htm), opposed a teaching he associated with Praxeas that collapsed the Father and Son into one personal identity. He argued instead that the Father and Son must be distinguished. [Hippolytus, *Against the Heresy of One Noetus*, chapters 1-3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0521.htm), similarly reported that Noetus identified the Father with the Son and opposed that identification.

### Creeds and Later Reception

The [Nicene Creed in Catholic use](https://www.usccb.org/prayers/nicene-creed) names the one God as Father, distinguishes Jesus Christ as his Son, and speaks of the Holy Spirit. Its language [distinguishes Father and Son](son-of-man/distinct.md). The later [Athanasian Creed](https://bookofconcord.org/athanasian-creed/) is more direct: it says *“neither confounding the persons nor dividing the substance”* and distinguishes the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

The [Catholic Catechism, paragraphs 253-255](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P17.HTM), teaches that the divine persons are really distinct.  The [Westminster Confession of Faith, chapter 2.3](https://opc.org/wcf.html#Chapter_02), received by confessional Presbyterian Protestants, likewise distinguishes three persons in the one Godhead.

## Biblical Evidence

Although Deuteronomy 6:4 and Isaiah 44:6 affirm the **[one God](shema.md)**, neither says that the Father and his appointed Son are one personal subject.

The older [prophetic pattern](https://prophecies.ofgod.info/messianic) [distinguishes God and His servant](son-of-man/distinct.md). In Isaiah 42:1, God says, *“I have put my **Spirit** upon **him**”* (ESV). Matthew 12:17-21 applies that servant passage to Jesus. In the same pattern, Isaiah 61:1 describes the speaker as anointed by the Spirit and sent by the Lord GOD; Jesus applies this passage to his mission in Luke 4:17-21. The New Testament application confirms the messianic reading rather than making the prophetic speaker identical with the sender.

Jesus then appeals to a legal distinction: *“the testimony of **two people** is true”*, naming himself and *“the **Father who sent me**”* (John 8:17-18, ESV). Jesus addresses the Father as *“the only true **God**”* and himself as *“Jesus Christ whom you have **sent**”* (John 17:3, ESV). His appeal to **two** witnesses bears more weight against a one-subject reading than the baptism scene alone.

Jesus' prayer *“not as **I will**, but as **you will**”* (Matthew 26:39, ESV) likewise presents a **real relationship**, not merely three visual signs. The same Father-Son relations are surveyed in [Jesus Interacted With God](son-of-man/distinct.md).

The apostolic distinction is similarly direct: *“there is one **God**, and there is one **mediator** between God and men, **the man Christ Jesus**”* (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). A mediator is distinguished from the God to whom he mediates. Neither argument requires treating God's Spirit as another god.

## Conclusion

The [historical witnesses](#historical-rejection) rejected the confusion of Father and Son, but Scripture supplies the decisive test. The [biblical relationships and comparison](#biblical-evidence) show the Father and his human Son as genuinely distinct while God's Spirit acts among them. **Modalism's one-person claim is unbiblical and disproved by these distinctions.**
