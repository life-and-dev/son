# Love of God

*If the Father loved His Son, why send him to die instead of dying Himself?* This is a serious moral question, especially for anyone who has seen a parent demand suffering from a child. It deserves more than an assurance that the outcome was good.

The question also needs an accurate account of what Christians claim. The Father is [distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) from the Son. Christians do not generally say that *the Father died*. Most Christians affirm that **the Son suffered and died**.

## The Father's Compassion and Love

The LORD describes Himself as **merciful, gracious and patient, abounding in steadfast love**, forgiving sin without simply clearing the guilty (Exodus 34:6–7). These belong together. Mercy does not mean that wrongdoing is harmless. Justice does not mean that God has stopped caring for those caught in it.

Psalm 103:8, 13 compares the LORD’s compassion with a **father’s compassion for his children**. Genesis 6:6 says that human evil grieved Him. These passages come before any account of Jesus’ death. They do not picture a distant God who needs to be persuaded to care, or one who enjoys watching suffering.

John 3:16–17 says God loved the world and sent His Son so that people might have life rather than perish. Romans 5:8 states, “but God shows his **love** for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.” Romans 5:9–11 also speaks of judgement, reconciliation and salvation. Love here does not erase the problem of sin or pretend that judgement is unreal.

Likewise, 1 John 4:9–10 places the initiative with God. The Father sent His Son because **He loved**, not because the Son *persuaded an unwilling Father to become merciful*.

## The Son Gives Himself Willingly

An unwilling child handed over to violence would be a disturbing comparison. Jesus’ own words resist it. Speaking of his life, he says, “No one takes it from me, but I **lay it down of my own accord**” (John 10:18). His [temptations](../../son-of-man/temptations.md) show that his obedience was tested (Matthew 4:1–11). He also says that he received this charge from his Father. His willing action does not cancel [the Father’s initiative](#the-fathers-compassion-and-love). The Father’s command does not erase the Son’s agency.

Willingness, however, does not turn suffering into something easy or make consent a general excuse for another person to demand a sacrifice. Jesus’ particular mission gives no parent, leader or institution permission to force someone else into harm. The claim is about what this Son gave of himself, not a rule that vulnerable people must accept abuse.

## His Human Suffering Was Real

Jesus did not face death without anguish. In Gethsemane he prayed, “Yet **not what I will, but what you will**” (Mark 14:36).

Isaiah does not soften the Father’s involvement when it speaks of “the **will of the LORD to crush him**” (Isaiah 53:10). Nor does the Father’s purpose excuse the executioners. Peter says Jesus was delivered up according to “the **definite plan and foreknowledge of God**”, while telling his hearers, “you **crucified and killed**” him. “**God raised him up**” (Acts 2:23–24).

Isaiah says the servant will “**make many to be accounted righteous**” (Isaiah 53:11). This states the saving end of his suffering, though Scripture does not answer every question about why the Father chose this means.

## The New Covenant and Its Blood

God warned Adam, “you shall **surely die**” (Genesis 2:17). Paul traces death to human sin and contrasts Adam’s disobedience with “the **one man’s obedience**” of Christ (Romans 5:12, 19). The Father chose to send his obedient human Son rather than die himself. Paul also connects human death with resurrection through a man (1 Corinthians 15:21). Scripture presents this as the Father’s saving course, not proof that an external rule required the only possible remedy. “The **wages of sin is death**, but the **free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord**” (Romans 6:23).

Israel’s broken Sinai covenant is distinct from Adam’s death consequence. The Father promises, “I will make a **new covenant**” (Jeremiah 31:31–32): “I will put my **law within them**” and “I will **forgive their iniquity**” (Jeremiah 31:33–34). Paul says the later Sinai law “**does not annul a covenant previously ratified by God**” with Abraham (Galatians 3:15–18). The promised new covenant does not depend on treating broken Sinai as unchangeable.

At Sinai Moses declared, “Behold the **blood of the covenant**” (Exodus 24:8). Blood was necessary “to make **atonement**” (Leviticus 17:11). Jesus calls his death “my **blood of the covenant**”, poured out “for the **forgiveness of sins**” (Matthew 26:28), and calls the cup “the **new covenant in my blood**” (1 Corinthians 11:25). His death thus inaugurates the promised forgiveness and renewal. This does not establish a blood requirement for every instance of [forgiveness](https://word.ofgod.info/terms/forgiveness). Leviticus 5:11–13 allows fine flour and says the person “**shall be forgiven**”.

## The Rescue Mission

Paul says people are “**justified by his grace as a gift**” through “the **redemption that is in Christ Jesus**” (Romans 3:24). He also describes them as “**set free from sin**” (Romans 6:22). His explicit phrase “**bought with a price**” addresses the Corinthians, not the Romans (1 Corinthians 6:20; 7:23). Purchase and slavery language conveys costly liberation.

In the same account of redemption, Christ is the one “**whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood**” (Romans 3:25). Jesus says he gives his life as “a **ransom for many**” without naming a payee (Mark 10:45). Isaiah describes the servant bearing sin and making “**an offering for guilt**” (Isaiah 53:10–12). Paul also says Christ “**gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God**” (Ephesians 5:2).

The Father’s initiative addresses justice without depicting a payment that made him begin to love (Romans 3:25–26; 5:8–9).

These images belong to one **rescue mission** (salvation) at [the cross](the-cross.md). God warned Adam that disobedience would bring death (Genesis 2:16–17). The humans then disobeyed (Genesis 3:6). Their sin created the need for rescue, not a desire in the Father to cause pain. A firefighter may willingly risk injury to reach someone trapped. The aim is rescue, not suffering to please a commander.

## Raised, Honoured and Followed

The Son was not discarded after suffering. He was “**obedient to the point of death**”; “**Therefore God has highly exalted him**” (Philippians 2:8–9). Psalm 110:1 speaks of the king at God’s “**right hand**”. Peter says, “**This Jesus God raised up**”, and that God made him “**both Lord and Christ**” (Acts 2:32–36). His rule is “**to the glory of God the Father**” (Philippians 2:11). Resurrection and honour do not erase his anguish, but they show that death was not the end of the Father’s purpose for him.

Those who receive God’s gift are called to trust him and follow Jesus, not to earn rescue by a transaction. Jesus says, “If you **love me**, you will **keep my commandments**” (John 14:15). Paul’s question about continuing in sin after receiving grace rejects that course (Romans 6:1–4). This shared saving purpose is no claim that everyone is automatically saved or that the executioners’ violence was good.

## Conclusion

The Father’s sending of the Son is not presented as cruelty. [The Father's compassion](#the-fathers-compassion-and-love) moves Him to rescue, and [the Son acts willingly](#the-son-gives-himself-willingly), despite [real suffering](#his-human-suffering-was-real). The Father’s purpose is [forgiveness and renewal](#the-new-covenant-and-its-blood), not pain for its own sake. Those who killed Jesus remain responsible, and [God raises and honours him](#raised-honoured-and-followed). This does not make harming an innocent person permissible or settle every question about why the Father chose this course.
