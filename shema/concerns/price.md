# The Price of Salvation

The penalty for [sin is death](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/darkness).

*How can the death of one human being matter for billions of people?*

The concern is based on the following assumptions:

1. *One man's blood cannot be enough to pay for billions of people.*
2. *Only God is worthy to pay the price.*
3. *Unless someone believes Jesus is God Himself, their faith is insufficient.*

## The Father promises a new covenant

In Jeremiah 31:31–34, the LORD promises a [new covenant](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/covenants/christ) with the house of Israel and the house of Judah. Its blessings reach further than an outward declaration of acquittal: God will put His law within His people, they will know Him and belong to Him, and He says, “I will **forgive their iniquity**, and I will remember their sin no more.” Forgiveness and renewed relationship belong to the same promise.

Isaiah develops related promises. The servant is given “as a **covenant** for the people” (Isaiah 42:6; see also 49:8). Isaiah 53:4–12 portrays him bearing sins and suffering so that others receive peace and are accounted righteous. Isaiah 54:10 speaks of God’s covenant of peace. Isaiah 55:1–7 invites people to receive what they cannot buy, recalls the everlasting covenant and the sure mercies promised to David, and calls the wicked to return to the LORD, “for he will **abundantly pardon**.” Reading these passages together is a synthesis; Isaiah does not present a numbered ranking of the cross’s purposes.

Jeremiah names Israel and Judah, not an unnamed replacement people. Ephesians 2:11–22 describes Gentiles as formerly strangers to the covenants of promise, then brought near **by Christ’s blood**. In Christ, those once far off join one household with those once near. The widening of covenant blessing must not erase the people to whom Jeremiah’s promise was made.

## Blood, forgiveness, and the cost of covenant

At Sinai, Moses spoke the LORD’s words to Israel, the people agreed to them, and he sprinkled blood in connection with the covenant (Exodus 24:3–8). Blood was not a decorative signature. Against that background, Jesus says of the cup, “this is my **blood of the covenant**, which is poured out for many **for the forgiveness of sins**” (Matthew 26:28). Mark 14:24 likewise says “my blood of the covenant”. Paul records Jesus’ words, “This cup is the **new covenant in my blood**” (1 Corinthians 11:25).

These statements join covenant, sacrifice, and forgiveness. The cost is Jesus’ own life, willingly given, not an arbitrary formality. Nor does covenant language mean that God overlooks sin without dealing with it. Jesus’ death establishes what the Father promised while confronting the wrong from which people need forgiveness and release.

## Ransom is deliverance, not a headcount

Jesus says he came “to give his life **as a ransom for many**” (Mark 10:45; see Matthew 20:28). Paul calls him “the man Christ Jesus,” who “gave himself as a **ransom for all**” (1 Timothy 2:5–6). Believers have been bought with a price (1 Corinthians 6:20; 7:23), and 1 Peter 1:18–19 contrasts perishable silver and gold with Christ’s precious blood. Together, these passages describe costly deliverance. They do *not name a commercial payee, nor give Satan legitimate ownership of humanity, nor set a blood allowance per person, nor require an infinite victim*.

Psalm 49:5–15 keeps the cost in view without turning it into a market calculation. It says **no one** can ransom another or “give to God the **price of his life**” (verse 7). Therefore *wealth cannot purchase escape from death*. Yet its answer is not that deliverance is impossible: “But **God will ransom my soul** from the power of Sheol” (verse 15). **God determines the price.**

**The Father supplies** what people cannot. Isaiah 53:10–12 portrays **the *(single)* servant’s offering for guilt** and says he will “make **many** to be accounted righteous” and that “he bore the sin of **many**” (verses 11–12).

The New Testament applies the servant’s innocent suffering to Jesus (1 Peter 2:22–24). Paul likewise contrasts Adam and Christ: “by the **one man’s obedience** the **many** will be made righteous” (Romans 5:19; see verses 15–18). Romans 5:17 also describes receiving God’s gracious gift. These passages explain one-for-many deliverance through God’s provision and the obedience of his representative. God requires **quality, not quantity**. They do not calculate how much blood each person requires. Colossians 2:13–15 connects forgiveness and the cancellation of debt with the defeat of hostile powers, *not a payment made to Satan*.

## A sacrifice to God, not a reluctant Father’s bargain

Jesus “gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and **sacrifice to God**” (Ephesians 5:2). An offering *to God* is not automatically a literal commercial payment deposited with God. Leviticus 17:11 says God **gave** blood on the altar to make atonement: provision begins with him. Romans 3:24–26 likewise presents God as the one who puts Christ forward, while treating his blood, righteousness, and the former sins he had passed over as serious matters. Forgiveness is neither indifference to sin nor an attempt to persuade an unwilling Father.

Romans 5:8–11 makes that initiative personal: God shows his love while people are still sinners. Through Christ’s blood they are justified; through his death they are reconciled; through his life they will be saved from wrath. First John 4:9–10 likewise grounds the sending of the Son for sins in [God’s prior love](./love-of-god.md). Judgement is real, yet the Father who provides the sacrifice is the Father who loves. Jesus’ offering genuinely deals with sin (1 Peter 3:18).

## Why the human Messiah is worthy

Psalm 49:7–15 rules out a person purchasing escape from death with wealth. It does not say God cannot provide a human mediator. In the law, the Father says of sacrificial blood, “I have **given it for you** on the altar to make atonement for your souls” (Leviticus 17:11). That verse concerns altar offerings, not an independent authorization for any person to offer himself. It establishes that atonement depends on God’s provision, not a price humans set.

Isaiah 53:10–12 portrays the servant’s guilt offering and vindication within God’s purpose. Peter applies the servant’s innocent suffering to Jesus: “He committed **no sin**” (1 Peter 2:22), and “He himself **bore our sins** in his body on the tree” (verse 24). Sinlessness matters, but these passages do not suggest sinlessness alone would make any human a savior. God gave his Son (John 3:16), “put forward” Christ in connection with his blood (Romans 3:25), and raised and exalted him after his obedience unto death (Acts 2:32–36; Philippians 2:8–11). The case for Jesus’ offering rests on God’s provision and vindication of this obedient Messiah, not a rule that only God can be worthy to die. Trinitarian readers may understand these passages as consistent with the Son’s deity; none states that deity is a prerequisite for the offering’s acceptance.

## Forgiven people receive a new life

The covenant changes more than a legal description. Romans 6:3–23 links union with Jesus’ death and resurrection to the old self being crucified, freedom from sin’s mastery, and a new allegiance expressed in conduct. Paul says he speaks “in **human terms**” when describing slavery to righteousness (verse 19). His contrast between sin’s wages of death and God’s gift of life is not an instruction for sinners to pay God with their own blood. It calls forgiven people to live as those no longer ruled by sin.

This fits Jeremiah’s promise of a law written within. In 2 Corinthians 3:3–6, Paul describes a new-covenant ministry of the Spirit writing on human hearts. In 2 Corinthians 5:14–19, Christ’s death and risen life redirect how people live, while God reconciles the world to himself through Christ. Titus 2:14 joins Jesus’ self-giving to a people eager for good works. Liberation and renewal are consequences of his costly act, not substitutes for it.

## Why a human Messiah matters

Romans 5:15–19 does not apologise for the obedient one **being a man**. That is central to its contrast with Adam. God anointed Jesus (Acts 10:38), raised him from death (Acts 2), and exalted the one who had obeyed even to death (Philippians 2:8–11). The Father’s help and the resurrection do not make Jesus’ obedience or suffering unreal. They show whose purpose his costly, willing act fulfilled.

Trinitarian Christians also affirm that Jesus truly suffered as a human being and distinguish the Father from the Son. The disagreement should not be framed as though they necessarily believe in fake suffering. The question here is whether Scripture needs Jesus to be Almighty God for his sacrifice to be effective. These passages instead place confidence in the Father’s gracious covenant and his genuinely human Messiah.

## What faith in Jesus confesses

John states his purpose: “these are written so that **you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, [the Son of God](../../index.md)**, and that by believing you may have life in his name” (John 20:31). The apostolic gospel includes that Christ “**died for our sins** in accordance with the Scriptures” and “was **raised on the third day** in accordance with the Scriptures” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4). These texts affirm faith in Jesus and his saving work without stating the later doctrinal formula as a condition.

Paul writes, “if you confess with your mouth that **Jesus is Lord** and believe in your heart that **God raised him from the dead**, you will be saved” (Romans 10:9). Confessing Jesus as Lord must not be reduced to a modern claim about ontology; the wider discussion of “Lord” in Romans 10:11–13 also deserves attention. John testifies that “the **Father has sent his Son** to be the Savior of the world” and says that whoever confesses “Jesus is **the Son of God**” abides in God (1 John 4:14–15). These statements identify what their authors affirm; they neither supply an exhaustive doctrinal checklist nor authorize a verdict on any individual’s salvation.

## Conclusion

The Father’s [promised covenant](#the-father-promises-a-new-covenant), Jesus’ [costly self-offering](#blood-forgiveness-and-the-cost-of-covenant) frame the answers:

1. **One man for many:** The [ransom and representative-obedience passages](#ransom-is-deliverance-not-a-headcount) describe God’s deliverance through one servant and one obedient man, not a blood-volume quota.
2. **Worthiness:** God [provided and vindicated the sinless, obedient Messiah](#why-the-human-messiah-is-worthy). The cited passages do not state that only God can be worthy to offer this sacrifice.
3. **Faith:** The [gospel and confession passages](#what-faith-in-jesus-confesses) call for faith in Jesus as Christ, Son of God, and Lord, and in God’s raising him. They do not explicitly make belief that Jesus is God Himself a condition or settle any individual’s salvation.
