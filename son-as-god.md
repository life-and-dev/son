# The Son as God

The question of whether Jesus should be called "God" has been a central and often contentious issue throughout Christian history. Centuries of theological debate, from the Arian controversy of the fourth century to the present day, have centered on how to define the nature of the Son in relation to the Father. This article explores the complexities of this issue by examining the biblical and historical use of the term "God."

## The Flexible Meaning of "God"

In ancient Hebrew and Greek, the words for "god" (`Elohim` and `Theos`) were used with a degree of flexibility that is unfamiliar to modern English speakers. These terms were not always reserved for the supreme, Almighty God. On several occasions in the Old Testament, the title `Elohim` is applied to human leaders, such as kings and judges, to signify their authority as representatives of God (Exodus 7:1; Psalm 45:6–7). This historical context is crucial for understanding how the early followers of Jesus might have understood titles of divinity.

## What Scripture Affirms About the Son

The New Testament attributes extraordinary power and authority to Jesus. The majority of Christians agree that the scriptures affirm that Jesus:

*   Will judge humanity and forgive sins (Matthew 10:32-33; John 5:22; 1 John 2:1; Mark 2:10)
*   Will return with an army of angels (Matthew 16:27-28)
*   Will rule over the world (Matthew 28:18; Psalm 2:8-12; Isaiah 42:1-4; Hebrews 2:7-8; Philippians 2:10-11)
*   Has the authority to heal (John 4:46-54, 5:8-9)
*   Has authority over death (John 5:21; Revelation 1:17-18)
*   Looks after his people (John 10:14)
*   Is eternal (Revelation 1:17-18)

These passages clearly establish a unique and exalted status for the Son. However, the question remains whether these affirmations equate to the title "God" in the absolute, monotheistic sense.

## "Son of God" vs. "Son as God"

The Bible frequently uses the title "Son of God" to describe Jesus (Matthew 4:3; Mark 1:1; John 1:34; Romans 1:4). This phrase emphasizes his unique relationship with the Father and his divine origin. In contrast, the phrase "the Son as God," which would imply that Jesus is the Almighty God himself, is not found in scripture. This distinction is the foundation of a theological debate that has continued for centuries.

## Addressing Common Counter-Arguments

Two passages are often cited to support the claim that Jesus is God: John 1:1 and John 20:28.

*   **John 1:1:** This verse states, "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." While the final clause appears to be a direct statement of Jesus's divinity, the original Greek (`kai theos en ho logos`) is more nuanced. The absence of a definite article before `theos` ("God") has led some scholars to suggest that the phrase is best understood as describing the *nature* of the Word as divine, rather than identifying the Word as the one God (`ho theos`).

*   **John 20:28:** When Thomas sees the resurrected Jesus, he exclaims, "My Lord and my God!" This is often interpreted as a direct confession of Jesus as God. However, an alternative interpretation is that this is a dual exclamation: Thomas acknowledges Jesus as his "Lord" and then, in awe, gives praise to the Father as "my God," recognizing God's power in the resurrection.

## Conclusion

The Bible bestows upon Jesus the highest honors and authority, yet it consistently distinguishes between him and God the Father. This distinction is crucial for maintaining clarity in monotheistic faith.

*   The term "god" had a more flexible meaning in the ancient world than it does today, and was sometimes used to refer to powerful human leaders. (See [The Flexible Meaning of "God"](#the-flexible-meaning-of-god))
*   While the scriptures affirm the supreme authority and eternal nature of the Son, they do not use the phrase "the Son as God." (See ["Son of God" vs. "Son as God"](#son-of-god-vs-son-as-god))
*   Common counter-arguments, such as John 1:1 and John 20:28, have alternative interpretations that are consistent with the distinction between the Son and the Father. (See [Addressing Common Counter-Arguments](#addressing-common-counter-arguments))

By reserving the title "God" for the Almighty Father, it is possible to fully honor the Son and all that the scriptures affirm about him, while at the same time preserving the core principles of monotheism.
