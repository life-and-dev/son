# Jesus Claims to Be One With the Father

The statement "I and the Father are one" is often cited as a direct claim to divinity by Jesus. However, a closer look at the linguistic context and other biblical uses of similar phrasing reveals a different perspective. This article explores whether this unity refers to being the same being or having a shared purpose and mission.

Jesus told His disciples:

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

## The Greek Word for One

In John 10:30, the Greek word for "one" is *hen* (ἕν). This is the neuter form of the word. In Greek, the masculine form *heis* (εἷς) would typically be used to indicate that two things are the same person or being. The use of the neuter *hen* suggests a unity of essence, purpose, or action rather than personal identity.

This distinction is significant when compared to the Shema in Deuteronomy 6:4. The Septuagint (LXX), which is the Greek translation of the Old Testament, uses the masculine *heis* to describe God as "one". By using *hen* in John 10:30, the text points toward a functional unity. This means Jesus and the Father are united in their work and mission.

However, Jesus does **not** claim to be the Father. When Jesus says two separate persons are one, he means they are united or "joined together". A group of people could also be considered "one":

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that **they may be one, even as we are one**. — John 17:11 (ESV)
 >
> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that **they may be one even as we are one**, I in them and you in me, that **they may become perfectly one**, so that the world may know that you sent me and **loved them even as you loved me**. — John 17:22-23 (ESV)

## Jesus Defends His Statement

When the religious leaders accused Jesus of making himself God, he responded by quoting the scriptures. He pointed out that those to whom the word of God came were called "gods" in the Law.

> Jesus answered them, “Is it not written in your Law, ‘I said, you are gods’? If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be broken—do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, ‘You are blaspheming,’ because I said, ‘I am the Son of God’?” — John 10:34-36 (ESV)

Jesus did not claim to be the Most High God. Instead, he defended his title as the "Son of God" by showing that human leaders were also given divine titles due to their delegated authority.
Jesus was not asking the Father to turn his disciples into gods. Just as the disciples are distinct individuals, yet united as one. So are [Jesus and the Father distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md), yet united as one.

## Conclusion

The claim that Jesus and the Father are one refers to a unity of purpose and mission rather than ontological identity. The [Greek grammar](#the-greek-word-for-one) and Jesus' own [defense](#jesus-defends-his-statement) demonstrate that this unity signifies a shared mission. Numerous [biblical parallels](#humans-can-also-be-one-with-each-other) show that separate individuals can be described as "one" without being the same person.
