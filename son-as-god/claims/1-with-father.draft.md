# Jesus Claims to Be One With the Father

Many readers encounter the statement of Jesus that he and the Father are one
and assume it defines his divine nature. However, a closer look at how the Bible
uses the concept of oneness suggests a different perspective. This claim may
not describe an identity of being, but rather a profound unity of purpose and
mission.

## The Unity of the Father and the Son

When Jesus declared his unity with the Father, he used language that emphasized
their shared mission.

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

In the original Greek text, the word for "one" is *hen* (neuter). Scholars often
note that if Jesus intended to claim he and the Father were the same person or
being, the masculine *heis* would typically be used. The use of the neuter
suggests a functional unity or a "one thing" in terms of action and will.

Jesus later used this same language when praying for his disciples. He asked
that they might experience the same kind of oneness that he shares with God.

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that they may be
> one, even as we are one. — John 17:11 (ESV)

Jesus also explained that this unity comes from sharing the glory he received
from the Father.

> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one
> even as we are one, I in them and you in me, that they may become perfectly
> one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them even as you
> loved me. — John 17:22-23 (ESV)

Jesus was not asking the Father to turn his disciples into gods. Just as the
disciples remain distinct individuals while being united in purpose, Jesus and
the Father are [distinct individuals](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) who are
united as one in their work.

## Oneness Among Humans

The Bible provides several examples where two or more distinct entities are
described as being "one." These examples help clarify that oneness does not
require a literal merging of identities.

### United in Marriage

The most common biblical example of oneness is found in marriage. Although a
husband and wife remain two separate people, they are described as a single
unit.

> Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his
> wife, and they shall become one flesh. — Genesis 2:24 (ESV)

Jesus reaffirmed this concept when teaching about the beginning of creation.

> But “God made them male and female” from the beginning of creation. “This
> explains why a man leaves his father and mother and is joined to his wife,
> and the two are united into one.” Since they are no longer two but one, let
> no one split apart what God has joined together. — Mark 10:6-9 (NLT)

The apostle Paul also used this "one flesh" terminology when warning against
sexual immorality.

> Or do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute becomes one body
> with her? For, as it is written, “The two will become one flesh.”
> — 1 Corinthians 6:16 (ESV)

### Unity of Spirit and Soul

Oneness can also describe a deep spiritual or emotional connection between
individuals.

> But he who is joined to the Lord becomes one spirit with him.
> — 1 Corinthians 6:17 (ESV)

The Law of Moses uses similar language to describe a very close friend.

> If your brother, the son of your mother, or your son or your daughter or the
> wife you embrace or your friend who is as your own soul entices you
> secretly... — Deuteronomy 13:6 (ESV)

This type of soul-level unity was famously seen in the relationship between
David and Jonathan.

> As soon as he had finished speaking to Saul, the soul of Jonathan was knit to
> the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.
> — 1 Samuel 18:1 (ESV)

The early Christian community also exemplified this unity. They were described
as having a single heart and soul despite being a large group of people.

> Now the full number of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no
> one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they
> had everything in common. — Acts 4:32 (ESV)

## Responding to Accusations

When the Jewish leaders accused Jesus of making himself God because of his
claims of oneness, Jesus defended himself by appealing to the scriptures.

> Jesus answered them, “Is it not written in your Law, ‘I said, you are gods’?
> If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be
> broken—do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world,
> ‘You are blaspheming,’ because I said, ‘I am the Son of God’?”
> — John 10:34-36 (ESV)

Jesus pointed to Psalm 82:6 to show that even human judges were sometimes
called "gods" in a representational sense. His defense suggests that his claim
to be "one" with the Father was a statement of his divine commission and
representative role, rather than a claim to be God himself.

## Conclusion

The claim that Jesus and the Father are one describes a [unity of
purpose](#the-unity-of-the-father-and-the-son) rather than an identity of
being. This is consistent with other biblical examples of oneness, such as the
[union in marriage](#united-in-marriage) or the [unity of spirit and
soul](#unity-of-spirit-and-soul) shared between believers. Jesus' own
[response to his critics](#responding-to-accusations) reinforces that his
oneness is based on his role as the one whom the Father sent.
