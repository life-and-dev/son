# Jesus Claims to be One with the Father

The statement made by Jesus regarding his relationship with God the Father often
sparks significant theological discussion. This exploration examines the context
of his claim to be "one" and how the Bible uses similar terminology to describe
unity among humans and between believers and God. Understanding these various
forms of oneness provides clarity on the nature of the unity Jesus described.

## Unity of Purpose and Essence

Jesus made a direct statement about his relationship with the Father during his
ministry.

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

There are different perspectives on what this "oneness" signifies. Many
interpret this as an ontological unity. This means that Jesus and the Father
share the same divine essence or substance. This view suggests that they are
distinct persons but one God. Trinitarians often argue that the immediate
reaction of the listeners in John 10:31, who picked up stones to stone him for
blasphemy, indicates they understood him to be claiming equality with God in
essence.

Others view this as a functional unity or a unity of purpose. This
interpretation suggests that Jesus and the Father are united in their will,
mission, and actions. Supporters of this view point to the Greek grammar used
in the passage. The word for "one" in John 10:30 is the neuter *hen* (ἕν),
rather than the masculine *heis* (εἷς). A neuter "one" typically denotes unity
of purpose, action, or will. If Jesus intended to claim he was the same person
as the Father, the masculine *heis* would have been the more appropriate
grammatical choice.

Jesus uses the same neuter *hen* to describe the unity he desires for his
followers.

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that they may
> be one, even as we are one. — John 17:11 (ESV)
>
> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one
> even as we are one, I in them and you in me, that they may become perfectly
> one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them even as you
> loved me. — John 17:22-23 (ESV)

Jesus was not suggesting that his disciples would become part of the Godhead or
lose their individual identities. Instead, he prayed for a profound unity of
heart and purpose among them. This was modeled after the unity he shared with
the Father. Just as the disciples remained distinct individuals while being
"one," Jesus and the Father are [presented as distinct
entities](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) who are perfectly united in their work.

Critiques of the "unity of purpose" view often point to the surrounding context
of John 10. They argue that the power to protect the sheep mentioned in verses
28 and 29 is a divine prerogative. Therefore, the "oneness" must include the
power and nature required to exercise that prerogative. However, defenders of
the "unity of purpose" view respond by noting that Jesus explicitly states the
Father *gave* him the sheep and is greater than all (John 10:29). This suggests
his authority is delegated rather than being an inherent part of an identical
essence.

## Human Expressions of Oneness

The Bible uses the concept of oneness to describe various human relationships.
This illustrates that being "one" does not necessarily mean being the same
person or entity.

### Oneness in Marriage

The most common biblical example of two distinct individuals becoming one is
found in the description of marriage.

> So the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon the man, and while he slept
> took one of his ribs and closed up its place with flesh. And the rib that the
> LORD God had taken from the man he made into a woman and brought her to the
> man.
>
> Then the man said, “This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my
> flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.”
>
> Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his
> wife, and they shall become one flesh. And the man and his wife were both
> naked and were not ashamed. — Genesis 2:21-24 (ESV)

Jesus later reaffirmed this concept when teaching about marriage.

> But Jesus responded, “He wrote this commandment only as a concession to your
> hard hearts. But ‘God made them male and female’ from the beginning of
> creation. ‘This explains why a man leaves his father and mother and is
> joined to his wife, and the two are united into one.’ Since they are no
> longer two but one, let no one split apart what God has joined together.”
> — Mark 10:5-9 (NLT)

This "one flesh" union represents a unique and intimate bond. However, the
husband and wife remain two separate people. The apostle Paul also applies this
concept to illustrate the seriousness of sexual immorality.

> Or do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute becomes one body
> with her? For, as it is written, “The two will become one flesh.”
> — 1 Corinthians 6:16 (ESV)

### Spiritual Unity with the Lord

Believers are also described as being in a state of oneness with Jesus Christ
through the Holy Spirit.

> But he who is joined to the Lord becomes one spirit with him.
> — 1 Corinthians 6:17 (ESV)

This spiritual oneness signifies a deep connection and alignment with the
Spirit of Christ. It does not imply that the believer becomes Christ himself.

### Unity of Heart and Soul

The Bible further describes deep friendship and communal harmony as a form of
being "one soul" or "one heart."

> If your brother, the son of your mother, or your son or your daughter or the
> wife you embrace or your friend who is as your own soul...
> — Deuteronomy 13:6 (ESV)

The friendship between David and Jonathan is a classic example of this
soul-knit unity.

> As soon as he had finished speaking to Saul, the soul of Jonathan was knit to
> the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul. And Saul took him
> that day and would not let him return to his father’s house. Then Jonathan
> made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul.
> — 1 Samuel 18:1-3 (ESV)

The early Christian community also exhibited this level of unity.

> Now the full number of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no
> one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they
> had everything in common. — Acts 4:32 (ESV)

In all these examples, "oneness" describes a perfect harmony and union between
distinct individuals. This provides a framework for understanding Jesus' own
claim of being one with the Father.

## Conclusion

The claim of Jesus to be one with the Father can be understood through
different theological lenses. While some emphasize a [unity of
essence](#unity-of-purpose-and-essence), others highlight a [unity of purpose
and will](#unity-of-purpose-and-essence) consistent with how the term is
applied to [believers](#unity-of-purpose-and-essence). Biblical examples of
oneness in [marriage](#oneness-in-marriage), [spiritual
life](#spiritual-unity-with-the-lord), and [close
relationships](#unity-of-heart-and-soul) demonstrate that two or more distinct
individuals can be described as "one" without losing their separate identities.
