# Jesus Claims to Be One With the Father

In the Gospel of John, Jesus makes a statement that has become a central point
of theological discussion for centuries. He declares a unique connection between
himself and God. This claim has led to various interpretations regarding the
nature of their relationship. Understanding this statement requires a close look
at the biblical context and the language used in the original text.

## The Statement of Unity

Jesus told His disciples:

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

This statement is often viewed through two primary theological lenses.
Trinitarians generally interpret this as a claim to ontological oneness. They
believe Jesus is stating that he and the Father share the same divine essence or
being. Unitarians typically view this as a unity of purpose or will. They argue
that Jesus and the Father are working together in perfect harmony.

### The Greek Context

The Greek word used for "one" in John 10:30 is *hen*. This is a neuter form of
the word. In Greek grammar, a masculine form (*heis*) would typically be used to
indicate numerical oneness or being the same person. The neuter form often
signifies a unity of essence, purpose, or action.

### The Reaction of the Jews

The immediate context of the statement provides insight into how the original
audience understood Jesus. The religious leaders responded with hostility.

> The Jews picked up stones again to stone him. Jesus answered them, “I have
> shown you many good works from the Father; for which of them are you going to
> stone me?” The Jews answered him, “It is not for a good work that we are going
> to stone you but for blasphemy, because you, being a man, make yourself God.”
> — John 10:31-33 (ESV)

The religious leaders interpreted his words as a claim to deity. Jesus, however,
responded by quoting the Law.

> Jesus answered them, “Is it not written in your Law, ‘I said, you are gods’?
> If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be
> broken—do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world,
> ‘You are blaspheming,’ because I said, ‘I am the Son of God’?” — John 10:34-36
> (ESV)

Jesus pointed to Psalm 82:6 where humans are called "gods" because of their
divine appointment or authority. He used this to defend his own claim as the
"Son of God." He emphasized his commission and the works he performed in the
Father's name.

## The Nature of Oneness

While some see John 10:30 as a claim to be the same being as God, other passages
suggest a different kind of unity. When Jesus says two separate persons are one,
he often means they are united or joined together. A group of people could also
be considered one:

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that **they may
> be one, even as we are one**. — John 17:11 (ESV)
>
> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that **they may be one
> even as we are one**, I in them and you in me, that **they may become
> perfectly one**, so that the world may know that you sent me and **loved them
> even as you loved me**. — John 17:23 (ESV)

Jesus was not asking the Father to turn his disciples into gods. The disciples
are distinct individuals who are united as one. In the same way,
[Jesus and the Father are distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) but united as
one. This comparison in John 17 suggests that the oneness between the Father and
the Son is the same type of unity that believers are called to have with one
another.

## Human Unity

Humans can also be one with each other in various ways that do not involve
becoming the same individual.

### One Flesh

The Bible describes marriage as a form of oneness between two distinct people.

> So the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon the man, and while he slept
> took one of his ribs and closed up its place with flesh. And the rib that the
> LORD God had taken from the man he made into a woman and brought her to the
> man.
>
> Then the man said, “This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh;
> she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.”
>
> Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his
> wife, and **they shall become one flesh**. And the man and his wife were both
> naked and were not ashamed.
>
> — Genesis 2:21-24 (ESV)

Jesus also quoted this passage to emphasize the permanence of the marriage bond:

> But Jesus responded, “He wrote this commandment only as a concession to your
> hard hearts. But ‘God made them male and female’ from the beginning of
> creation. ‘This explains why a man leaves his father and mother and is
> **joined to his wife**, and **the two are united into one**.’ Since **they are
> no longer two but one**, let no one split apart what God has **joined
> together**.” — Mark 10:5-9 (NLT)

The apostle Paul also uses this terminology:

> Or do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute **becomes one body**
> with her? For, as it is written, *“The two will become one flesh.”* — 1
> Corinthians 6:16 (ESV)

### One Spirit

Believers are described as being in a spiritual union with Christ.

> But he who is joined to the Lord (Jesus Christ) **becomes one spirit** with
> him. — 1 Corinthians 6:17 (ESV)

### One Soul

Friendship and community are also described using the language of oneness.

> If your brother, the son of your mother, or your son or your daughter or the
> wife you embrace or your friend **who is as your own soul**... — Deuteronomy
> 13:6 (ESV)
>
> As soon as he had finished speaking to Saul, **the soul of Jonathan was knit
> to the soul of David**, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul. And Saul took
> him that day and would not let him return to his father’s house. Then Jonathan
> made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul. — 1 Samuel
> 18:1-3 (ESV)
>
> Now the full number of those who believed were of **one heart and soul**, and
> no one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they
> had everything in common. — Acts 4:32 (ESV)

## Conclusion

The claim that Jesus and the Father are one is a significant theological
statement with [multiple interpretations](#the-statement-of-unity). While it is
often used to support the idea of shared essence, the
[Greek grammar](#the-greek-context) and
[Jesus' own defense](#the-reaction-of-the-jews) suggest a more nuanced meaning.
The [nature of their oneness](#the-nature-of-oneness) is compared to the unity
between believers, which is a union of purpose and love rather than identity.
This type of unity is also reflected in
[human relationships](#human-unity), where distinct individuals become one in
flesh, spirit, or soul.
