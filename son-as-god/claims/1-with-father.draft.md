# Jesus Claims to Be One With the Father

The Gospel of John records a powerful statement from Jesus that has sparked intense theological debate for centuries. When Jesus declared that he and the Father are one, his listeners immediately recognized the weight of his words. This article explores the biblical context of this claim to understand how the scriptures define oneness between distinct individuals.

Jesus told his disciples:

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

The Greek word used for "one" in this verse is *hen*, which is in the neuter gender. If Jesus had intended to claim he was the same person as the Father, the masculine form *heis* would have been expected. Instead, the use of the neuter *hen* signifies a unity of purpose, action, or will rather than a personal identity or personhood.

However, Jesus does not claim to be the Father. When Jesus says two separate persons are one, he means they are united. They are joined together in their mission. A group of people could also be considered one:

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that **they may be one, even as we are one**. — John 17:11 (ESV)
>
> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that **they may be one even as we are one**, I in them and you in me, that **they may become perfectly one**, so that the world may know that you sent me and **loved them even as you loved me**. — John 17:23 (ESV)

Jesus was not asking the Father to turn his disciples into gods. The disciples remain distinct individuals while being united as one. In the same way, [Jesus and the Father are distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). They are two individuals who are nevertheless united as one.

## The Jews' Reaction and Jesus' Defense

When Jesus made this statement, the Jews picked up stones to stone him. They accused him of blasphemy, claiming he made himself out to be God. Jesus responded by quoting the scriptures:

> Jesus answered them, “Is it not written in your Law, ‘I said, you are gods’? If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be broken—do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, ‘You are blaspheming,’ because I said, ‘I am the Son of God’?” — John 10:34-36 (ESV)

Jesus referred to Psalm 82:6, where God addresses human judges or angelic beings as "gods". His argument was that if the scriptures could use the title "gods" for those who received the word of God, then it was not blasphemous for him to call himself the "Son of God". This defense suggests that Jesus was not claiming to be the Almighty God himself. Instead, he was claiming a unique relationship and commission from the Father.

## Conclusion

The biblical evidence suggests that when Jesus claimed to be one with the Father, he was referring to a [unity of purpose and mission](#jesus-claims-to-be-one-with-the-father). His use of the neuter [Greek word *hen*](#jesus-claims-to-be-one-with-the-father) points toward this functional oneness rather than an identity of person. Jesus' [defense against the Jews](#the-jews-reaction-and-jesus-defense) further clarified that his claim to be the Son of God was consistent with how the scriptures describe relationships with the Divine. Furthermore, numerous biblical examples show that [distinct human individuals](#humans-can-also-be-one-with-each-other) can be described as one without losing their separate identities.
