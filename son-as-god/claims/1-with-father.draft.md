# Jesus and the Father Are One

The statement "I and the Father are one" is often cited as a definitive claim of
identity or essence. However, a closer look at the context and other biblical
examples of "oneness" suggests a different interpretation. This article
explores how Jesus used the concept of unity to describe his relationship with
God and how similar language is applied to human relationships throughout the
scriptures.

## Unity in Purpose

Jesus spoke these words to his disciples:

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

This unity does not imply that Jesus is the Father. Instead, it describes a
perfect union of will and action. Jesus clarified this type of unity when
praying for his followers:

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that they may be
> one, even as we are one. — John 17:11 (ESV)
>
> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one
> even as we are one, I in them and you in me, that they may become perfectly
> one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them even as you
> loved me. — John 17:23 (ESV)

Jesus was not asking for his disciples to become divine beings. He prayed that
they would be united in the same way he is united with the Father. Just as the
disciples remain distinct individuals while being united,
[Jesus and the Father are distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) persons who
are one in purpose.

## Human Examples of Unity

The Bible frequently uses "oneness" to describe deep connections between human
beings. These examples help to illustrate that being "one" does not mean being
the same person.

### One Flesh

The most common example of human oneness is marriage. In the creation account,
the union of a man and a woman is described as becoming "one flesh."

> So the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon the man, and while he slept
> took one of his ribs and closed up its place with flesh. And the rib that the
> LORD God had taken from the man he made into a woman and brought her to the
> man. Then the man said, "This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my
> flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man."
>
> Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his
> wife, and they shall become one flesh. And the man and his wife were both
> naked and were not ashamed. — Genesis 2:21-24 (ESV)

Jesus later emphasized this point when discussing marriage:

> But Jesus responded, "He wrote this commandment only as a concession to your
> hard hearts. But 'God made them male and female' from the beginning of
> creation. 'This explains why a man leaves his father and mother and is joined
> to his wife, and the two are united into one.' Since they are no longer two
> but one, let no one split apart what God has joined together."
> — Mark 10:5-9 (NLT)

The Apostle Paul also used this language to describe physical union:

> Or do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute becomes one body with
> her? For, as it is written, "The two will become one flesh."
> — 1 Corinthians 6:16 (ESV)

### One Spirit

Beyond physical union, the Bible speaks of a spiritual oneness with the Lord.

> But he who is joined to the Lord becomes one spirit with him.
> — 1 Corinthians 6:17 (ESV)

### One Soul

Deep friendship and communal life are also described using the language of
oneness. The law of Moses describes a close friend as being like one's own soul.

> If your brother, the son of your mother, or your son or your daughter or the
> wife you embrace or your friend who is as your own soul...
> — Deuteronomy 13:6 (ESV)

The relationship between David and Jonathan is another powerful example of souls
being knit together in unity.

> As soon as he had finished speaking to Saul, the soul of Jonathan was knit to
> the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul. And Saul took him
> that day and would not let him return to his father's house. Then Jonathan
> made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul.
> — 1 Samuel 18:1-3 (ESV)

The early Christian community also exhibited this same heart and soul unity.

> Now the full number of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no
> one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they had
> everything in common. — Acts 4:32 (ESV)

## Understanding the Oneness

The Greek word used for "one" in John 10:30 is *hen*, which is neuter. If Jesus
had intended to say he and the Father were the same person, the masculine *heis*
would have been expected. The neuter form indicates a "one thing," referring to
a unity of purpose, will, or action.

When the Jewish leaders accused Jesus of blasphemy for this statement, his
defense further clarified his position. He pointed to Psalm 82:6, where human
judges are called "gods," and asked:

> Do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, 'You
> are blaspheming,' because I said, 'I am the Son of God'?
> — John 10:36 (ESV)

Jesus did not claim to be God the Father. He identified himself as the one sent
by the Father, emphasizing his role as the Son of God who acts in perfect
harmony with God's will.

## Conclusion

The claim that Jesus and the Father are one refers to a
[unity in purpose](#unity-in-purpose) rather than an identity of essence. This
is supported by [human examples of unity](#human-examples-of-unity) where
distinct individuals are described as one flesh, spirit, or soul.
[Understanding the oneness](#understanding-the-oneness) confirms that Jesus'
language pointed to his functional oneness with the Father as the Son of God.
