# Jesus and the Father Are One

When Jesus declared that he and the Father were one, his audience reacted with
strong emotion. This statement is often used to support the idea that Jesus is
claiming to be the same person as God. However, the Bible uses similar language
to describe relationships between humans and between believers and God.
Exploring these parallels provides a clearer understanding of what it means to
be "one" in a biblical context.

## Unity of Purpose

In John 10:30, Jesus makes a statement about his relationship with the Father.

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

The Greek word used for "one" in this verse is *hen*, which is in the neuter
gender. In Greek, the masculine word for "one" (*heis*) would be used to
indicate that two things are numerically the same person or entity. By using the
neuter *hen*, the text signifies a unity of essence, purpose, or action rather
than a numerical identity.

This distinction is consistent with how Jesus describes the unity of his
followers. He prays for his disciples to be "one" using the same neuter word:

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that they may be
> one, even as we are one. — John 17:11 (ESV)
>
> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one
> even as we are one, I in them and you in me, that they may become perfectly
> one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them even as you
> loved me. — John 17:22-23 (ESV)

Jesus was not asking the Father to turn his disciples into a single person or
into gods. Just as the disciples are distinct individuals yet united as one, so
are [Jesus and the Father distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) yet united as
one.

## Response to Critics

The reaction of the Jewish leaders in John 10:31-33 is often cited as evidence
that Jesus was claiming to be God. They picked up stones to kill him, accusing
him of blasphemy because he, "a mere man," claimed to be God.

However, Jesus' immediate response in verses 34-36 clarifies his position. He
points to Psalm 82:6, where human judges are called "gods" (*elohim*) because
they represented God's authority. Jesus argues that if those to whom the word of
God came could be called "gods," then he is certainly justified in calling
himself the "Son of God." This defense shows that his claim to be "one" with the
Father was a claim of divine authority and shared mission, rather than a claim
to be the Father himself.

## Examples of Functional Unity

The concept of multiple individuals becoming "one" appears throughout the Bible
to describe various types of close, functional relationships.

### One Flesh

In the Old Testament, the relationship between a husband and wife is described
as becoming "one flesh."

> So the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon the man, and while he slept
> took one of his ribs and closed up its place with flesh. And the rib that the
> LORD God had taken from the man he made into a woman and brought her to the
> man. Then the man said, “This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my
> flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.”
> Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his
> wife, and they shall become one flesh. — Genesis 2:21-24 (ESV)

Jesus also quoted this concept to emphasize that the two remain distinct people
but are united by God:

> But Jesus responded, “He wrote this commandment only as a concession to your
> hard hearts. But ‘God made them male and female’ from the beginning of
> creation. ‘This explains why a man leaves his father and mother and is joined
> to his wife, and the two are united into one.’ Since they are no longer two
> but one, let no one split apart what God has joined together.”
> — Mark 10:5-9 (NLT)
>
> Or do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute becomes one body with
> her? For, as it is written, “The two will become one flesh.”
> — 1 Corinthians 6:16 (ESV)

### One Spirit

Unity can also be spiritual, as described in the relationship between a
believer and Christ.

> But he who is joined to the Lord becomes one spirit with him.
> — 1 Corinthians 6:17 (ESV)

### One Soul

Emotional and social unity is also described using the word "one."

> If your brother, the son of your mother, or your son or your daughter or the
> wife you embrace or your friend who is as your own soul...
> — Deuteronomy 13:6 (ESV)
>
> As soon as he had finished speaking to Saul, the soul of Jonathan was knit to
> the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul. And Saul took him
> that day and would not let him return to his father’s house. Then Jonathan
> made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul.
> — 1 Samuel 18:1-3 (ESV)
>
> Now the full number of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no
> one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they
> had everything in common. — Acts 4:32 (ESV)

## Conclusion

The biblical use of the term "one" frequently describes a
[unity of purpose](#unity-of-purpose) and mission between distinct individuals.
In John 10:30, Jesus uses linguistic distinctions and
[scriptural arguments](#response-to-critics) to define his relationship with the
Father as a functional oneness. This same
[functional unity](#examples-of-functional-unity) is seen in marriage, spiritual
fellowship, and deep human friendships, where individuals remain separate beings
while acting as one.
