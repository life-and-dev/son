# Jesus Claims to Be One With the Father

When Jesus declared his oneness with the Father, he used language that has sparked centuries of theological debate. Many readers wonder if this statement implies that Jesus is the same person as God or if it points toward a different kind of relationship. By examining the biblical context and the original language, one can uncover the deeper meaning behind this remarkable claim.

## Oneness in the New Testament

Jesus told his disciples:

> I and the Father are one. — John 10:30 (ESV)

### The Greek Context

In the original Greek text of John 10:30, the word for "one" is *hen* (ἕν), which is in the neuter gender. If Jesus intended to claim that he and the Father were the same person or shared the same ontological essence, he would likely have used the masculine form *heis* (εἷς). The use of the neuter *hen* typically signifies a unity of purpose, action, or will. This same construction appears when Paul describes himself and Apollos as being "one" in their ministry (1 Corinthians 3:8). In that context, no one argues that Paul and Apollos are the same being.

However, Jesus does not claim to be the Father. When Jesus says two separate persons are one, he means they are united or joined together. A group of people could also be considered one:

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that **they may be one, even as we are one**. — John 17:11 (ESV)

> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that **they may be one even as we are one**, I in them and you in me, that **they may become perfectly one**, so that the world may know that you sent me and **loved them even as you loved me**. — John 17:22-23 (ESV)

Jesus was not asking the Father to turn his disciples into gods. Just as the disciples are distinct individuals, yet united as one, so are [Jesus and the Father distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md), yet united as one.

### Response to the Accusation of Blasphemy

Some critics point to the reaction of the Jewish leaders in John 10:33 as proof that Jesus was claiming to be God. They accused him of blasphemy, saying, "you, being a man, make yourself God." However, Jesus’ own response clarifies his position. He quoted Psalm 82:6, where human judges are referred to as "gods" because they represented God's authority.

> Jesus answered them, “Is it not written in your Law, ‘I said, you are gods’? If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be broken—do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, ‘You are blaspheming,’ because I said, ‘I am the Son of God’?” — John 10:34-36 (ESV)

By using this argument, Jesus demonstrated that his title as the Son of God was a matter of divine appointment and representation. He did not affirm their accusation that he was making himself equal to the Father in essence. Instead, he maintained his identity as the one sent by the Father.

For more detailed scholarly analysis of this passage, see the [BiblicalUnitarian.com commentary on John 10:30](https://www.biblicalunitarian.com/videos/understanding-john-10-30).

## Humans Can Also Be One With Each Other

### One Flesh

> So the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon the man, and while he slept took one of his ribs and closed up its place with flesh. And the rib that the LORD God had taken from the man he made into a woman and brought her to the man. Then the man said, “This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.” Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and **they shall become one flesh**. And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed. — Genesis 2:21-24 (ESV)

Also, Jesus quoted this:

> But Jesus responded, “He wrote this commandment only as a concession to your hard hearts. But ‘God made them male and female’ from the beginning of creation. ‘This explains why a man leaves his father and mother and is **joined to his wife**, and **the two are united into one**.’ Since **they are no longer two but one**, let no one split apart what God has **joined together**.” — Mark 10:5-9 (NLT)

> Or do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute **becomes one body** with her? For, as it is written, *“The two will become one flesh.”* — 1 Corinthians 6:15 (ESV)

### One Spirit

> But he who is joined to the Lord (Jesus Christ) **becomes one spirit** with him. — 1 Corinthians 6:17 (ESV)

### One Soul

> If your brother, the son of your mother, or your son or your daughter or the wife you embrace or your friend **who is as your own soul**... — Deuteronomy 13:6 (ESV)

> As soon as he had finished speaking to Saul, **the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David**, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul. And Saul took him that day and would not let him return to his father’s house. Then Jonathan made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul. — 1 Samuel 18:1-3 (ESV)

> Now the full number of those who believed were of **one heart and soul**, and no one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they had everything in common. — Acts 4:32 (ESV)

## Conclusion

The biblical concept of oneness emphasizes unity of purpose rather than ontological identity. The [Greek context](#the-greek-context) of John 10:30 suggests a shared mission between the Father and the Son. Jesus' [response to his critics](#response-to-the-accusation-of-blasphemy) further clarifies that his oneness with God does not imply he is God himself. Furthermore, various [human examples](#humans-can-also-be-one-with-each-other) of being "one flesh," "one spirit," or "one soul" demonstrate that two distinct entities can be considered one without losing their individual identities.
