# One with the Father

Jesus told His disciples:

> I and the Father are one. -- John 10:30

Although the neuter word *hen* means “one”, but grammar alone does not define same essence. John uses the same phrase when Jesus prays that believers may be "one as he and the Father are one" (John 17:11,21-23).

> Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that **they may be one, even as we are one**. -- John 17:11 (ESV)

and

> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that **they may be one even as we are one**, I in them and you in me, that **they may become perfectly one**, so that the world may know that you sent me and **loved them even as you loved me**. -- John 17:23

Believers do not become one being. Jesus did not ask the Father to enable his disciples to join the [Godhead](../../godhead.md).

Trinitarians typically argue that John 10 concerns Jesus’ divinity because his opponents accuse him of making himself God:

> The Jews answered him, “It is not for a good work that we are going to stone you but for **blasphemy**, because **you, being a man, make yourself God**.” — John 10:33 (ESV)

However, John’s Gospel repeatedly portrays Jesus’ opponents as misunderstanding his words and making false accusations. They misunderstand his reference to “this temple” (John 2:19–22), falsely accuse him of having a demon (John 8:48–49), and charge him with wrongdoing despite Pilate repeatedly finding no guilt in him (John 18:30–38; 19:4–6). Their accusation in John 10:33, therefore, should not automatically be treated as an accurate interpretation of Jesus’ words.

Instead of affirming their accusation, Jesus answers it by citing Psalm 82:6:

> Jesus answered them, “Is it not written in your Law, ‘I said, you are gods’? If he called them gods to whom the word of God came⁠—and Scripture cannot be broken⁠— do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, ‘You are blaspheming,’ because I said, ‘**I am the Son of God**’? — John 10:34-36 (ESV)

Psalm 82 portrays God judging among the “gods,” rebuking them for unjust judgment, and declaring that they will “die like men” (Psalm 82:1–7). According to the interpretation that identifies these figures as human judges or rulers, they are called “gods” because they exercise authority delegated by God and not because they are equal to him.

Jesus’ reasoning is that if Scripture can call people who received God’s word “gods” without committing blasphemy, then his claim to be God’s sanctified and commissioned Son cannot, by itself, be condemned as blasphemy (John 10:34–36).

Nor does shared “oneness” language erase distinction between persons or beings. John 10:30 and 1 Corinthians 6:17 both use hen (“one”)

> But he who is joined to the Lord *(Jesus Christ)* **becomes one spirit** with him. -- 1 Corinthians 6:17 (ESV)

Believers become “one spirit” with Christ, yet do not become Christ himself. Likewise, believers can be “one heart and soul” without becoming one person. Therefore, people becoming one with God or with each other does not mean they are same being.

> Now the full number of those who believed were of **one heart and soul**, and no one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they had everything in common. -- Acts 4:32 (ESV)

## Conclusion

John 10:30 presents oneness with the Father as unity, not sameness of being. Jesus’ response to the charge of blasphemy identifies him as [the Son of God](../../index.md). Likewise, believers share analogous oneness with God and Christ without becoming gods.
