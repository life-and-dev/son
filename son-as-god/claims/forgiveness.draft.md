# Did Jesus Forgive Sin Against God?

Mark 2 records Jesus forgiving a paralysed man’s sins and validating his authority by healing him. The account raises whether this action identifies Jesus as the Father or whether the Father genuinely authorised his Messiah to administer forgiveness.

## Defining Forgiveness

The Old Testament first identifies the LORD—the God Jesus calls Father—as the source of pardon. The LORD promises:

> “For **I will forgive their iniquity**, and **I will remember their sin no more**.” — Jeremiah 31:34 (ESV)

Psalm 32 describes the same mercy as forgiveness, covering, and the refusal to count guilt against a person:

> “Blessed is the one whose **transgression is forgiven**, whose **sin is covered**. Blessed is the man against whom the LORD **counts no iniquity**, and in whose spirit there is no deceit.” — Psalm 32:1-2 (ESV)

Here, “forgive” means pardon or non-counting of guilt; announcement, atonement, and removal of consequences remain distinct. Forgiveness does not mean that the wrongdoing never happened.

In Mark 2:5, “forgiven” translates a form of the Greek verb *aphiēmi*. Depending on context, this verb may mean to release, leave, or forgive. [The lexical entry for *aphiēmi*](https://biblehub.com/greek/863.htm) gives this range, but the scene itself determines that remission of sins is in view. The word’s lexical range does not decide whether the authority exercised is underived or granted.

## Forgiveness Through Representatives

Scripture assigns different roles in forgiveness without treating every participant as equal. **Nathan announces the LORD’s pardon**, while priests mediate prescribed atonement. These precedents do not make their roles identical to Jesus’ authority, but they show that **the Father may deal with sin through appointed human representatives**.

### Nathan Announces Pardon

After David confesses his sin, Nathan replies:

> “The LORD also has **put away your sin**; you shall not die.” — 2 Samuel 12:13 (ESV)

Nathan speaks the LORD’s decision about an offence against the LORD. He is neither the injured party nor the source of mercy. **He appears to announce the LORD’s pardon as his prophet.** The consequence stated in the next verse nevertheless follows, confirming that forgiveness does not pretend the wrongdoing never happened or necessarily remove every consequence.

Nathan’s announcement is relevant but limited. He does **not claim personal authority to forgive** or validate such authority by a miracle. His role must therefore not be equated with Jesus’ role in Mark 2.

### Priests Mediate Atonement

Leviticus 4 concerns prescribed offerings for unintentional sins. Verse 35 states, “And the priest shall make **atonement** for him for the sin that he has committed, and **he shall be forgiven**” (ESV).

The priest carries out the appointed atoning rite, and forgiveness follows according to the LORD’s provision. This is mediation of atonement, not an independent priestly power to pardon any sin. It differs from both Nathan’s prophetic announcement and Jesus’ personal exercise of authority.

## Jesus’ Authority to Forgive

Jesus personally tells the paralysed man:

> “Son, your sins are **forgiven**.” — Mark 2:5 (ESV)

The scribes recognise this as a claim concerning the Father’s prerogative:

> “Why does this man speak like that? He is blaspheming! Who can **forgive sins but God alone**?” — Mark 2:7 (ESV)

Jesus does not retreat to Nathan’s kind of announcement. He claims that the Son of Man possesses the relevant authority:

> “But that you may know that the Son of Man has **authority on earth to forgive sins**” — Mark 2:10 (ESV)

He then commands the man to rise, take up his bed, and go home. The immediate healing publicly validates his claim (Mark 2:11-12). **Jesus personally exercises authority**, identifies himself as [the Son of Man](../../son-of-man.md) who has it, and **supplies a miracle as confirmation**. The miracle confirms his authority, though a miracle does not by itself settle the bearer’s identity. See [Jesus Did Miracles](../jesus-did-miracles.draft.md#did-jesus-miracles-prove-he-is-god).

The scene strongly invites a divine-prerogative reading. That reading entails divine identity only if God could not grant this authority to his Messiah. Matthew 9:8 and Acts 5:30-31 provide contrary evidence to that exclusion.

Matthew’s parallel identifies the authority as given:

> “When the crowds saw it, they were afraid, and they glorified God, who had **given such authority to men**.” — Matthew 9:8 (ESV)

The verse presents authority as given to human recipients, but by itself does not settle whether Jesus has divine identity. The crowd is not said explicitly to deny his deity.

Post-resurrection testimony makes the source and purpose of Jesus’ position explicit:

> “The God of our fathers **raised Jesus**, whom you killed by hanging him on a tree. God **exalted him at his right hand as Leader and Savior**, **to give repentance to Israel and to grant forgiveness of sins**.” — Acts 5:30-31 (ESV)

Acts does not describe the chronology of Jesus’ original commissioning in Mark. It directly states that the Father raised and exalted Jesus to give repentance and forgiveness. His authority is genuine precisely as appointed authority, not a mere verbal report with no power behind it.

## Does Authority Require Divine Identity?

The central distinction is between genuine, received, and underived authority.

* **Genuine authority** means that commands and decisions are valid and effective.
* **Received, or derived, authority** means that another authorised the bearer to exercise it.
* **Underived, or inherent, authority** originates in the bearer rather than being conferred by another.

Mark establishes that Jesus’ authority is genuine, but it does not by itself establish that his authority is underived.

Genesis 41:39-44 gives a concrete illustration of this distinction. Pharaoh places Joseph over his house and all Egypt, and the people must obey Joseph. Joseph’s authority is real and effective, but Pharaoh conferred it. Joseph does not thereby become Pharaoh or the source of royal authority. Joseph illustrates received authority only.

The Trinitarian inference may be stated:

1. Sins are ultimately offences against the Father, who alone is their final judge.
2. Jesus personally forgives sins and proves that he has authority to do so.
3. Therefore, Jesus possesses divine identity.

The conclusion follows only if the second premise also means that *Jesus’ authority cannot be granted*. That **additional claim is not established** by Mark 2. John 20:21-23 also demonstrates that forgiveness language can operate through a commission.

> “Peace be with you. As the Father has **sent me**, even so **I am sending you**.” — John 20:21 (ESV)
>
> “If you **forgive the sins of any**, they are forgiven them; if you **withhold forgiveness** from any, it is withheld.” — John 20:23 (ESV)

John 20 shows **commissioned people using forgiveness language with real authority**, but it does not equate the disciples with Jesus.

This evidence answers how Jesus may forgive sins against the Father without being the injured party. The Father, against whom sin is ultimately committed, may authorise his Messiah to **administer His pardon**. Mark 2 does not identify Jesus as the injured party. It presents him administering forgiveness for sins before the Father. He exercises the authority of his messianic office over sins before the Father.

## Does Delegated Authority Validate Priest-Declared Forgiveness?

Roman Catholics regard a priest’s sacramental declaration of forgiveness—called “absolution”—as valid by reading John 20:21-23 with the keys in Matthew 16:19 and the authority to bind and loose in Matthew 18:18. Through apostolic succession, bishops and ordained priests are understood to continue this ministry, with priests acting *in persona Christi* under their bishops. The priest therefore serves Christ in the sacrament rather than originating forgiveness. The [Catechism’s account of the minister of penance](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_two/section_two/chapter_two/article_4/viii_the_minister_of_this_sacrament.html) presents this view in §§1461, 1465-1466.

The disputed step is the extension of genuine apostolic commissioning into an exclusive Roman sacramental office inherited through succession, a requirement of individual confession to a priest for grave post-baptismal sin, and effective priest-declared forgiveness. Scripture does not explicitly establish those claims. They are therefore disputed inferences from the commissioning texts, not dishonest inventions. The objection is not merely that priests are not God, but that this specific continuing office and authority require biblical establishment.

Absolution and indulgences are distinct. Absolution concerns forgiveness of guilt in the sacrament of penance. [Official Catholic teaching on indulgences](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_two/section_two/chapter_two/article_4/x_indulgences.html) concerns temporal punishment after guilt has already been forgiven.

Luther’s 1517 objection focused on the indulgence system and its abuses rather than simply rejecting every declaration of forgiveness. His [*Ninety-five Theses* 5-7](https://www.projectwittenberg.org/pub/resources/text/wittenberg/luther/web/ninetyfive.html) insisted that the pope declares God’s remission rather than originating the removal of guilt. Thesis 6 states in part:

> “The pope cannot remit any guilt, except by declaring and confirming that it has been remitted by God; or, to be sure, by remitting cases reserved to his judgment. . . .” — Martin Luther, *Ninety-five Theses*, thesis 6

Scripture explicitly appoints and authenticates Jesus. He acts according to the Father’s will rather than independently (John 5:30). God attests him through signs (Acts 2:22), raises and exalts him to grant repentance and forgiveness (Acts 5:30-31), and anoints him with the Holy Spirit and power (Acts 10:38). Jesus claims authority to forgive and confirms that claim by healing the paralysed man (Mark 2:10-12). The Father is the source of pardon, Jesus is the appointed agent, and forgiveness is proclaimed through Jesus’ name (Acts 10:43).

Scripture joins that gospel promise to repentance and faith (Luke 24:47; Acts 13:38-39). It also presents confession to God (Psalm 32:5; 1 John 1:9) and mutual confession with prayer among believers (James 5:16). No spoken formula makes unrepentant sin forgiven.

Christians today may announce the gospel promise, reassure a repentant person of God’s pardon through Jesus, and forgive personal wrongs. They do not independently create pardon or need a Roman priestly formula to make God forgive.

## Conclusion

[Forgiveness is the Father’s pardon or non-counting of guilt](#defining-forgiveness), while [Nathan and the priests illustrate distinct representative roles without equating those roles with Jesus](#forgiveness-through-representatives). [Jesus personally exercises genuine authority and validates it by healing](#jesus-authority-to-forgive), but [Scripture also identifies that authority as granted rather than necessarily underived](#does-authority-require-divine-identity). [That principle leaves Roman Catholic priest-declared forgiveness as a separate, disputed claim requiring evidence of a continuing sacramental office](#does-delegated-authority-validate-priest-declared-forgiveness). Jesus forgiving sins therefore does not by itself require that Jesus is God, although granted authority alone does not prove that he cannot be divine.
