# Did Jesus Forgive Sin Against God?

Mark 2 shows Jesus exercising authority to forgive that is real and effective. On an underived reading, this authority is inherent in Jesus rather than conferred, which supports a divine-identity argument because forgiveness is the Father’s prerogative. On a received reading, the Father genuinely grants authority that Jesus personally exercises under his commission, which challenges the use of Mark 2 as proof of divine identity. This article tests both readings before considering what authority Christians and church leaders have today.

## Defining Forgiveness and Authority

Several related acts must be distinguished:

* **God’s judicial pardon** means that the Father forgives guilt and does not count it against a person.
* **Interpersonal forgiveness** means that an injured person releases another from a personal debt or offence.
* **Announcement or assurance** reports God’s promise of pardon without necessarily causing it.
* **Community discipline and restoration** concern a person’s standing and fellowship within a church.
* **Catholic sacramental absolution** is the ministerial forgiveness that Catholic teaching assigns to an ordained priest in the sacrament of penance.

Authority also requires three distinctions:

* **Genuine authority** is real and effective.
* **Received authority** is real authority granted by the Father and personally exercised under his commission.
* **Underived authority** is inherently possessed rather than conferred.

Personal exercise proves that the authority is genuine, but it does not by itself identify the authority’s source. In Mark 2:5, “forgiven” translates a form of the Greek verb *aphiēmi*, which can mean to release, leave, or forgive according to context. [The lexical entry for *aphiēmi*](https://biblehub.com/greek/863.htm) gives this range. The scene establishes that remission of sins is in view, but the word’s range does not decide whether the authority is underived or received.

## Old Testament Source and Representatives

Jeremiah 31 and Psalm 32 identify the LORD, the God Jesus calls Father, as the source of pardon:

> “For **I will forgive their iniquity**, and **I will remember their sin no more**.” — Jeremiah 31:34 (ESV)

> “Blessed is the one whose **transgression is forgiven**, whose **sin is covered**. Blessed is the man against whom the LORD **counts no iniquity**, and in whose spirit there is no deceit.” — Psalm 32:1-2 (ESV)

These passages describe forgiveness as pardon, covering, and non-counting of guilt. They establish the LORD as the source without excluding every representative role. Nathan reports the LORD’s decision, and priests perform appointed atonement. Their roles are real, but their limits must be tested before they are compared with Jesus’ authority.

### Nathan Announces Pardon

After David confesses his sin, Nathan replies:

> “The LORD also has **put away your sin**; you shall not die.” — 2 Samuel 12:13 (ESV)

Nathan gives a real prophetic assurance about an offence against the LORD. He is neither the injured party nor the source of mercy, so the context supports reporting the LORD’s pardon rather than independently causing it. The consequence stated in the next verse still follows, showing that pardon does not necessarily remove every consequence.

Nathan establishes that an authorised representative may announce pardon. He does not claim personal authority to forgive or validate authority equivalent to Jesus’ claim in Mark 2.

### Priests Mediate Atonement

Leviticus 4 concerns prescribed offerings for unintentional sins. Verse 35 states, “And the priest shall make **atonement** for him for the sin that he has committed, and **he shall be forgiven**” (ESV).

The priest performs a real, appointed act within the LORD’s provision, and forgiveness follows. This supports agency in a process related to pardon, but it does not give the priest independent discretion to pardon any sin. The role differs from Nathan’s announcement and from Jesus’ direct declaration in Mark 2.

## Strongest Underived-Authority Case

### Case for Underived Authority

Jesus personally tells the paralysed man:

> “Son, your sins are **forgiven**.” — Mark 2:5 (ESV)

The scribes understand the declaration as pardon before God rather than forgiveness of a personal offence against Jesus:

> “Why does this man speak like that? He is blaspheming! Who can **forgive sins but God alone**?” — Mark 2:7 (ESV)

Jesus does not explicitly reject their premise that forgiveness is God’s prerogative. Instead, he claims the relevant authority for the Son of Man:

> “But that you may know that the Son of Man has **authority on earth to forgive sins**” — Mark 2:10 (ESV)

Jesus then commands the man to rise, take up his bed, and go home. The immediate healing validates the declaration and the claimed authority (Mark 2:11-12). Within this scene, Jesus speaks directly, does not appeal explicitly to the Father, invokes the Son of Man title, and answers a divine-prerogative objection with a [miracle](../jesus-did-miracles.draft.md#did-jesus-miracles-prove-he-is-god). This combination gives the underived and divine-identity reading substantial contextual force.

### Challenge to Underived Authority

The scribes raise the objection, but Mark does not identify their reasoning as the narrator’s final explanation of Jesus’ identity. Jesus proves that he has authority, not that its source is underived. Personal action is compatible with commissioned authority, and Scripture elsewhere describes God authenticating agents through miracles. Acts 2:22, for example, describes signs “that God **did through him**” (ESV).

Mark’s silence about the Father within this episode cannot establish that no commission exists. The scene therefore strongly establishes genuine authority and gives the underived reading contextual support, but it does not distinguish inherent authority from commissioned authority.

## Strongest Received-Authority Case

### Case for Received Authority

Daniel’s Son of Man receives rather than originates his royal authority:

> “And to him was **given dominion and glory and a kingdom**, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him.” — Daniel 7:14 (ESV)

This background matters because Jesus claims authority as the Son of Man in Mark 2. Matthew’s parallel also records that the crowd “glorified God, who had **given such authority to men**” (Matthew 9:8, ESV). The plural “men” and its referent are broad or ambiguous, and the statement reports the crowd’s response rather than an explicit narrator explanation. It therefore gives moderate, not decisive, support to received authority.

John 5:19-30 is more explicit about source. The Son “can do nothing of his own accord” (verse 19), the Father “has **given all judgment to the Son**” (verse 22), has “**granted the Son also to have life in himself**” (verse 26), and “has **given him authority to execute judgment**” (verse 27, ESV). Jesus again says, “I can do nothing on my own” (verse 30, ESV).

John also presents Jesus’ teaching as belonging to the one who sent him (John 7:16-18), his command as given by the Father (John 12:49-50), and his authority over all flesh as given by the Father (John 17:2).

This pattern extends into Jesus’ earthly ministry. Acts 2:22 says that God did mighty works, wonders, and signs “**through him**” (ESV). Acts 10:38 says that “God **anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power**” and that “God was with him” (ESV).

Acts 5:30-31 gives the most direct statement about Jesus’ forgiveness role:

> “The God of our fathers **raised Jesus**, whom you killed by hanging him on a tree. God **exalted him at his right hand as Leader and Savior**, **to give repentance to Israel and forgiveness of sins**.” — Acts 5:30-31 (ESV)

This text directly assigns the exalted Jesus a God-given role in granting repentance and forgiveness. Because it describes exaltation after the resurrection, it does not by itself reconstruct the chronology or mechanism of Mark 2.

The risen Jesus likewise says, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been **given to me**” (Matthew 28:18, ESV), and Acts 10:42 calls him the one “**appointed by God to be judge** of the living and the dead” (ESV). These post-resurrection texts strengthen the received-authority framework but do not automatically explain the earlier event.

### Challenge to Received Authority

No statement of a grant appears within Mark 2. Its direct pronouncement, “God alone” objection, Son of Man claim, and validating miracle remain weighty evidence for the underived reading. A Trinitarian interpretation can also understand authority as given to Jesus in his incarnate role without denying that he possesses divine identity.

The received-authority case is therefore cumulative. Its broader texts explicitly describe the source of Jesus’ teaching, works, judgement, dominion, and forgiveness role, but interpreting those statements as excluding inherent divine authority would go beyond what they directly say.

## Direct Comparison

| Issue                 | Underived reading                                                  | Received reading                                                    | Evidence assessment                                  |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Mark 2 declaration    | Direct pardon, no explicit appeal to the Father                    | A commissioned agent may speak and act personally                   | Proves exercise, not origin                          |
| “God alone” objection | Jesus answers the objection by claiming authority and healing      | The scribes’ premise is not identified as the narrator’s conclusion | Gives underived reading contextual force, not proof  |
| Son of Man framework  | Title may support divine identity                                  | Daniel 7 says the Son of Man receives dominion                      | Explicitly favours received authority                |
| Broader ministry      | Grants can describe Jesus’ incarnate role without denying divinity | John 5 and related texts repeatedly identify the Father as source   | Stronger explicit support for received authority     |
| Forgiveness role      | Mark 2 presents direct authority during earthly ministry           | Acts 5:30-31 assigns the exalted Jesus a God-given forgiveness role | Direct support, limited by post-resurrection setting |

*Assessment: received authority is better supported overall, with moderate to high confidence, because the broader testimony repeatedly identifies the Father as the source of Jesus’ authority, words, works, and forgiveness role. Mark 2 establishes real authority but leaves its source implicit.* This conclusion challenges Mark 2 as sufficient proof of underived divine identity. It does not prove that Jesus cannot be divine.

## John 20 and Commissioned Forgiveness

John 20:21-23 joins forgiveness language to sending and the Spirit:

> “Peace be with you. As the Father has **sent me**, even so **I am sending you**.” — John 20:21 (ESV)
>
> “If you **forgive the sins of any**, they are forgiven them; if you **withhold forgiveness** from any, it is withheld.” — John 20:23 (ESV)

The Greek uses ordinary forgive or release vocabulary (*aphiēmi*) and hold or retain vocabulary (*krateō*). The perfect passive forms describe the resulting status of sins as forgiven or retained, but they do not determine the cause or mechanism. Grammar alone does not show whether the disciples cause, announce, recognise, or mediate that status.

No recoverable original Aramaic wording survives. The Syriac Peshitta is a translation. Its ܫܒܩ (*šbaq*) root also appears in interpersonal-forgiveness contexts, so it cannot settle the authority question. See the [Dukhrana linguistic analysis of John 20:23](https://www.dukhrana.com/peshitta/analyze_verse.php?lang=en&verse=John+20:23&source=ubs).

Personal forgiveness remains linguistically possible. However, the surrounding sending, Spirit, and retention language indicates more than an ordinary private feeling. Several models therefore require testing:

* **Gospel proclamation** connects forgiveness or retention with accepting or rejecting the message about Jesus.
* **Community discipline** connects the words with authoritative decisions about fellowship and restoration.
* **Recognition** treats the disciples as declaring a status God has determined.
* **Delegated mediation or absolution** treats their commissioned act as an effective means through which pardon is applied.

Each model explains part of the context, but no one model is grammatically required. John 20 supports a consequential commission without settling whether its exercise is proclamation, discipline, recognition, sacramental mediation, or some combination.

## Authority of Modern Christians

Modern Christians receive real authority and responsibilities, but these are not identical to Jesus’ authority in Mark 2.

### Personal Wrongs

Jesus commands believers to forgive personal debts and offences. The prayer and warning in Matthew 6:12,14-15 connect receiving mercy with forgiving others, while Matthew 18:21-35 rejects limits on such forgiveness. This is genuine interpersonal forgiveness by the injured person. It is not an independent act that erases guilt before the Father.

### Gospel Proclamation

Christians and apostolic witnesses announce the promise of forgiveness through Jesus. Acts 10:43 says that everyone who believes in him “receives **forgiveness of sins through his name**” (ESV). Acts 13:38-39 likewise proclaims forgiveness and justification through Jesus. In 2 Corinthians 5:18-20, God entrusts the message of reconciliation to Christ’s ambassadors. The messenger announces and appeals on the basis of God’s act rather than becoming the source of judicial pardon.

### Discipline and Restoration

Matthew 18:15-20 authorises communal action concerning an unrepentant member and places binding and loosing in that context. First Corinthians 5 directs exclusion from fellowship in a serious case, while 2 Corinthians 2:5-11 directs forgiveness, comfort, and reaffirmed love when discipline has achieved its purpose. These are consequential decisions about fellowship, discipline, and restoration. They should not be equated automatically with erasing guilt before God.

### Withholding and Its Limits

John 20’s retention language does not license vindictive unforgiveness. Christians remain commanded to forgive personal wrongs. Relational restoration, trust, access, and church fellowship may await repentance, demonstrated change, and adequate safety, but God’s judicial pardon is not controlled by another person’s resentment.

Scripture therefore supports personal forgiveness, gospel proclamation, and community discipline as real but distinct responsibilities. It does not clearly grant modern Christians independent authority to remove judicial guilt before the Father.

## Roman Catholic Priests

Roman Catholic doctrine presents a connected account of priestly absolution:

1. God alone forgives sins, and Jesus exercises divine authority to forgive.
2. Jesus entrusts a forgiveness and retention commission to the apostles in John 20:21-23, read with the keys in Matthew 16:19 and binding and loosing in Matthew 18:18.
3. The apostolic ministry continues through bishops as successors to the apostles.
4. Priests collaborate with bishops through Holy Orders and exercise this ministry sacramentally.
5. In absolution, the priest acts as Christ’s minister rather than as an independent source of pardon.

The Catechism states the first two claims in §§1441-42. Its [account of the minister of penance](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_two/section_two/chapter_two/article_4/viii_the_minister_of_this_sacrament.html) identifies bishops and priests as ministers in §§1461-66 and describes the priest as a sign and instrument of God’s mercy.

The Catechism also teaches in [§1456](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4D.HTM) that confession to a priest is an essential part of the sacrament and requires known grave sins to be confessed.

This doctrine requires several premises beyond the direct wording of John 20:

* the apostolic commission is transferable beyond the apostles;
* bishops are its intended successors for this power;
* priests participate in it through ordination;
* forgiveness and retention refer to sacramental absolution rather than only proclamation, recognition, or discipline;
* the commission entails private priestly confession for grave sin; and
* Roman Catholic jurisdiction and succession validly preserve this authority.

Catholic teaching supports these premises through Scripture read with Tradition and the Church’s sacramental doctrine.

*Within that framework, the resulting system is coherent. Scripture alone, however, does not explicitly establish every link.*

The claim that ordained priests continue this authority through sacramental absolution is therefore not demonstrated by John 20, the keys, and binding and loosing without those additional Catholic premises.

## Conclusion

[Received authority is better supported by the explicit broader testimony](#direct-comparison), and Mark 2 alone cannot prove underived divine identity. 

[Modern Christians have real authority to forgive personal wrongs, proclaim the gospel promise, and administer community discipline](#authority-of-modern-christians), but not clearly to remove judicial guilt independently before the Father.

[Roman Catholic priestly absolution forms a coherent Scripture-plus-Tradition system](#roman-catholic-priests), but its succession, priesthood, sacramental, confessional, and jurisdictional premises extend beyond what Scripture explicitly establishes.
