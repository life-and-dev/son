# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The relationship between Jesus and the glory of God has long been a subject of
theological inquiry. While the Old Testament emphasizes that God does not share
His glory with others, the New Testament frequently speaks of Jesus being
glorified. Exploring these passages reveals how both statements can be true
within their respective contexts. It also invites readers to consider what this
implies about the identity of Christ.

## Does Jesus Contradict Isaiah?

A common point of tension arises when comparing the declarations of the LORD in
Isaiah with the statements made by Jesus.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament), that is My name;
> And **My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**,
> nor My [praise](../../nature.md) to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus spoke of his own glorification on several occasions.

> And Jesus answered them, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified.” — John 12:23 (ESV)
>
> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and **I am
> [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory)** in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

[Out of context](../../nature.md#concerns), it might seem like Jesus contradicts
the words of Isaiah. Some argue this proves that Jesus is [the
LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament). This argument is often supported
by [the confusion caused by removing God's name](../../nature.md#bible-translations)
from many translations. In those versions, the name of God is replaced with "the
LORD." This is the same title often used for Jesus.

## The Context of God's Glory

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the primary issue is idolatry. God does not want
to share His glory with foreign idols or "carved images." However, as the Father
of Jesus, God has no problem with people glorifying His Son. Glorifying the Son
automatically glorifies the Father as well.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

In the Septuagint (LXX) translation of Isaiah 42:8, the Greek word for "another"
is *heteros*. This refers to another of a different kind, such as an idol. In
contrast, the New Testament often uses *allos* when referring to another of the
same kind. This distinction suggests that while God will not give His glory to a
different kind of being like a false god, He can share it with His Son.

## Glorifying the Son Glorifies the Father

A human father is often honored when his son is praised. This praise reflects
well on the father for how he raised his child. Similarly, honoring Jesus is a
way to honor God.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

In the context of John 17, Jesus prayed for this mutual glorification.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify
> you**, since [you have given him authority over all
> flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance), [to give eternal life to all
> whom you have given him](../similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father)."
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

Giving glory to Jesus serves to glorify God the Father. The two are not in
competition.

## Distinction Between the Giver and Receiver of Glory

Jesus himself stated that glorifying oneself is not the goal. He pointed to the
Father as the source of his glory.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me**, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’" — John 8:54 (ESV)

The fact that Jesus is glorified by the Father indicates that [Jesus is distinct
from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). One person is giving the
glory, and the other is receiving it. This distinction is central to
understanding the relationship described in the Gospels.

## Critique

Critics often argue that because Jesus possesses the glory of God, he must be
God. They cite John 17:5 where Jesus asks for the glory he had with the Father
before the world existed. Trinitarians believe this proves an eternal, shared
essence.

However, others point out that glory is something "given" to Jesus. Daniel 7:14
states that dominion and glory were given to the Son of Man. If Jesus were the
source of all glory, it would not need to be bestowed upon him. Furthermore, the
use of *heteros* in the Septuagint supports the idea that God excludes idols
from His glory. This exclusion does not necessarily apply to His chosen
representative or His Son.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the exclusivity of God's glory
described in Isaiah. Instead, it demonstrates a relationship where [honoring the
Son](#glorifying-the-son-glorifies-the-father) ultimately [brings glory to the
Father](#glorifying-the-son-glorifies-the-father). The New Testament highlights
a [clear distinction](#distinction-between-the-giver-and-receiver-of-glory)
between the God who gives glory and the Son who receives it. This harmony
allows for both the [rejection of idols](#the-context-of-gods-glory) and the
[exaltation of Christ](#does-jesus-contradict-isaiah).
