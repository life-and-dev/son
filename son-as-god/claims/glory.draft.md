# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The concept of glory is central to biblical theology. It represents the weight,
honor, and brilliance of God. A perceived tension exists between Old Testament
prohibitions against sharing divine glory and New Testament descriptions of
Jesus being glorified. Exploring these passages reveals how the relationship
between the Father and the Son preserves the uniqueness of God while honoring
the one he sent.

## The Prohibition in Isaiah

In the book of Isaiah, God declares his name and his exclusive right to receive
glory and praise. This statement is often used to argue that God does not share
his divine attributes or honors with any other being.

> I am the LORD, that is My name; and My glory I will not give to another, nor
> My praise to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

The term "the LORD" in this passage refers to YHVH, the personal name of God.
You can find more about this in the article about [God in the Old Testament](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament).
Some readers interpret this verse as an absolute ban on any other being
receiving glory. This leads to the conclusion that if Jesus is glorified, he
must either be the same being as YHVH or be in contradiction with this
scripture.

## The Glorification of Jesus

Despite the declaration in Isaiah, the New Testament frequently speaks of Jesus
being glorified. Jesus himself spoke of an appointed time for this to occur.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

In his final prayer with his disciples, Jesus also noted that he had already
been glorified through his followers.

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

Without considering the context, these statements might appear to oppose the
message of Isaiah. Some observers suggest that this proves Jesus is YHVH. This
argument is often strengthened by the common practice of replacing the divine
name with "the LORD" in English translations. This can lead to [confusion](../../nature.md#bible-translations)
because both God and Jesus are addressed with this title.

## Distinguishing Idolatry From Divine Delegation

A closer look at the context of Isaiah 42:8 shows that the warning is directed
against "carved images" and foreign idols. God does not want his honor to be
attributed to inanimate objects or false deities. This is a different
situation than God choosing to honor his own Son.

Critiques often point to the word "another" in Isaiah 42:8 to claim that God
excludes every other person from receiving any form of glory. However, the
Septuagint uses the Greek word *heteros* for "another" in this verse. This
term specifically refers to another of a different kind, such as an idol. In
contrast, the word *allos* would refer to another of the same kind. This
distinction suggests that God does not share his glory with different kinds of
beings like idols. He may still share it with those who are of his own kind or
his own family.

The New Testament explains that the glorification of Jesus is not a
competition with God. Instead, it is a process that ultimately brings glory
back to the Father.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and
> glorify him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

A human father is often honored when his son is respected. This does not take
away from the father's own honor. It instead reflects well on his character and
his role as a parent. The Bible applies this logic to the relationship between
God and Jesus.

> That all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

## The Father as the Source of Glory

Jesus did not claim to be the original source of his own glory. He
consistently pointed to the Father as the one who bestowed honor upon him. This
highlights that [Jesus is distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md).

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> Who glorifies me, of Whom you say, ‘He is our God.’” — John 8:54 (ESV)

In his prayer in John 17, Jesus asked the Father to glorify him so that he
could in turn glorify the Father. He linked this glory to the [authority](../../son-of-man/distinct.md#jesus-have-less-authority-than-god)
and [tasks](../../son-as-god/similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father)
that God had given him.

> Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify You,
> since You have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to
> all whom You have given him. — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

This relationship of delegation shows that the glory Jesus receives is a gift
from God. It is not an independent claim to being the source of all glory.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the exclusive claims of God in
the Old Testament. The [prohibition in Isaiah](#the-prohibition-in-isaiah)
specifically targets the worship of idols and false gods. In contrast, the
[glorification of Jesus](#the-glorification-of-jesus) is a divine delegation
that honors the Son while simultaneously [glorifying the Father](#the-father-as-the-source-of-glory).
By [distinguishing between idolatry and delegation](#distinguishing-idolatry-from-divine-delegation),
one can see how both testaments work together to present a unified message
about honor and glory.
