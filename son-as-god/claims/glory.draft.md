# The Son Is Glorified

The Scriptures contain a profound statement about the exclusivity of God's
honor. Many readers find themselves puzzled when they encounter passages where
Jesus appears to receive or request glory. This exploration looks into the
harmony between the prophetic declarations of the Old Testament and the
experiences of the Son of Man.

## The Exclusivity of God's Glory

The prophet Isaiah recorded a firm declaration from God regarding His unique
status and the honor due to Him.

> I am the LORD, that is My name;
> And My glory I will not give to another, nor My praise to carved images.
>
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

This verse establishes that [the LORD][shema-yhvh] does not share His glory with
others. However, the New Testament records several instances where Jesus is
described as being glorified.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

Later, Jesus affirmed this status during His prayer to the Father.

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

At first glance, these statements might seem to contradict the declaration in
Isaiah. Some interpret this as proof that Jesus must be [the LORD][shema-yhvh].
This argument often stems from confusion caused by the removal of God's name
from many translations. The title "the LORD" is used for both the Almighty and
for Jesus, which can lead to a blending of their identities.

## Distinguishing Idols From the Son

The context of Isaiah 42:8 is crucial for understanding the nature of this
glory. The primary focus of the passage is God's refusal to share His honor with
foreign idols or carved images. These are inanimate objects that represent a
different kind of being altogether.

The Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Septuagint, provides further
clarity. In Isaiah 42:8, the word used for "another" is *heteros*. This term
typically refers to another of a different kind. This implies that God will not
give His glory to idols, which are entities entirely distinct and separate from
His nature.

In contrast, the New Testament often uses the word *allos* when referring to
another of the same kind. As the Father of Jesus, God has no conflict in
glorifying His Son. The glorification of the Son does not compete with the
Father. Instead, it serves to honor the Father who sent Him.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

## The Relationship of Father and Son

The relationship between a father and a child provides a helpful parallel. A
human father is not diminished when his son is honored. On the contrary, the
success and honor of the son often bring greater glory to the father. It
reflects well on the character and leadership of the parent.

> ... that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does
> not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him.
> — John 5:23 (ESV)

Jesus consistently pointed back to the Father as the source of His authority
and honor. He prayed for glory specifically so that the Father would be
glorified in return.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify You,
> since You have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to
> all whom You have given him. — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

The honor given to Jesus is an extension of the honor due to God. If someone
gives glory to Jesus, they are ultimately glorifying the Father who empowered
Him.

## Glory Received Not Taken

Jesus made it clear that He did not seek honor for Himself through His own
efforts. He recognized that self-glorification is empty and meaningless.

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> Who glorifies me, of Whom you say, ‘He is our God.’ — John 8:54 (ESV)

For the Father to glorify the Son, there must be a distinction between them.
Jesus has to be [distinct from God the Father][distinct] to receive glory from
Him. This dynamic reinforces the idea that their relationship is one of shared
purpose rather than a struggle for exclusive status.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the exclusivity of God's honor.
The prohibition in Isaiah 42:8 focuses on [distinguishing idols from the
Son](#distinguishing-idols-from-the-son), ensuring that glory is not given to
false gods. Instead, the [relationship of Father and Son](#the-relationship-of-father-and-son)
demonstrates that honoring the Son ultimately serves to glorify the Father.
Because Jesus [receives glory rather than taking it](#glory-received-not-taken)
for Himself, His honor remains perfectly aligned with the unique status of the
one true God.
