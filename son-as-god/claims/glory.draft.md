# Does God Share His Glory With Jesus?

The Old Testament clearly states that God will not share His glory with anyone
else. However, Jesus frequently spoke about being glorified by God and sharing
in His honor. This seeming contradiction leads many to question the relationship
between the Father and the Son. By examining the context and the original
language, one can find a harmony between these two perspectives.

## The Contrast Between Isaiah and Jesus

> "I am the LORD, that is My name; and My glory I will not give to another, nor
> My praise to graven images." — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Jesus, however, made several statements that seem to challenge this declaration.
For example, he spoke of his impending honor during his ministry.

> "And Jesus answered them, 'The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified.'" — John 12:23 (ESV)

Later, in his prayer for his disciples, Jesus reaffirmed this state of being
honored.

> "All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them." — John
> 17:10 (ESV)

Taken out of context, these verses might seem to show Jesus contradicting the
word of the LORD in Isaiah. Some use this apparent conflict to argue that Jesus
must be the LORD himself. They suggest that if God shares glory only with
himself, then Jesus must be part of that same divine identity. This argument
often gains traction because modern Bible translations frequently replace God's
unique name with the title "the LORD." Since Jesus is also called "Lord," this
linguistic overlap can create confusion about their distinct identities. More
information on this topic is available in the discussion of how [Jesus
reinforced the belief in a single
God](../../shema.md#jesus-reinforced-the-jewish-belief-of-a-single-god).

## Glorifying the Son Glorifies the Father

A closer look at the context of Isaiah 42:8 reveals that the primary issue was
idolatry. The LORD declared he would not share his glory with foreign idols or
graven images. In contrast, the relationship between God and his Son is not
competitive. As the Father of Jesus, God has no objection to his Son being
honored. In fact, honoring the Son serves to honor the Father as well.

> "If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once." — John 13:32 (ESV)

This dynamic is similar to how a human father feels honored when his son is
respected. If a child reflects the good character and upbringing provided by the
parent, the praise given to the child naturally extends to the parent. Jesus
explicitly taught that this was the intended order of honor.

> "All may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father." — John 5:23 (ESV)

## The Source of Jesus' Glory

In the context of John 17, Jesus prayed for the glory that comes from God. He
did not claim this honor independently but asked for it to be bestowed upon him
so that he could, in turn, bring glory back to the Father.

> "When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> 'Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you,
> since you have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to all
> whom you have given him.'" — John 17:1,2 (ESV)

Jesus himself emphasized that self-glorification is meaningless. He pointed to
the Father as the true source of his honor.

> "Jesus answered, 'If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me, of whom you say, “He is our God.”'" — John 8:54 (ESV)

For the Father to glorify the Son, they must be distinct from each other. An
individual does not give something to themselves in the way described here. This
distinction is vital for understanding how Jesus can be glorified without
violating the principle set forth in Isaiah. One can find more evidence for this
in the discussion on how [Jesus is distinct from God the
Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md).

## A Linguistic Defense: Heteros vs Allos

Critics might still argue that "another" in Isaiah 42:8 is absolute and excludes
even a Son. However, the Septuagint uses the word *heteros* for "another" in this
verse. The Septuagint is the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament. In
Greek, *heteros* often refers to another of a different kind. In contrast, the
word *allos* refers to another of the same kind.

By using *heteros*, the text implies that God will not give his glory to
another of a different kind. This specifically refers to false idols and pagan
gods. Jesus is the Son who is of the same kind as his Father. Therefore, he
does not fall into the category of a *heteros* "other." This linguistic nuance
supports the idea that God can share his glory with his Son without
contradicting his own decree against idols. This view is supported by biblical
scholars who distinguish between divine essence and the objects of idolatry. For
more information on this perspective, one can consult the [Revised English
Version Commentary](https://www.revbible.com/comm/Isaiah/42/8).

## Conclusion

The conflict between God's refusal to share His glory and the honor given to
Jesus is resolved by examining the biblical context ([read
more](#the-contrast-between-isaiah-and-jesus)). Honoring the Son is actually a
way of honoring the Father rather than competing with Him ([see
evidence](#glorifying-the-son-glorifies-the-father)). Jesus receives his glory
from God rather than claiming it for himself, maintaining the distinction
between them ([further reading](#the-source-of-jesus-glory)). Linguistic
evidence also suggests that the prohibition on sharing glory specifically
applies to different kinds of beings, such as idols ([linguistic
defense](#a-linguistic-defense-heteros-vs-allos)).
