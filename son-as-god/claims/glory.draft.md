# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

Biblical texts often present statements that appear to contradict one another
at first glance. One notable example involves the concept of divine glory. In
the Old Testament, God declares His name and His refusal to share His glory
with anyone else. Conversely, the New Testament depicts Jesus speaking of his
own glorification. This article examines these passages to understand how
these claims coexist within the biblical narrative.

## The Apparent Contradiction

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament), that is My name;
> And **My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**,
> nor My [praise](../../nature.md) to carved images.
>
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus spoke of himself being glorified.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to
> be glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

Jesus also stated that he is glorified in his followers.

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and
> **I am [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory)** in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

Without considering the [context](../../nature.md#concerns), it might appear
that Jesus contradicts the statement in Isaiah. To resolve this perceived
contradiction, some argue that this serves as proof that Jesus is
[the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament). This argument is often
reinforced by the [confusion caused by removing God's name](../../nature.md#bible-translations)
from translations. In many versions, the name of God is replaced with
"the LORD," a title also applied to Jesus.

## Understanding the Biblical Context

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the primary concern is that God does not share
His glory with foreign idols or carved images. The Septuagint (LXX) version of
this verse uses the Greek word *heteros* for "another." This term often refers
to another of a different kind, such as an idol. In contrast, the word *allos*
is used for another of the same kind. This distinction suggests that while God
will not give His glory to a different kind of being like an idol, He may
share glory within the context of His relationship with His Son.

As the Father of Jesus, God may choose to glorify His Son. This act of
glorifying the Son also serves to glorify the Father.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and
> glorify him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

A human father is often honored when his son is praised. This praise reflects
well on how the father raised the child. Similarly, honoring the Son can be
seen as a way of honoring the Father.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father.
> — John 5:23 (ESV)

Both the statement in Isaiah and the claims of Jesus can be viewed as correct
within their respective contexts. In the prayer recorded in John 17, Jesus
requests glorification for the purpose of glorifying the Father.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and
> said, “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may
> glorify You**, since [You have given him authority over all
> flesh](../../son-of-man/distinct.md#jesus-have-less-authority-than-god),
> [to give eternal life to all whom You have given
> him](../../son-as-god/similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father)."
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

When glory is given to Jesus, it is ultimately directed toward God the Father.
Jesus emphasized that self-glorification is without value and that his glory
comes from the Father.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my
> Father Who glorifies me**, of Whom you say, ‘He is our God.’”
> — John 8:54 (ESV)

This dynamic suggests that
[Jesus is distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) in order
to be glorified by Him.

## Conclusion

The biblical [apparent contradiction](#the-apparent-contradiction) between
God's refusal to share His glory and Jesus' glorification is resolved by
[understanding the biblical context](#understanding-the-biblical-context).
The prohibition in Isaiah primarily targets false idols rather than the unique
relationship between the Father and the Son. Ultimately, the glorification of
Jesus serves to further glorify God the Father, maintaining a consistent
theological narrative throughout the scriptures.
