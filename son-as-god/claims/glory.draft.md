# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The relationship between the glory of the Father and the glory of the Son is a central theme in the New Testament. While God declares that he will not give his glory to another, Jesus speaks of being glorified and sharing in the Father's glory. This apparent tension invites a deeper exploration of how these statements harmonize within the biblical narrative.

## Isaiah Challenge

> I am the LORD, that is My name;
> And My glory I will not give to another, nor My praise to carved images.
>
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Jesus made several statements that seem to claim a share in this glory. For example, he spoke of his impending glorification:

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified. — John 12:23 (ESV)

He also spoke of a shared glory between himself and the Father:

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and I am glorified in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

## Context of Idolatry

When read without considering the broader context, it might seem like Jesus contradicts the words of the prophet Isaiah. This was a common [concern](../../nature.md#concerns) for those trying to understand the relationship between the Father and the Son. Some argue that this claim to glory proves Jesus is the LORD himself. This argument often relies on the [confusion](../../nature.md#bible-translations) caused by removing God's personal name from many Bible translations. When the name is replaced with the title "the LORD," which is also used for Jesus, it can blur the distinction between the two.

The context of Isaiah 42:8 reveals that the primary issue was God's refusal to share his glory with foreign idols and carved images. The Septuagint (LXX) uses the Greek word *heterō* (ἑτέρῳ) in this verse, which means "another of a different kind." This distinguishes it from *allos*, which means "another of the same kind." This linguistic choice suggests that God's refusal applies to ontologically different entities like idols, rather than the Son who shares the Father's nature.

As the Father of Jesus, God has no problem with the honor given to his Son. Any glory given to the Son ultimately brings glory back to the Father. Critics might argue that any sharing of glory violates the absolute monotheism expressed in Isaiah. However, the New Testament presentation shows a reciprocal relationship where the Son's glorification is the vehicle for the Father's own glory.

## Reciprocal Glorification

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

The relationship between the Father and the Son is not one of competition. A human father is honored when his son is respected, as it reflects well on the father's character and upbringing. Similarly, the Father supports and exalts the Son. This [act](../similarities/act.md) of shared honor ensures that the Father remains the ultimate source of all glory.

> That all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

## Evidence of Shared Glory

Both Isaiah and Jesus spoke truthfully within their respective contexts. In his prayer in John 17, Jesus asked for glorification so that he might, in turn, glorify the Father.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said, "Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify You, since you have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to all whom you have given him." — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

Any glory given to Jesus serves the purpose of magnifying God the Father. Jesus himself clarified that self-glorification is without value.

> Jesus answered, "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who glorifies me, of whom you say, 'He is our God.'" — John 8:54 (ESV)

This statement highlights that [Jesus is distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) from God the Father. He does not take glory for himself but receives it from the one who sent him.

## Conclusion

The biblical record demonstrates that God's glory is not compromised when he chooses to honor his Son. The [Isaiah challenge](#isaiah-challenge) is resolved when considering the [context of idolatry](#context-of-idolatry). The [reciprocal glorification](#reciprocal-glorification) between the Father and the Son ensures that all honor given to Jesus ultimately magnifies the Father. The [evidence of shared glory](#evidence-of-shared-glory) shows that Jesus remains distinct from the Father while acting as the perfect reflection of his glory.
