# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

How can Jesus claim to be glorified if God explicitly states that He will not
give His glory to another? This apparent contradiction between the words of the
prophets and the statements of Jesus has long intrigued theologians and
students of the Bible alike. Understanding the nature of this glory and to
whom it is directed reveals a deeper harmony within the scriptures that might
not be immediately obvious.

## The Prohibition of Glory

The prophet Isaiah recorded a clear statement from God regarding His unique
status and the glory that belongs to Him alone.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh), that is My name;
> And **My glory I will not give to another**, nor My praise to carved
> images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus made several statements that seem to indicate he also receives glory.

> "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified."
> — John 12:23 (ESV)

Jesus also stated:

> "All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and **I am glorified** in them."
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

When viewed out of context, it might seem like Jesus is contradicting the words
of Isaiah. Some use this apparent conflict to argue that Jesus must be
[the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh) himself. They suggest that if God does not
share His glory with anyone else, then Jesus receiving glory proves he is God.
This argument is often reinforced by the confusion caused when the divine name
is replaced with the title "the LORD" in many Bible translations.

However, a closer look at the original language in the Septuagint (the Greek
translation of the Old Testament) provides an important defense. The Greek
word used for "another" in Isaiah 42:8 is *heteros*. This word refers to
another of a different kind, as noted in the [NET Bible
notes](https://classic.netbible.org/bible/Isaiah+42).
In this context, God is refusing to share His glory with foreign idols and
carved images, which are of a completely different nature. This does not
preclude God from sharing glory with His Son, who is of the same kind.

## The Glorification of the Son

The context of Isaiah 42:8 focuses on God's refusal to share His praise with
foreign idols. As the Father of Jesus, God has no problem with the
glorification of His Son. When the Son is glorified, it automatically brings
glory to the Father as well.

> "If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and
> glorify him at once." — John 13:32 (ESV)

The relationship between the Father and the Son is not one of competition but
of mutual support. A human father is honored when his son is respected and
successful because it reflects well on the father's guidance and character.

## Shared Glory

Jesus prayed for this shared glory shortly before his crucifixion. He
recognized that his own glorification was inextricably linked to the glory
of His Father.

> "Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify
> you**, since [you have given him authority over all
> flesh](../../nature.md), [to give eternal life to all whom you have given
> him](../similarities/act.draft.md)." — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

In this framework, giving glory to Jesus is an act that honors God the Father.
This principle is explicitly stated in the Gospels.

> "All may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father." — John 5:23 (ESV)

Jesus also emphasized that true glory must come from God rather than from
oneself. He made it clear that he was not seeking his own glory independently.

> "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who glorifies
> me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’" — John 8:54 (ESV)

This statement highlights that [Jesus is distinct from God the
Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). If they were the same person, the act
of the Father glorifying the Son would lose its meaning. The distinction
allows for a genuine relationship where glory is shared and returned between
two distinct persons.

## Conclusion

The apparent contradiction between [the prohibition of
glory](#the-prohibition-of-glory) in Isaiah 42:8 and [the glorification of the
Son](#the-glorification-of-the-son) in the Gospels is resolved by
understanding the nature of this glory. In Isaiah, God is refusing to share
His glory with foreign idols and carved images. This does not prevent God
from sharing [shared glory](#shared-glory) with His Son, whose glorification
also honors the Father. The distinction between the two allows for a
relationship where glory is given and received, further confirming that they
are distinct persons.
