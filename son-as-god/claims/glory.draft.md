# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

A common argument for the divinity of Jesus Christ centers on the concept of
glory. If God declares that He will not give His glory to another, yet Jesus is
seen receiving and claiming glory, how can this be reconciled? This article
investigates the biblical relationship between the glory of the Father and the
glory of the Son to see if they are indeed in competition or if there is a
deeper, harmonious connection.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament), that is My name;
> And **My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**,
> nor My [praise](../../nature.md) to carved images.
>
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus said:

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

And:

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and **I am
> [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory)** in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

[Out of context](../../nature.md#concerns), it seems like Jesus contradicts
Isaiah. Some would argue this is proof that Jesus is [the
LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament) to solve this contradiction.
This argument is supported by [the confusion caused by removing God's
name](../../nature.md#bible-translations). The name is replaced with "the LORD"
which is also a title used for Jesus.

## Isaiah's Context

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the issue was that God did not want to share His
glory with foreign idols. He mentions "carved images" specifically. However, as
the Father of Jesus, He has no problem with anyone glorifying His Son. The Son
of God glorifies His Father in return.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

## Mutually Glorified

No parent should be competing with their children. They should instead support
their children. For example, a human father is also honored when someone honors
his son. This honor implies that the father raised his son well.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does
> not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

Therefore, both Isaiah and Jesus are correct. In the context of John 17, Jesus
prayed a few verses earlier:

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify
> You**, since [You have given him authority over all
> flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance), [to give eternal life to all whom
> You have given
> him](../../son-as-god/similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father)." — John
> 17:1-2 (ESV)

In other words, if someone gives glory to Jesus, it also glorifies God the
Father of Jesus.

## Septuagint Distinction

The Greek translation of the Old Testament, known as the Septuagint, provides
further clarity on Isaiah 42:8. In this version, the word used for "another" is
*heteros*. This term signifies another of a different kind. In the context of
Isaiah, God is refusing to give His glory to idols. These are entities of a
completely different nature than Him. [Scholarly
analysis](../../nature.md#bible-translations) of the Septuagint supports this
distinction between different types of "others" in the Greek language.

This is different from the word *allos*. That word refers to another of the
same kind. Jesus is the Son of God and is of the same kind as His Father.
Therefore, God sharing glory with His Son does not violate His decree. He is
not giving His glory to a "different kind" of being like an idol. Instead, He
is glorifying the one who perfectly reflects His own being.

## Critiques

Some might argue that any sharing of glory contradicts the absolute nature of
God's statement in Isaiah. They believe that if Jesus receives glory, he must
be the same person as the LORD. However, this view overlooks the relational
nature of the Father and the Son. Jesus frequently emphasizes that his glory is
not self-derived.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> Who glorifies me**, of Whom you say, ‘He is our God.’" — John 8:54 (ESV)

This distinction is crucial. If Jesus were the same person as the Father, the
act of one glorifying the other would be meaningless. The fact that the Father
glorifies the Son proves they are distinct individuals. The Son receives glory
from the Father to ultimately reflect that glory back to Him. This relationship
shows that [Jesus has to be distinct from God the
Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) to be glorified by Him.

## Conclusion

The apparent contradiction between Isaiah and the claims of Jesus is resolved
when examining the [historical context](#isaiahs-context) of the Old Testament.
God's refusal to share His glory was directed at [idols and carved
images](#septuagint-distinction) of a different kind, not His own Son. In fact,
the Father and Son are [mutually glorified](#mutually-glorified) when honor is
given to the one whom God sent. This dynamic requires that the Father and Son
remain [distinct individuals](#critiques) rather than being the same person.
