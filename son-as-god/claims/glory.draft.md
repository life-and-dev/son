# Jesus Claims to be Glorified

The concept of glory is central to the biblical understanding of God's character
and His relationship with humanity. Many readers find a challenging tension
between the Old Testament's declaration that God will not share His glory and
the New Testament's portrayal of Jesus being glorified. This article examines
the scriptural context of these statements to clarify how Jesus' glorification
harmonizes with the unique honor reserved for God the Father.

> “I am the LORD, that is My name; and My glory I will not give to another, nor
> My praise to carved images.” — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

## Jesus Also Claims Glory

Jesus also speaks of his own glorification.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified.” — John 12:23 (ESV)

He also affirms his status among his followers.

> “All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.” — John
> 17:10 (ESV)

When these statements are viewed in isolation, they might appear to contradict
the message in Isaiah. Some interpreters use this perceived contradiction to
argue that Jesus must be [the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh). This perspective is
often reinforced by the common practice of replacing God's distinct name with
the title "the LORD." Since Jesus is also called Lord, it can lead to confusion
between the two.

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the focus is on God's refusal to share His glory
with foreign idols and "carved images." The Septuagint (LXX), an ancient Greek
translation of the Old Testament, uses the word *heteros* for "another" in this
verse. In Greek, *heteros* typically refers to another of a different kind.
This suggests that God will not share His glory with idols, which are of a
completely different nature than Himself. However, as the Father of Jesus, God
has no problem with His Son being glorified. This glorification of the Son
naturally results in honor for the Father as well.

> “If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once.” — John 13:32 (ESV)

The relationship between a father and a child provides a helpful parallel. A
human father is often honored when his son receives recognition, reflecting
well on how the father raised him. In a similar way, honoring the Son is a way
of honoring the Father who sent him.

> “...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does
> not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him.” — John 5:23 (ESV)

## Mutual Glorification

The words of Jesus in John 17 provide further clarity on how this relationship
works. He prayed to the Father just before his arrest.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you,
> since [you have given him authority over all flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance),
> [to give eternal life to all whom you have given him](../similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father).”
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

When glory is given to Jesus, it ultimately serves to glorify God the Father
as well. Jesus also emphasized that true glory must come from the Father
rather than from oneself.

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who
> glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’” — John 8:54 (ESV)

This dynamic requires that [Jesus be distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md).
One person cannot glorify another if they are in fact the same individual.

## Conclusion

The claim that Jesus is glorified does not contradict the exclusive glory of
God the Father. Instead, it highlights their unique relationship. The
[glory of Jesus](#jesus-also-claims-glory) is not a competition with the
Father, but a reflection of it. By
[understanding the biblical context](#mutual-glorification) and the
distinction between God and idols, it becomes clear that honoring the Son is
the way to honor the Father.
