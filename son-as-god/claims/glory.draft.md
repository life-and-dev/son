# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The relationship between the glory of God and the glory of Jesus has long been
a subject of theological discussion. Some point to Old Testament passages where
God declares He will not share His glory with another. However, the New
Testament frequently speaks of Jesus being glorified. Understanding how these
statements coexist requires a closer look at the nature of their relationship.
The context of these declarations reveals a harmony rather than a
contradiction.

> I am the LORD; that is my name; my glory I give to no other, nor my praise to
> carved idols.  — Isaiah 42:8 (ESV)

Yet Jesus spoke of his own glorification on several occasions.

> And Jesus answered them, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified.”  — John 12:23 (ESV)
>
> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.  —
> John 17:10 (ESV)

## The Isaiah 42 8 Challenge

At first glance, it seems that Jesus contradicts the words of God in Isaiah.
Some argue this proves Jesus is the LORD because he receives glory that God
says He will not give to another. This argument often relies on the fact that
"the LORD" is used for both the Father and Jesus in many English translations.
One can explore this further in the discussion on the
[divine name](../../shema.md).

However, the context of Isaiah 42:8 reveals that God was speaking about foreign
idols and carved images. He was declaring His exclusivity over false gods. He
was not necessarily excluding His own Son from sharing in His honor.

## A Shared Glory

The Father has no conflict in glorifying His Son because glorifying the Son
ultimately brings glory to the Father. They are not in competition.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you,
> since you have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to
> all whom you have given him.”  — John 17:1,2 (ESV)
>
> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and
> glorify him at once.  — John 13:32 (ESV)

A human parallel can help illustrate this point. A father is honored when his
son is honored. The son's success and character reflect well on the father who
raised him. In a similar way, the honor given to Jesus reflects the glory of
God.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does
> not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him.  — John 5:23 (ESV)

In the Septuagint translation of Isaiah 42:8, the Greek word used for "another"
is [*heteros*](https://biblehub.com/greek/2087.htm). This term often implies
"another of a different kind." Since Jesus is the Son of God, he is of the same
kind as his Father. Therefore, God sharing glory with His Son does not violate
His decree not to give glory to *heteros*. This refers to idols or different
kinds of gods.

## The Source of Glory

Jesus himself clarified that he did not seek his own glory independently of the
Father. He recognized the Father as the source of his honor.

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’”  — John 8:54 (ESV)

Jesus must be [distinct](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) from God the Father to
be glorified by Him. This distinction is crucial for understanding how the
Father can give glory to the Son without contradicting His nature.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the exclusivity of God's glory
declared in [Isaiah 42 8](#the-isaiah-42-8-challenge). Instead, the New
Testament reveals a [shared glory](#a-shared-glory) where the Father honors the
Son and the Son honors the Father. Jesus is not a competing idol but the
[source of glory](#the-source-of-glory) who points back to the Father.
