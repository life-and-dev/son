# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The concept of glory in the Bible often raises questions about the unique status
of God. Many readers notice a tension between the Old Testament declaration that
God will not share His glory and the New Testament accounts of Jesus being
glorified. In a biblical context, to be glorified means to be honored, praised,
or revealed in one's true splendor and dignity. This article explores the
relationship between these statements and how they may be understood within
their biblical context.

## The Declaration of God's Uniqueness

In the Old Testament, the uniqueness of God is emphasized through His refusal to
share His glory with false deities. Isaiah recorded this firm boundary.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament), that is My name;
> And **My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**,
> Nor My [praise](../../nature.md) to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

This passage indicates that God does not allow His honor or splendor to be
attributed to idols or foreign gods. The term used for "another" in the
Septuagint (LXX) is *heterō*, which refers to another of a different kind. This
suggests that the prohibition specifically targets entities that are
fundamentally different from God, such as "carved images" mentioned in the same
verse. Scholars note that this distinction [implies that God may share His
glory](https://hermeneutics.stackexchange.com/questions/95415/if-the-lord-gives-his-glory-to-no-other-then-what-is-the-glory-that-jesus-gives)
with those of the same nature, such as His Son, while excluding idols.

## Jesus' Statements on Glory

Despite the declaration in Isaiah, the New Testament frequently describes Jesus
as being glorified or claiming glory.

> And Jesus answered them, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

Later, in a prayer to the Father, Jesus spoke of his relationship with those
given to him.

> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and **I am
> [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory)** in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

[Out of context](../../nature.md#concerns), these statements might seem to
contradict the message of Isaiah. Some interpreters argue that this overlap in
glory serves as proof that Jesus is [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament).
This view is often bolstered by [the confusion caused by removing God's
name](../../nature.md#bible-translations) in many translations. In these
versions, both the Father and Jesus are referred to as "the Lord."

## Resolving the Apparent Contradiction

The context of Isaiah 42:8 suggests that the primary issue is God's refusal to
share His glory with foreign idols. In contrast, the glorification of Jesus is
presented as an extension of the Father's own glory. Because Jesus is the Son of
God, honoring him is seen as honoring the Father who sent him. Jesus is
described as the perfect reflection of God's nature.

> He is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature,
> and he upholds the universe by the word of his power. — Hebrews 1:3 (ESV)

This dynamic can be compared to a human relationship. A father is often honored
through the success and integrity of his son. When a son is glorified, it
reflects positively on the father who raised him.

> that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. — John 5:23 (ESV)

If the glory given to Jesus ultimately points back to God, there is no
competition for honor. Therefore, both the Old Testament and the New Testament
can be seen as consistent.

## The Father's Role in Glorifying the Son

The source of Jesus' glory is consistently identified as the Father. Jesus did
not seize glory for himself. Instead, he received it through his obedience and
the Father's appointment.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

The apostles also witnessed this bestowal of honor.

> For when he received honor and glory from God the Father, and the voice was
> borne to him by the Majestic Glory, “This is my beloved Son, with whom I am
> well pleased,” — 2 Peter 1:17 (ESV)

Jesus expressed this dependence in his prayer. He requested that the Father
glorify him so that he might, in turn, glorify the Father.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify
> you**, since [you have given him authority over all
> flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance), [to give eternal life to all whom
> you have given him](../similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father).”
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

Jesus also emphasized that self-glorification is without value. He maintained
that his status was entirely dependent on the Father's testimony and actions.

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who
> glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’” — John 8:54 (ESV)

These statements indicate that [Jesus is distinct from God the
Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). For one to glorify the other, there must
be a distinction between the giver and the receiver of that glory.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus [does not contradict](#resolving-the-apparent-contradiction)
the declaration that God will not give His glory to another. This is because the
[prohibition in Isaiah](#the-declaration-of-gods-uniqueness) targets foreign
idols rather than the Son. Honoring Jesus [resolves the apparent
conflict](#resolving-the-apparent-contradiction) because it ultimately
[glorifies the Father](#resolving-the-apparent-contradiction). Furthermore, the
fact that the Father [bestows this glory](#the-fathers-role-in-glorifying-the-son)
highlights the [distinction between the
two](#the-fathers-role-in-glorifying-the-son).
