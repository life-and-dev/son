# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

Many readers find a puzzling tension between the Old Testament and the words
of Jesus regarding glory. In the book of Isaiah, God declares that He will
not give His glory to another. Yet, Jesus frequently spoke of his own
glorification and even asked the Father to glorify him. This has led many to
wonder if Jesus was claiming to be the same being as the LORD, or if there is
a deeper relational meaning behind these statements.

> I am the LORD, that is My name;
> And **My glory I will not give to another**, nor My praise to carved images.
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus said:

> And Jesus answered them, saying, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified.” — John 12:23 (ESV)

and

> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and **I am glorified** in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

## The Warning Against Idolatry

Out of context, it might appear that Jesus contradicts the statement in
Isaiah. Some argue this proves that Jesus is the LORD. This argument is
often supported by the confusion caused by removing God's name and replacing
it with "the LORD," which is a title also applied to Jesus.

Trinitarians often point to John 17:5, where Jesus asks for the glory he had
with the Father before the world existed. They reason that since God will not
give His glory to "another," Jesus must be that same God. However, the Greek
word for "another" in Isaiah 42:8 (LXX) is *heteros*. This term refers to
another of a different kind. Examples include a false god or a carved image.
It does not necessarily preclude God from sharing His glory with His own Son,
who is the "express image of His person" (Hebrews 1:3).

Furthermore, the context of Isaiah 42:8 shows that the primary issue was God's
refusal to share His glory with foreign idols. As the Father of Jesus, God
has no conflict with the glorification of His Son. When the Son is
glorified, that honor ultimately reflects back upon the Father who sent him.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and
> glorify him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

## The Source of Jesus' Glory

A human father is often glorified and honored when others show respect to
his son. This honor indicates that the father raised his son well. In a
similar way, God encourages the honoring of His Son.

> that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

Both Isaiah and Jesus are correct within their respective contexts. In John
17, Jesus prayed specifically for this mutual glorification.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and
> said, “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may
> glorify you, since you have given him authority over all flesh, to give
> eternal life to all whom you have given him.” — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

Giving glory to Jesus serves to glorify God the Father. Jesus himself
emphasized that it is useless to seek one's own glory independently.

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’ — John 8:54 (ESV)

## A Necessary Distinction

Because the Father is the one who glorifies the Son, [Jesus has to be
distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). One person
does not usually "glorify" themselves in the same sense that they receive
glory from another distinct party. This relationship confirms that the
glory Jesus receives is a gift and an appointment from the Father, rather
than an inherent claim to be the same being who spoke in Isaiah 42:8.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the exclusiveness of God's
glory described in Isaiah. Instead, it demonstrates a [harmonic
relationship](#the-source-of-jesus-glory) where the Father [glorifies the
Son](#the-source-of-jesus-glory) to ultimately [bring glory back to
Himself](#the-warning-against-idolatry). This distinction shows that Jesus
is the [authorized representative of God](../../son-as-god.md#the-son-as-god)
rather than an idol competing for worship.
