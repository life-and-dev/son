# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The concept of glory is central to the biblical narrative of God and His
relationship with His people. While the Hebrew Scriptures emphasize that God
alone is worthy of ultimate glory, the New Testament frequently speaks of Jesus
being glorified. This has led to intense theological debate: how can Jesus be
glorified if God declares He will not share His glory with another?

## The Apparent Contradiction

The starting point for this discussion is often a powerful declaration from the
prophet Isaiah.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh), that is My name;
> And **My [glory](../../index.md) I will not give to another**, nor My
> [praise](../../index.md) to carved images.
>
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus made statements that seem to claim this very glory. During his
ministry, he spoke of his impending glorification.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

In his high priestly prayer, Jesus also noted that he is glorified in his
followers.

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and **I am
> [glorified](../../index.md)** in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

[Out of context](../../index.md), it seems like Jesus contradicts Isaiah. To
solve this apparent contradiction, some argue that this proves Jesus is
[the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh). This argument is supported by the confusion
caused by replacing God's name with "the LORD". This is the same title often
used for Jesus.

Critics often point out that God is absolute in His statement: He will not give
His glory to *another*. They argue that if Jesus is not God, then God would be
violating His own word by glorifying him.

## Divine Delegation of Glory

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the primary issue was that God did not want to
share His glory with foreign idols or "carved images." However, as the Father
of Jesus, God has no problem with people glorifying His Son. The glorification
of the Son automatically results in the glorification of the Father.

The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) provides further
insight. In Isaiah 42:8, the word used for "another" is *heteros*. This term
often refers to another of a *different* kind. This implies that God will not
give His glory to idols, which are of a completely different nature than
Himself. Since Jesus is the Son of God, sharing the same "kind" of nature as his
Father, the prohibition against giving glory to a *heteros* (a different kind)
does not necessarily apply to him.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and
> glorify him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

The relationship can be compared to that of a parent and child. A parent is not
in competition with their child. Instead, they support their success. A human
father is honored when someone praises his son. This praise reflects well on the
father who raised him.

> That all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

Therefore, both Isaiah and Jesus can be correct. In John 17, Jesus prayed about
this mutual glorification.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify
> You**, since [You have given him authority over all
> flesh](../similarities/act.draft.md), [to give eternal life to all whom You
> have given him](../similarities/act.draft.md). — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

If someone gives glory to Jesus, it also glorifies God the Father. Jesus himself
stated that seeking his own glory would be useless.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me**, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’” — John 8:54 (ESV)

This implies that [Jesus is distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md).
He must be a separate person to be glorified by the Father.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not violate God's declaration in Isaiah.
Instead, it fulfills the Father's plan. By [honoring the Son](#divine-delegation-of-glory),
believers ultimately bring glory to the Father who sent him. This relationship
shows that Jesus is the [distinct representative](../../son-of-man/distinct.md)
of God. His glory is not stolen from God but is [given by the Father](#divine-delegation-of-glory)
to accomplish their shared purpose.
