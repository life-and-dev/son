# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The concept of glory in the Bible often leads to intense theological debate. In
the Old Testament, God is very clear about His unique status and His refusal to
share His glory with anyone else. However, in the New Testament, Jesus
frequently speaks about his own glory and being glorified by the Father. This
apparent tension between the two testaments requires a closer look at the
context and the relationship between God and His Son.

In Isaiah 42:8, God makes a definitive statement about His glory:

> I am the LORD, that is My name; And My glory I will not give to another, Nor
> My praise to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet, Jesus made several statements that seem to claim a share in this glory. For
example, he said:

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

Later, in a prayer to his Father, he stated:

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

## Understanding the Context of Isaiah

At first glance, it might seem that Jesus is contradicting the words of God in
Isaiah. Some argue that this proves Jesus is the LORD Himself. This argument is
often supported by the observation that God's name was replaced with "the LORD"
in many translations, which is the same title used for Jesus.

However, the context of Isaiah 42:8 specifically addresses the issue of
idolatry. God was declaring that He would not share His glory with foreign
idols or "carved images." The Hebrew word used for "another" in this verse is
*acher*, which refers to something different or strange.

## A Father Glorifying His Son

As the Father of Jesus, God does not view the glorification of His Son as
competition. Instead, when the Son is glorified, it ultimately brings glory to
the Father as well. Jesus explained this dynamic:

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

A human parallel can help illustrate this point. A father is not diminished
when his son is honored. Instead, the father feels a sense of pride and honor
because the success of the son reflects well on the father who raised him. This
is why the scriptures encourage the honoring of the Son:

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father.
> — John 5:23 (ESV)

Therefore, both Isaiah and Jesus are correct. In John 17, Jesus prayed:

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you,
> since you have given him authority over all flesh, [to give eternal life to
> all whom you have given him][act].”
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

When someone gives glory to Jesus, they are honoring the one whom God Himself
has chosen to glorify. This act does not take away from God's glory but rather
fulfills His purpose.

## The Source of Glory

Jesus was careful to point out that he did not seek glory for himself. He
recognized that his glory came directly from the Father:

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’ — John 8:54 (ESV)

This statement highlights that [Jesus is distinct from God the Father][dist].
If Jesus were the Father, he would be glorifying himself, which he explicitly
said would make his glory "nothing." Instead, his glory is valid because it is
bestowed upon him by another.

## Defending Against Critiques

Critiques of this view often point to the Septuagint (LXX) translation of
Isaiah 42:8. In the Greek text, the word for "another" is *heteros*, which means
another of a different kind. This supports the idea that God will not give His
glory to a different kind of being, such as an idol.

In contrast, when Jesus speaks of "another" Helper in John 14:16, the word used
is *allos*, meaning another of the same kind. Some argue that since the Son is
of the same kind as the Father, the prohibition in Isaiah does not apply to him.
While this reinforces the close bond between the Father and the Son, it does
not necessitate that they are the same individual. The Father can share His
glory with His Son because the Son acts in perfect unity with the Father's
will.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict God's declaration in Isaiah 42:8.
[God's refusal to share glory][context] was directed at idols, not His own Son.
Instead, the [Father chooses to glorify the Son][fgs] to bring glory to Himself.
Jesus also made it clear that his [glory comes from the Father][source] rather
than from himself, confirming their distinct roles.
