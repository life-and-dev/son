# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

In the Old Testament, the LORD declares that He will not share His glory with
another. However, the New Testament frequently speaks of Jesus Christ being
glorified, both by the Father and through his followers. This apparent tension
raises important questions about the nature of divine honor and the relationship
between God and His Son. By examining the context of these statements and the
original language used, we can gain a clearer understanding of how these
biblical truths coexist.

## The Prohibition of Sharing Glory

The foundational statement regarding God's exclusive glory is found in the
prophecy of Isaiah.

> I am the LORD, that is My name;
> And My glory I will not give to another, nor My praise to carved images.
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

This verse emphasizes that God does not share His unique status or worship with
idols or foreign gods. The term "another" in this context refers to something
fundamentally different from God, such as a carved image.

## Jesus Is Glorified

Despite the declaration in Isaiah, the New Testament records several instances
where Jesus speaks of his own glorification.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified."
> — John 12:23 (ESV)
>
> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

Some interpret these passages as proof that Jesus is the LORD. They argue that
if God will not give His glory to another, and Jesus receives glory, then Jesus
must be God. This view is often supported by the fact that many modern
translations use "the LORD" to refer to both the Father and the Son. This usage
can lead to confusion.

## Glory Through the Father

An alternative view suggests that Jesus' glorification does not contradict
Isaiah because it is God the Father who glorifies him. When the Son is
glorified, it ultimately brings honor back to the Father.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify
> him at once.
> — John 13:32 (ESV)

This relationship can be compared to a human parent and child. A father is
honored when his son is praised for his accomplishments. The praise given to the
son reflects well on the father who raised him.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him.
> — John 5:23 (ESV)

In his high priestly prayer, Jesus explicitly asks the Father to glorify him so
that he may, in turn, glorify the Father.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come;
> glorify your Son that the Son may glorify You, since You have given him
> authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to all whom You have given
> him.”
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

## Distinction in Glorification

Jesus himself clarified that seeking his own glory would be meaningless. His
status depends entirely on the honor bestowed upon him by the Father.

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who
> glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’”
> — John 8:54 (ESV)

This statement highlights a distinction between the one who gives glory and the
one who receives it. If Jesus were identical to the Father in every sense, the
act of one glorifying the other would lose its relational significance. This
concept is explored further in the context of
[Jesus' distinction from God](../../son-of-man/distinct.md).

## Diverse Perspectives on Divine Glory

Theological traditions offer different ways to reconcile these passages.

### The Trinitarian Perspective

Trinitarians often point to the Greek text of the Septuagint (LXX) for
Isaiah 42:8. The word used for "another" is *heterō*, which means "another of a
different kind." They argue that God will not give His glory to a different kind
of being, such as an idol. However, because they believe Jesus is of the same
essence (*ousia*) as the Father, sharing glory within the Godhead does not
violate this principle. In this view, Jesus is another of the same kind
(*allos*), not a different kind (*heteros*).

### The Unitarian Perspective

Unitarians emphasize that Jesus receives glory as a gift from God. They argue
that if Jesus were God by nature, he would already possess all glory and would
not need it bestowed upon him. They see the act of glorification as evidence of
Jesus' subordination and distinction from the Father. From this perspective,
Isaiah 42:8 remains a declaration that God will not share His supreme authority
with any other being. Jesus' glory is a derived honor given because of his
perfect obedience.

## Conclusion

The relationship between the exclusive glory of God and the glorification of
Jesus is a central theme in Christian theology. The prohibition in
[Isaiah 42:8](#the-prohibition-of-sharing-glory) primarily targets the worship
of idols. The New Testament reveals that [Jesus is glorified](#jesus-is-glorified)
through his relationship with the Father. This process is seen as
[glory through the Father](#glory-through-the-father), where honoring the Son
serves to honor God. Ultimately, the
[distinction in glorification](#distinction-in-glorification) points to a
relational dynamic between God and His Son, which can be interpreted through
[diverse perspectives](#diverse-perspectives-on-divine-glory) such as
Trinitarianism or Unitarianism.
