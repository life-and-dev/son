# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The Bible contains many statements that appear to contradict each other at first
glance. One of the most striking involves the concept of glory. In the Old
Testament, God declares that He will not give His glory to another. Yet, in the
Gospel of John, Jesus speaks frequently about his own glorification. He even
prays for the Father to glorify him. This raises an important question for
anyone seeking to understand the relationship between God and His Son. Does
Jesus claim for himself a status that God explicitly reserved for Himself alone?

## The Apparent Contradiction

The starting point for this discussion is a powerful declaration from the
prophet Isaiah. In this passage, God identifies Himself by His personal name.
He sets a clear boundary regarding His honor.

> I am the LORD, that is My name; And My glory I will not give to another, Nor
> My praise to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Some people use this verse to argue that Jesus must be the same being as the
LORD. They suggest that any other interpretation would force a contradiction
between Isaiah and the words of Jesus. For example, Jesus spoke of his impending
glorification during his earthly ministry.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

Later, in a prayer to his Father, Jesus stated that he already possessed glory
through his followers.

> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

## Understanding the Context of Isaiah

To resolve this apparent conflict, one must look at the specific context of
Isaiah 42:8. The passage is primarily concerned with idolatry. God is
contrasting Himself with "carved images" and "molded images" (Isaiah 42:17).
He declares that He will not share His unique position or receive praise
alongside lifeless idols.

The Greek translation of the Old Testament is called the Septuagint. It
provides further clarity. It uses the word *heteros* for "another." This word
refers to another of a different kind. This implies that God will not give His
glory to someone completely outside of His divine purpose. This includes false
gods or idols. It does not necessarily exclude someone who is of the same kind.
It also does not exclude one who acts as His perfect representative.

## The Relationship Between Father and Son

The New Testament describes a relationship where the Father and the Son are
not competing for glory. Instead, they are perfectly aligned. When the Son is
glorified, it serves to glorify the Father as well. There is no jealousy
between them. A human father is often honored when his son achieves greatness.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

Jesus explains that the goal of his own honor is the ultimate honor of God.
He does not seek a glory that is independent of the Father.

> Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you.
> — John 17:1 (ESV)

This shared honor is intended to extend even further. Jesus prayed that his
disciples would also be brought into this circle of honor and unity.

> ... that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father.
> — John 5:23 (ESV)

## Distinction and Dependence

A crucial point in this discussion is that Jesus does not glorify himself.
He is dependent on the Father for the honor he receives. If Jesus were the
same being as God, the concept of one person glorifying the other would lose
its relational meaning.

> Jesus answered, "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me, of whom you say, 'He is our God.'" — John 8:54 (ESV)

For the Father to glorify the Son, there must be a [clear distinction
between them][1]. The Son receives authority and glory because it has been
granted to him by the Father. This delegated glory does not rob God of His
status. It demonstrates His power working through His chosen representative.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the declaration in Isaiah
because it occurs within the context of a [Father-Son relationship][2]. God
does not share His glory with idols. However, He freely bestows it upon His Son
to accomplish His divine purposes. This [delegated glory][3] ultimately returns
to the Father. This ensures that God remains the source of all honor. By
understanding this [biblical context][4], the [apparent contradiction][5] is
resolved.
