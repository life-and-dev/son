# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

In the Hebrew Scriptures, God declares that He will not share His glory with
anyone else. This statement creates a profound theological question when
compared with the New Testament, where Jesus frequently speaks of his own
glorification. Does this imply that Jesus is claiming to be God Himself, or is
there a way that God shares His glory with His Son without contradicting His
earlier declaration?

## The Apparent Contradiction

The starting point for this discussion is often found in the book of Isaiah:

> I am the LORD, that is My name; And My glory I will not give to another, Nor
> My praise to graven images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus said:

> And Jesus answered them, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified. — John 12:23 (ESV)

and

> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

Out of context, it seems like Jesus contradicts Isaiah. To solve this
contradiction, some would argue this proof that Jesus is
[the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh). This argument is supported by the confusion
caused by removing God's name. When the divine name is replaced with "the LORD,"
it becomes the same title used for Jesus. Critics often argue that since God
does not share His glory, and Jesus is glorified, Jesus must be God. However,
[this reasoning is often challenged](https://www.revbible.com/Isaiah/42/8)
because receiving glory does not automatically make one part of the Godhead.

## Shared Glory and Unity

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the issue was that God does not want to share His
glory with foreign idols ("carved images"). However, as the Father of Jesus, the
Son of God, He has no problem with anyone glorifying His Son. This is because
that automatically also glorifies Him.

The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) provides further
clarity on this distinction. In Isaiah 42:8, the word used for "another" is
*heteros*, which means another of a different kind. This indicates that God
will not give His glory to idols, which are a different kind of being
altogether. In contrast, Jesus prayed for the glory he shared with the Father.

> And now, Father, glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had
> with you before the world existed. — John 17:5 (ESV)

Since the Son is of the same kind as the Father, sharing glory between them does
not violate the prohibition against giving glory to *heteros* (different) gods.
[This distinction is highlighted in Greek scholarship](https://hermeneutics.stackexchange.com/questions/95415/if-the-lord-gives-his-glory-to-no-other-then-what-is-the-glory-that-jesus-gives)
regarding this verse. Jesus even shared this glory with his disciples, further
demonstrating that it can be shared with those in unity with God.

> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one
> even as we are one. — John 17:22 (ESV)

This shared glory points to a unity of purpose and mission rather than
ontological identity.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

No parent should be competing with their children. They should instead support
their children. For example, a human father is also glorified when someone
honors his son. This means that the father raised his son well.

> that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

Therefore, both Isaiah and Jesus are correct. In the context of John 17, Jesus
prayed a few verses earlier:

> Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you,
> since you have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to all
> whom you have given him. — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

In other words, if someone gives glory to Jesus, it also glorifies God the
Father of Jesus.

## Distinction of Persons

Jesus himself says that it is useless to glorify oneself:

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who
> glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’ — John 8:54 (ESV)

Therefore, [Jesus has to be distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md)
to be glorified by Him. This distinction is vital because glory is something
given from one person to another. If Jesus were the Father, he would be
glorifying himself, which he explicitly stated would make his glory "nothing."

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the statement that God will not
give His glory to another ([The Apparent Contradiction](#the-apparent-contradiction)).
This is because the context of the prohibition focuses on foreign idols rather
than God's own Son ([Shared Glory and Unity](#shared-glory-and-unity)). The
unity of purpose between the Father and the Son means that glorifying the Son
ultimately honors the Father ([Shared Glory and Unity](#shared-glory-and-unity)).
Furthermore, the distinction of persons remains clear as the Father is the one
who bestows glory upon the Son ([Distinction of Persons](#distinction-of-persons)).
