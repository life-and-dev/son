# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

The relationship between divine glory and the person of Jesus Christ presents a
profound biblical mystery. In the Old Testament, the Creator makes an absolute
declaration about the exclusivity of his glory. Yet, the New Testament records
Jesus speaking frequently about his own glorification. This apparent tension has
sparked extensive theological inquiry throughout history. By examining the
context of these statements, one can gain a deeper understanding of how these
two seemingly contradictory claims might actually harmonize.

## Isaiah Challenge

The foundational challenge to the idea of shared glory comes from a specific
declaration in the book of Isaiah.

> I am the LORD, that is My name;
> And My glory I will not give to another,
> Nor My praise to carved images.
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

This verse seems to establish a strict boundary. God declares that his name and
glory are reserved for him alone. This claim often serves as a primary argument
for the exclusive nature of the Deity. If God refuses to share his glory, any
claim of glory by another would appear to be a direct contradiction of this
divine decree. Some interpret this to mean that if Jesus is glorified, he must
be the same being as the LORD mentioned in the prophecy.

## Context of Idolatry

The context of Isaiah 42 focuses on the futility of idols. God contrasts his
power and foresight with the "carved images" that cannot speak or act. The Greek
Septuagint translation of this verse uses the word *heterō* for "another." This
term specifically refers to another of a different kind, such as a lifeless
idol. This is distinct from the word *allos*, which means another of the same
kind.

This linguistic distinction suggests that God is not necessarily excluding his
own Son. The prohibition targets ontologically different entities, like idols of
wood and stone, from receiving the honor due to the Creator. In this light, the
glory given to Jesus does not compete with God but rather fulfills his
redemptive purpose. Critiques often argue that "another" must mean any entity
other than the Father. However, the immediate reference to "carved images"
suggests that the exclusion is specifically directed at false gods and idols.

## Reciprocal Glorification

Jesus frequently spoke about his glorification as a means to bring honor to the
Father. He did not view his glory as something stolen or separate from God.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified."
> — John 12:23 (ESV)
>
> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

Jesus explains that the Father is the source of this glory. The glorification of
the Son results in the glorification of the Father. This reciprocal
relationship ensures that all honor ultimately rests with God.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once.
> — John 13:32 (ESV)

Just as a human father is honored when his son is respected, the Father is
glorified when the world recognizes the Son. This mutual honor is a central
theme in the ministry of Jesus.

## Evidence of Shared Glory

The New Testament suggests that the glory Jesus possesses is not a new or
independent glory. He identifies it as a glory shared with the Father from
eternity.

> And now, Father, glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had
> with you before the world existed.
> — John 17:5 (ESV)

This shared glory supports the command that all should honor the Son in the same
manner as the Father. This does not necessarily imply they are the same person,
but that they share a unique bond of authority and purpose.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him.
> — John 5:23 (ESV)

Jesus also clarifies that he does not seek his own glory in a way that would
bypass the Father. He recognizes that true glory comes from God.

> Jesus answered, "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who
> glorifies me, of whom you say, 'He is our God.'"
> — John 8:54 (ESV)

Therefore, Jesus remains distinct from the Father while receiving glory from
him. This distinction is necessary for the act of glorification to have meaning.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not violate the [exclusive nature](#isaiah-challenge)
of God's glory. When understood in its [proper context](#context-of-idolatry),
the prohibition in Isaiah targets false idols rather than the Son. The
[mutual honor](#reciprocal-glorification) between the Father and the Son
demonstrates a shared purpose in the plan of salvation. Ultimately, Jesus is the
recipient of a [shared glory](#evidence-of-shared-glory) that points all praise
back to the Father.
