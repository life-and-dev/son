# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

Does Jesus claim for himself the glory that God says he will not give to another? This article explores the relationship between the glory of the Father and the glory of the Son. It examines whether they are in competition or perfect harmony.

## An Apparent Conflict in Glory

> I am the LORD, that is My name;
> And My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another, nor My [praise](../../nature.md) to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet Jesus said:

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

and

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and I am [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory) in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

When taken [out of context](../../nature.md#concerns), these statements might appear to show Jesus contradicting Isaiah. Some interpret this apparent contradiction as proof that Jesus is [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament). This argument often relies on [the confusion caused by removing God's name](../../nature.md#bible-translations) from many translations. In those versions, the divine name is replaced with "the LORD," which is the same title used for Jesus.

## Isaiah Context

In the context of Isaiah 42:8, the primary issue was that God does not want to share His glory with foreign idols or carved images. However, as the Father of Jesus, He encourages the glorification of His Son. Glorifying the Son automatically brings glory to the Father as well.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

## Glorifying the Son to Glorify the Father

A parent does not compete with their children. Instead, they support them. For example, a human father is honored when someone praises his son. Such praise indicates that the father raised his son well.

> That all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. — John 5:23 (ESV)

Therefore, both Isaiah and Jesus are correct. In the context of John 17, Jesus prayed a few verses earlier:

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said, "Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify You, since [You have given him authority over all flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance), [to give eternal life to all whom You have given him](../similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father)." — John 17:1,2 (ESV)

If someone gives glory to Jesus, that act also glorifies God the Father.

## Bestowed Glory Requires Distinction

Jesus himself says that it is useless to glorify oneself:

> Jesus answered, "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father Who glorifies me, of Whom you say, 'He is our God.'" — John 8:54 (ESV)

Therefore, [Jesus has to be distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md) to be glorified by Him.

## A Defense Against Critical Arguments

Some critics argue that God's statement in Isaiah 42:8 is absolute and excludes anyone else from receiving glory. However, the Greek translation of the Old Testament provides important nuance. In the Septuagint, the word for "another" in this verse is *heterō*. This term refers to another of a different kind, such as the idols and carved images mentioned in the same context. In contrast, the New Testament often describes the relationship between the Father and the Son using terms that imply they are of the same kind. Because the Son is the "radiance of God's glory" and the "exact representation of his being," the glory given to him does not violate the prohibition against giving glory to "another" of a different kind. Instead, glorifying the Son is a way of honoring the Father who sent him.

## Conclusion

The apparent conflict between Isaiah and the claims of Jesus resolves when one examines the [context of idolatry](#isaiah-context) in the Old Testament. God refuses to share His glory with foreign idols, but He actively [glorifies His Son](#glorifying-the-son-to-glorify-the-father) to bring honor to Himself. This [bestowed glory](#bestowed-glory-requires-distinction) demonstrates that Jesus is distinct from the Father while remaining perfectly united in purpose.
