# Jesus Shared in God's Glory

The concept of glory is central to the biblical understanding of God's majesty
and his relationship with humanity. A seemingly difficult tension arises when
one compares the Old Testament's declaration that God will not give his glory
to another with the New Testament's accounts of Jesus being glorified by the
Father. This article examines how these statements function together to reveal
a deeper truth about the nature of divine honor and the unique role of the Son
of God.

## The Prohibition of Idolatry

The prophet Isaiah recorded a powerful statement regarding God's exclusive
claim to honor. This passage is often cited to suggest that God never shares
his glory with any other being.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament), that is My name;
> and **My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**,
> nor My [praise](../../nature.md#worship) to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

The context of this verse is crucial for understanding its scope. God was
addressing the issue of foreign idols and "carved images" that the people of
Israel were tempted to worship. He was asserting his supremacy over lifeless
statues that could not see, hear, or save. The focus is on the contrast between
the living Creator and dead inventions of human hands.

## A Linguistic Distinction

Critiques often argue that if God gives his glory to no one else, yet Jesus
receives glory, then Jesus must be numerically identical to God. However, a
study of the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) provides a
nuanced perspective. In Isaiah 42:8, the Greek word used for "another" is
*heteros*.

In Greek, *heteros* typically refers to another of a different kind. By using
this word, the text emphasizes that God will not give his glory to beings that
are fundamentally different from him, such as man-made idols. In contrast, when
the New Testament speaks of "another" of the same kind, it often uses the word
*allos*.

This distinction suggests that while God refuses to share his unique divine
honor with false gods or created images, he can and does share his glory with
his Son. Jesus is described as being in the "form of God" and sharing a unique
unity with the Father. Therefore, the glorification of Jesus does not violate
the principle of Isaiah 42:8 because Jesus is not "another" in the sense of a
different, competing deity or an idol. More information on this distinction can
be found in the
[Revised English Version commentary](https://www.revbible.com/isaiah/42/8).

## The Glorification of the Son

Despite the prohibition against giving glory to idols, the New Testament
frequently speaks of Jesus being glorified. Jesus himself anticipated this
event as the culmination of his earthly mission.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

Jesus also expressed that his glory was inextricably linked with the Father's
glory. He saw no conflict between his own honor and the honor due to God.

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and
> **I am [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory)** in them. — John 17:10 (ESV)

The relationship described in the Gospels is one of shared honor between a
Father and a Son. When a parent is honored through the success and character of
their child, the two remain distinct individuals. The honor given to the child
reflects back onto the parent who raised and empowered them.

## Shared Honor and Purpose

Jesus made it clear that any glory he received was ultimately for the purpose
of honoring his Father. The two are united in purpose and mission.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

Just as a human father is honored when his son is respected, the Father is
glorified when the Son is honored. Jesus explicitly taught that honoring the
Son is a requirement for honoring the Father.

> All may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. — John 5:23 (ESV)

In his prayer before his crucifixion, Jesus asked the Father to glorify him so
that he could, in turn, bring glory back to the Father. He acknowledged that
his authority was a gift from God.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and
> said, “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may
> glorify You**, since
> [You have given him authority over all flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance),
> [to give eternal life to all whom You have given him](../similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father).
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

## Glory Through Distinction

For one person to glorify another, there must be a distinction between them.
Jesus emphasized that he did not seek his own glory through his own efforts.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> Who glorifies me**, of Whom you say, ‘He is our God.’ — John 8:54 (ESV)

This statement highlights that
[Jesus is distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). If they
were the same being in every sense, the act of one glorifying the other would
lose its relational significance. The Father chooses to bestow honor upon the
Son, and the Son faithfully returns that honor to the Father.

## Conclusion

The biblical narrative does not present a contradiction between Isaiah 42:8 and
the glorification of Jesus. The prohibition in Isaiah is directed at idols and
false gods who are of a different kind than the true God. In contrast, Jesus is
the Son of God who shares in the Father's glory through a unique relationship
of unity and purpose.

- The prohibition of glory being given to another refers to idols of a
  different kind ([#the-prohibition-of-idolatry](#the-prohibition-of-idolatry)).
- The linguistic distinction between *heteros* and *allos* clarifies that God
  can share glory with his Son

- Jesus' glorification serves to ultimately glorify the Father
  ([#shared-honor-and-purpose](#shared-honor-and-purpose)).
- The act of glorification requires a distinction between the Father and the
