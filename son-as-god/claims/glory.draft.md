# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

In the book of Isaiah, God declares that he will not give his glory to another.
Yet, the New Testament records Jesus frequently speaking of being glorified and
even asking the Father to glorify him. This apparent tension raises a profound
question: Does Jesus' claim to glory contradict the monotheistic declaration of
the Old Testament, or does it reveal a deeper relationship between the Father
and the Son?

## The Declaration in Isaiah

The Old Testament is clear about the exclusive nature of
[God's glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory). In the book of Isaiah, God
identifies himself by his name and asserts his unique status.

> I am the LORD, that is My name; and My glory I will not give to another, nor
> My praise to graven images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

This statement was made in a context where God was contrasting himself with
foreign idols and "graven images." He does not want to share his glory with
false gods that have no life.

## Jesus and the Father's Shared Glory

In contrast to the prohibition in Isaiah, Jesus spoke of himself being
glorified. He even claimed to have shared glory with the Father before the
world began.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

Jesus also stated his shared ownership and glorification with the Father.

> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

Some argue that these statements prove Jesus is [the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh).
This argument is often supported by the fact that many translations replace
God's name with "the LORD," which is the same title used for Jesus. However, the
relationship between God and Jesus suggests a sharing of glory rather than a
competition for it.

## A Difference of Kind

The Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Septuagint (LXX), provides a
helpful distinction in Isaiah 42:8. The word used for "another" is *heteros*,
which means another of a different kind. This implies that God will not give his
glory to idols, which are completely different from him.

In the New Testament, when Jesus speaks of another Helper in John 14:16, the
word used is *allos*, meaning another of the same kind. Since Jesus is the Son
of God, he is of the same kind as his Father. Therefore, God sharing glory with
his Son does not violate the principle in Isaiah, as Jesus is not a "different
kind" of god or a foreign idol. This linguistic distinction is noted by
scholars as a way to understand the [theological implications of allos and
heteros](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/401018996_Distinguishing_the_true_Christ_Semantic_and_theological_implications_of_allos_and_heteros_in_the_New_Testament)
in the New Testament.

## Glorifying the Son Glorifies the Father

Jesus explained that the glory he receives actually reflects back to God. There
is no conflict between the honor given to the Son and the honor due to the
Father.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

Just as a human father is honored when his son is praised for his character or
achievements, God is glorified when people honor his Son. Jesus explicitly
stated the purpose of this honor.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not
> honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. — John 5:23 (ESV)

In the context of his final prayer, Jesus asked the Father to glorify him so
that he could, in turn, glorify the Father. This shows a reciprocal relationship
of honor.

> Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you,
> since [you have given him authority](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance) over
> all flesh, [to give eternal life](../similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father)
> to all whom you have given him. — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

The authority Jesus received to give eternal life was granted by the Father.
This highlights that the Son's glory is derived from the Father. This pattern is
also seen in the prophecies of Daniel, where the Son of Man is given glory by
the Ancient of Days.

> And to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples,
> nations, and languages should serve him; his dominion is an everlasting
> dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be
> destroyed. — Daniel 7:14 (ESV)

## Distinction Between Giver and Receiver

Jesus himself emphasized that he did not seek his own glory. He acknowledged
that true glory must come from the Father.

> Jesus answered, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’” — John 8:54 (ESV)

For the Father to glorify the Son, they must be
[distinct from one another](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). One person does not
usually "glorify" himself in a meaningful way. Instead, the act of glorification
happens between two parties. This distinction allows the Father to be the source
of glory and the Son to be the recipient who then reflects that glory back to
the Giver.

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not compete with God's glory but rather serves
as a means through which the Father is honored
([Glorifying the Son Glorifies the Father](#glorifying-the-son-glorifies-the-father)).
Biblical evidence shows that God willingly shares his glory with his Son, a
relationship that is distinct from the prohibition against sharing glory with
idols ([A Difference of Kind](#a-difference-of-kind)). Ultimately, Jesus
receiving glory from the Father highlights their unique relationship while
maintaining their [distinct identities](../../son-of-man/distinct.md)
([Distinction Between Giver and Receiver](#distinction-between-giver-and-receiver)).
