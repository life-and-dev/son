# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

A profound theological tension arises when comparing the exclusive claims of God
in the Old Testament with the statements of Jesus in the Gospels. In the book of
Isaiah, God declares that He will not share His glory with another. Yet, Jesus
frequently spoke of his own glorification and even prayed for the Father to
glorify him. This raises an important question for any student of the Bible.
Does Jesus' glorification contradict the monotheistic declaration of the Old
Testament, or does it reveal a deeper relationship between the Father and the
Son?

## The Challenge of Isaiah 42:8

The foundational text for this discussion is found in the prophecies of Isaiah.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament), that is My name;
> And **My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**,
> nor My [praise](../../nature.md#praise) to carved images.
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

This verse establishes a clear boundary. God refuses to yield His honor or
splendor to any other entity. This is often interpreted as an absolute
prohibition against any being besides God receiving divine glory. However, Jesus
made several claims that seem to challenge this boundary.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and
> **I am [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory)** in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

At first glance, these statements appear to be in direct opposition. If God does
not give His glory to another, how can Jesus be glorified? Some interpret this
as proof that Jesus must be the LORD Himself. This reasoning is often reinforced
by the practice of replacing the divine name with "the LORD" in many Bible
translations. This practice can lead to confusion between the Father and the
Son.

## Glorifying the Son

The apparent contradiction is resolved when we examine the context of Isaiah and
the nature of the relationship between God and His Son. In Isaiah 42:8, the
immediate contrast is with "carved images." God refuses to share His glory with
foreign idols or false deities. These are entities of a completely different
kind than the true God.

A significant distinction exists in the Greek Septuagint (LXX) translation of
this verse. The word used for "another" is *heterō* (from *heteros*). This term
typically refers to another of a different kind. This suggests that God will not
give His glory to idols or other "gods" that are fundamentally different from
Him. In contrast, the New Testament often uses the word *allos* to describe
another of the same kind. As the Son of God, Jesus is of the same "kind" as his
Father. He is a member of the same family and shares the same purpose.

Trinitarians argue that sharing glory proves that Jesus is ontologically equal
to God. They suggest that only God can possess divine glory. However, Jesus
explained that the glory he received was a gift that could also be shared with
his disciples.

> The glory that You have given me I have given to them, that they may be one
> even as we are one. — John 17:22 (ESV)

This suggests a functional unity rather than a shared ontological essence. Just
as the Father gives glory to the Son, the Son gives glory to those who follow
him. This shared glory does not make the disciples part of a Godhead. Instead,
it reflects their participation in God's mission and character.

## Unity of Purpose

Jesus consistently pointed to the Father as the source of his glory. He did not
seek honor for himself independently.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> Who glorifies me**, of Whom you say, ‘He is our God.’” — John 8:54 (ESV)

In his high priestly prayer, Jesus explicitly linked his glorification to the
glorification of the Father.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify You**,
> since [You have given him authority over all flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance),
> [to give eternal life to all whom You have given him](../../son-as-god/similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father).
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

The purpose of Jesus receiving glory is to redirect that honor back to God. This
reflects a unity of mission and purpose.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. — John 5:23 (ESV)

When people honor the Son, they are ultimately honoring the Father who sent him.
There is no competition for glory between them. The Father is glorified when His
Son is successful in his redemptive mission.

## Distinct Roles

The fact that the Father glorifies the Son proves that they are distinct
persons. One cannot glorify oneself in the same way that another can bestow
honor.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> Who glorifies me**...” — John 8:54 (ESV)

For the Father to glorify the Son,
[Jesus must be distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md).
This distinction allows for a relationship of mutual love and honor. The Father
gives authority and glory to the Son, and the Son uses that authority to bring
people to the Father. The New Testament confirms that God highly exalted Jesus
and gave him a name above every name to the glory of God the Father
(Philippians 2:9-11).

## Conclusion

The glorification of Jesus does not contradict the declaration that God will not
give His glory to another. Instead, it illustrates a relationship of perfect
harmony. God refuses to share His glory with
[idols of a different kind](#glorifying-the-son), but He freely glorifies His
Son who shares His [mission and purpose](#unity-of-purpose). This glorification
does not compete with the Father but ultimately
[serves to honor Him](#unity-of-purpose). The distinct roles of the Father and
the Son allow for a relationship where [one can bestow honor upon the other](#distinct-roles)
for the benefit of all who believe.
