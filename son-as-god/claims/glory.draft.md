# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

A central tenet of the Old Testament is that God does not share His unique glory
with anyone else. However, the New Testament frequently describes Jesus as being
glorified and receiving honor similar to that of the Father. This apparent
tension raises important questions about the nature of Jesus and his
relationship to the Almighty. By examining the context of these claims, a
harmonious understanding can be reached that preserves the oneness of God while
fully honoring the Son.

## The Warning of Isaiah

The prophet Isaiah recorded a clear statement from the Creator regarding His
name and His glory.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#yhvh), that is My name;
> And **My glory I will not give to another**, nor My praise to carved images.
> — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

This declaration established a boundary between the true God and all other
entities. It specifically addressed the problem of idolatry, where people
attributed divine honors to "carved images" or false gods.

A common critical argument is that if God says He will not give His glory to
"another," then anyone who receives His glory must be God Himself. However, the
Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) uses the word *heteros*
for "another." In this context, *heteros* refers to another of a different kind,
such as a false idol. This suggests that while God does not share His glory with
different kinds of entities like idols, He can and does share it with His Son,
who is of the same kind in terms of nature and purpose.

## Jesus and the Claim to Glory

In contrast to the strict boundary in Isaiah, Jesus made several statements
about his own glorification.

> And Jesus answered them, saying, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified. — John 12:23 (ESV)

Later, in his prayer to the Father, he added:

> All mine are Yours, and Yours are mine, and **I am glorified** in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

Some interpreters suggest that if only God possesses glory and Jesus also
possesses glory, then Jesus must be the same being as the LORD. This argument is
often reinforced by the practice of replacing the divine name (YHVH) with "the
LORD" in English translations. Since Jesus is also called "Lord," the
distinction between the two can become blurred for many readers.

## Glory Through the Father

A closer look at the relationship between the Father and the Son reveals that
Jesus does not compete with God for glory. Instead, his glory is a gift from the
Father.

> If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in Himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

Jesus himself clarified that seeking his own glory would be meaningless.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> Who glorifies me**, of Whom you say, ‘He is our God.’ — John 8:54 (ESV)

This indicates that the glory of Jesus is not something he possesses
independently. It is bestowed upon him by the Father to accomplish a specific
purpose. When Jesus is honored, that honor ultimately reflects back to the
Father who sent him.

## Honoring the Son

The New Testament encourages believers to honor the Son in a manner that
parallels the honor given to the Father.

> All may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. — John 5:23 (ESV)

This shared honor does not violate the principle in Isaiah. In the same way that
a human father is honored when his son is praised for his character and
achievements, God the Father is glorified when His Son is recognized. Jesus
prayed for this mutual glorification.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify
> You**, since You have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal
> life to all whom You have given him. — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

By fulfilling his role and exercising the authority given to him, Jesus brings
glory to the Father. Therefore, the glorification of the Son is a primary means
by which God Himself is glorified in the world.

## Conclusion

The statements made by Isaiah and Jesus are not in conflict. While God will not
share His glory with false idols or "another" of a different kind, He chooses to
glorify His Son. This distinction is maintained because [Jesus is a separate
identity from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). He acts as the
Father's perfect representative, ensuring that all glory given to him
ultimately serves to honor the one true God.

* [Isaiah 42:8 warns](#the-warning-of-isaiah) that God will not share His glory
  with different kinds of entities, such as carved images.
* The claims of Jesus to possess glory do not mean he is the same being as the
  LORD, as his glory is a [gift from the Father](#glory-through-the-father).
* The shared honor between the Father and the Son [does not violate
  monotheism](#honoring-the-son) because the honor given to the Son reflects
  back to the Father.
