# Jesus Claims to Be Glorified

When reading the Bible, one might encounter statements that seem to stand in
tension with one another. A prominent example involves the declaration that God
will not give His glory to another, juxtaposed with the many instances where
Jesus speaks of his own glorification. This apparent conflict invites a closer
look at the context of these passages and the profound unity shared between the
Father and the Son.

## Understanding the Glory of God

The starting point for this discussion is often a specific declaration in the
book of Isaiah where God asserts His unique status.

> I am [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament), that is My name; and
> **My [glory](../../nature.md#gods-glory) I will not give to another**, nor My
> [praise](../../nature.md#worship) to carved images. — Isaiah 42:8 (NKJV)

Yet, in the New Testament, Jesus frequently speaks about being glorified.

> And Jesus answered them, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be
> glorified." — John 12:23 (ESV)
>
> All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and
> **I am [glorified](../../nature.md#gods-glory)** in them.
> — John 17:10 (ESV)

[Out of context](../../nature.md#concerns), it might appear that Jesus'
statements contradict the declaration in Isaiah. Some interpreters use this
apparent contradiction to argue that Jesus must be
[the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament) Himself. This perspective is
sometimes reinforced by
[the confusion caused by removing God's name](../../nature.md#bible-translations)
from many Bible translations, where the specific name of God is replaced with
"the LORD," a title also applied to Jesus.

## Shared Glory Between Father and Son

However, a closer examination of the context of Isaiah 42:8 reveals that the
primary focus is God's refusal to share His glory with foreign idols or
"carved images." In contrast, the relationship between God the Father and Jesus
the Son is one of perfect unity. When the Son is glorified, it does not take
away from the Father's glory; instead, it contributes to it.

> If God is glorified in him, god will also glorify him in himself, and glorify
> him at once. — John 13:32 (ESV)

This dynamic can be compared to the relationship between a human father and his
son. A father is not diminished when his son is honored. Rather, the son's honor
reflects well on the father who raised him. Jesus explained that the Father's
will is for the Son to receive honor as a reflection of the Father's own
authority.

> ...that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father.
> — John 5:23 (ESV)

Therefore, both Isaiah and Jesus are correct within their respective contexts.
In his prayer in John 17, Jesus makes it clear that his glorification is
intimately tied to the glorification of the Father.

> When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said,
> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you**,
> since [you have given him authority over all flesh](../../nature.md#jesus-inheritance),
> [to give eternal life to all whom you have given him](../similarities/act.md#jesus-judge-as-the-father).”
> — John 17:1-2 (ESV)

In this light, giving glory to Jesus is an act that ultimately glorifies God the
Father.

## Distinction Through Glorification

Jesus also emphasized that true glory is not something one claims for oneself,
but something received from God.

> Jesus answered, “**If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father
> who glorifies me**, of whom you say, ‘He is our God.’” — John 8:54 (ESV)

This statement highlights that
[Jesus is distinct from God the Father](../../son-of-man/distinct.md). For one
to glorify another, there must be two distinct parties involved. One bestows the
glory while the other receives it.

## Defense Against Critique

A common Trinitarian argument suggests that since God will not give His glory to
"another" (*Isaiah 42:8*), and Jesus is glorified (*John 17:5*), Jesus must be
God. However, the Greek Septuagint (LXX) translation of Isaiah 42:8 uses the
word *heterō* (a form of *heteros*). This typically refers to another of a
different kind. A prominent example would be a foreign idol. In contrast, when
the New Testament speaks of "another" like Jesus, it often uses *allos*, meaning
another of the same kind.

Reputable sources, such as the REV Bible commentary, point out that if
receiving glory made one part of the Godhead, then all believers would also be
part of the Godhead. Jesus explicitly stated that he has given the glory he
received to his followers.

> The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one
> even as we are one. — John 17:22 (ESV)

This indicates that glory is a gift that God can choose to bestow upon His Son
and His servants without violating His unique status.

## Conclusion

The apparent conflict between Isaiah and the Gospels is resolved by recognizing
the [unique relationship](#shared-glory-between-father-and-son) between the
Father and the Son. God does not share His glory with
[idols](#understanding-the-glory-of-god), but He chose to
[glorify His Son](#shared-glory-between-father-and-son), who in turn
[glorifies the Father](#distinction-through-glorification). Ultimately, the
glory given to Jesus serves to fulfill the Father's
[divine purpose](#defense-against-critique).
