# Jesus Claims to Be Good

When a man addressed Jesus as "Good Teacher," Jesus responded with a question that seemed to distance himself from the attribute of goodness. He asked why the man called him good, stating that only God is truly good. However, Jesus also referred to himself as the "good shepherd" in other teachings. This comparison invites a deeper exploration of the original Greek language and the cultural context of his day to understand what Jesus meant by these statements.

## Comparing Goodness

A comparison of Jesus' statements suggests a possible contradiction that requires further examination. In the Gospels of Mark, Luke, and Matthew, Jesus asks:

> Why callest thou me good? there is none good but one, that is, God. — Mark 10:18; Luke 18:19; Matthew 19:17 (KJV)

Yet, in the Gospel of John, Jesus explicitly identifies himself with the term "good":

> I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep. — John 10:11 (KJV)

When viewed without considering the context, these verses might seem to imply that Jesus is claiming to be God because he is "good." However, other scriptures indicate that people besides God can be described as "good." For example, Jesus spoke of good people in his parables and teachings:

> A good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things. — Matthew 12:35 (KJV)

> That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust. — Matthew 5:45 (KJV)

The Hebrew scriptures also describe individuals as good, while attributing the source of that goodness to God:

> The steps of a good man are ordered by the LORD: and he delighteth in his way. — Psalm 37:23 (KJV)

## Greek Terminology

The apparent contradiction is resolved by examining the specific Greek words used in the original text. The word translated as "good" in the interaction with the young man is *agathos*. This term refers to an intrinsic nature or being fundamentally good.

In contrast, when Jesus describes himself as the "good shepherd" in John 10, the Greek word is *kalos*. This term means noble, worthy, or of excellent character. It describes the quality of one's actions and character rather than their essential nature. By calling himself the good shepherd, Jesus was referring to his obedience to God. He committed himself to doing the Father's will rather than his own.

> And being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross. — Philippians 2:8 (KJV)

## Historical Context

The cultural context of the first century provides further insight into Jesus' response. A man ran up to Jesus and addressed him with a specific title:

> And as he was setting out on his journey, a man ran up and knelt before him and asked him, “Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?” — Mark 10:17; Luke 18:18; Matthew 19:16 (ESV)

In this period, rabbis were highly respected figures who taught and followed the law. The man's use of "Good Teacher" likely linked the status of a rabbi with the idea of being naturally good. Jesus challenged this assumption. He questioned why the man attributed intrinsic goodness to him simply because he was a teacher. He clarified that keeping the commandments or holding a religious office does not make a person good by nature. Only God possesses that inherent quality.

## The Divine Nature

Jesus used this encounter to demonstrate that inheriting eternal life is not a matter of outward religious observance. The man claimed to have kept all the commandments from his youth:

> And he answered and said unto him, Master, all these have I observed from my youth. — Mark 10:20 (KJV)

Despite his obedience, Jesus pointed out that the man's nature remained attached to his worldly possessions. To be "good" in the sense of *agathos*, a person must undergo a transformation of nature. This is achieved by taking on the nature of God rather than merely following rules.

> Whereby are given unto us exceeding great and precious promises: that by these ye might be partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust. — 2 Peter 1:4 (KJV)

## Different Perspectives

Some interpreters suggest that Jesus was not denying his own goodness but was instead testing the man. From this perspective, Jesus' question "Why do you call me good?" was intended to see if the man recognized that Jesus was indeed God, the only source of goodness. This view holds that Jesus was guiding the man to a deeper realization of his divinity.

However, others argue that Jesus was clearly distinguishing himself from God by stating that only God is good. They point to the different Greek words and the emphasis on his obedience to the Father as evidence. His goodness was derived from his relationship with God rather than being an inherent divine attribute.

## Conclusion

The study of Jesus' claims to goodness highlights the importance of language and context in biblical interpretation.

*   The use of different Greek terms distinguishes between [intrinsic nature and character](#greek-terminology).
*   The historical context shows that Jesus was [challenging the religious assumptions](#historical-context) of his time.
*   True goodness is presented as a [transformation of nature](#the-divine-nature) through God's promises rather than legalistic adherence to the law.
