# Jesus' Claim to Be "in" God

The Gospel of John contains many profound statements where Jesus speaks of an
intimate relationship between himself, his Father, and his disciples. One of the
most intriguing expressions he uses is the concept of being "in" the Father.
While some interpret this as a claim of ontological identity or shared divinity,
others point to the biblical use of similar language to describe the spiritual
union of all believers. Understanding the historical and scriptural context of
this phrase can provide clarity on whether Jesus was claiming to be God or
inviting his followers into a unique form of divine fellowship.

## Unity Through Communion

> I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you. Yet a little while and the
> world will see me no more, but you will see me. Because I live, you also will
> live. — John 14:18-19 (ESV)

The scriptures indicate that Jesus will return one day to collect the children
of God so that they can live with him and the Father.

> In that day you will know that I am in my Father, and you in me, and I in you.
> — John 14:20 (ESV)

When the Father, Jesus, and believers live "in" each other, it signifies a close
communion or fellowship. This type of relationship is described elsewhere in the
scriptures as a shared fellowship with both the Father and the Son.

> That which we have seen and heard we proclaim also to you, so that you too may
> have fellowship with us; and indeed our fellowship is with the Father and with
> his Son Jesus Christ. — 1 John 1:3 (ESV)

Living in unity with the Father and the Son suggests that followers will reflect
their character and actions.

> Whoever has my commandments and keeps them, he it is who loves me. And he who
> loves me will be loved by my Father, and I will love him and manifest myself
> to him.”
>
> Judas (not Iscariot) said to him, “Lord, how is it that you will manifest
> yourself to us, and not to the world?”
>
> Jesus answered him, “If anyone loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father
> will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him.”
> — John 14:21-23 (ESV)

## The Vine and the Branches

Jesus continues in the next chapter to explain the nature of this relationship:

> “I am the true vine, and my Father is the vinedresser. Every branch in me that
> does not bear fruit he takes away, and every branch that does bear fruit he
> prunes, that it may bear more fruit. Already you are clean because of the word
> that I have spoken to you. Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot
> bear fruit by itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless
> you abide in me. I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever abides in me
> and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do
> nothing. If anyone does not abide in me he is thrown away like a branch and
> withers; and the branches are gathered, thrown into the fire, and burned. If
> you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it
> will be done for you. By this my Father is glorified, that you bear much
> fruit and so prove to be my disciples. As the Father has loved me, so have I
> loved you. Abide in my love. If you keep my commandments, you will abide in
> my love, just as I have kept my Father’s commandments and abide in his love.
> — John 15:1-10 (ESV)

This comparison suggests that when believers "abide in" Jesus or when Jesus
"lives in" them, they do not become ontologically identical to Jesus. Similarly,
the presence of the Father in Jesus, or his submission to God's authority, does
not necessarily mean that Jesus is God. Instead, it reflects a shared purpose
and the way Jesus reveals the Father to others. Critics might argue that the
relationship between Jesus and the Father is unique and cannot be compared to
the relationship between Jesus and his disciples. However, the language Jesus
uses in his prayer in John 17 explicitly draws a parallel between these two
relationships. He prays that his followers may be one "even as" he and the
Father are one.

## Covenant Language

> When used in the sense of “in God,” or “in Christ,” the word “in” refers to a
> close communion, a tight fellowship. It was part of the covenant language of
> the day, when people spoke of being either “in” or “cut off from” the
> covenant. — Morgridge, pp. 116-117; Racovian Catechism, pp. 142-143;
> [Spirit & Truth Fellowship International](https://www.biblicalunitarian.com/verses/john-14-11)

## Perfectly One

Jesus also prayed to the Father rather than to himself:

> I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through
> their word, that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in
> you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you have
> sent me. The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may
> be one even as we are one, I in them and you in me, that they may become
> perfectly one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them
> even as you loved me. Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me,
> may be with me where I am, to see my glory that you have given me because you
> loved me before the foundation of the world. O righteous Father, even though
> the world does not know you, I know you, and these know that you have sent me.
> I made known to them your name, and I will continue to make it known, that the
> love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them.
> — John 17:20-26 (ESV)

## Conclusion

The language of being "in" God or "in" Christ describes a
[spiritual unity through communion](#unity-through-communion) rather than an
ontological identity. This [unity of purpose and fellowship](#the-vine-and-the-branches)
is a central theme in the Gospel of John, where Jesus invites his followers to
share in the same type of relationship he enjoys with the Father. By
understanding this [historical covenant language](#covenant-language), it
becomes clear that Jesus' statements were an invitation to
[perfect unity](#perfectly-one) for all believers.
