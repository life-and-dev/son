# Jesus Claims To Be The Good Shepherd

Scripture repeatedly applies shepherd imagery to God: Genesis 48:15, 49:24; Psalm 23:1, 28:9, 80:1; Isaiah 40:11; Jeremiah 31:10; Ezekiel 34:11-16; Micah 7:14.

If God is called "shepherd" and Jesus says, “I am the good shepherd” (John 10:11), does that identify Jesus as God?

## Words and Cultural Background

The Hebrew verb [רעה *rʿh*](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/7462.htm) can mean tending, feeding, or shepherding. In figurative settings, it can describe governing. Greek uses [ποιμήν](https://biblehub.com/greek/4166.htm), “shepherd”, and [ποιμαίνω](https://biblehub.com/greek/4165.htm), “to shepherd” or “to tend”. These are flexible leadership terms, not technical names restricted to deity.

In ancient Israelite and wider ancient Near Eastern literature, shepherding can picture guidance, provision, protection, justice, and rule. The image appears for gods and human kings. [SBL Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/shepherds-in-the-bible/) surveys biblical usage, the [Oxford ETCSL text of the Sumerian King List](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr211.htm) and [Yale’s Code of Hammurabi](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp) illustrate its wider royal setting. Human and divine usage therefore makes the term non-exclusive: calling a human ruler a shepherd does not, by that word alone, assign divinity.

Context can still make a larger claim. Lexical overlap alone cannot settle identity.

## Human Leaders Called Shepherds

The following inventory documents representative canonical uses of shepherd language for people. It is deliberately categorised, not presented as an exhaustive count of every translation or figurative flock image. It shows that the title and verb can apply to human leadership.

### Scope and Method

The inventory covers the 66-book Protestant canon. “Direct” entries explicitly call a human leader “shepherd(s)” or explicitly use a shepherding verb for leading people. “Qualified” entries are translation-dependent, prophetic, symbolic, or otherwise ambiguous. “Indirect” entries use flock imagery without naming a shepherd. Literal animal-keeping occupations are excluded.

#### David and 2 Samuel 24:17

David’s plea illustrates why textual form matters. In the [Masoretic Hebrew text](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/2_samuel/24-17.htm), David confesses his sin and asks that the hand be against him and his father’s house. He calls the people “these sheep”; the Masoretic text has flock or sheep imagery but no Hebrew shepherd noun, *רֹעֶה*.

The [Septuagint text](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=LXX%7Creference=2Sam.24.17) reads *ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ποιμήν*, “I am the shepherd”. A translation such as “I, the shepherd” therefore follows the Greek form, but adds wording not present in the extant Masoretic text. Both forms place David in a ruler-and-flock setting, but only the LXX explicitly names him shepherd.

### Qualified and Indirect Parallels

The following categories retain relevant texts without treating them as ordinary direct titles for human leaders.

#### Translation-Dependent, Prophetic, or Ambiguous Uses

These passages should not be counted as unambiguous ordinary-human-leader titles. 2 Samuel 24:17 concerns David, but its Hebrew and Greek textual forms differ; see [David and 2 Samuel 24:17](#david-and-2-samuel-2417). Isaiah 63:11 may refer to Moses or to Moses with Israel’s leaders, depending on textual and translation decisions. Jeremiah 6:3 uses shepherd imagery for invading leaders. Jeremiah 17:16 may be rendered “shepherd” or “leader”. Jeremiah 49:19, Jeremiah 50:44, and Jeremiah 51:23 occur in rhetorical or judgment contexts.

Ezekiel 34:23 and Ezekiel 37:24 call “My servant David” one shepherd. Christian readers often understand this promised Davidic ruler messianically, so these texts should not be counted as evidence that Jesus is merely an ordinary human leader. Micah 5:4 is also a prophetic ruler text. Zechariah 11:3-5,7-9,15-17 contains symbolic shepherd figures. Zechariah 13:7 is applied to Jesus in the Gospels. Ecclesiastes 12:11 speaks of “one shepherd”, which may refer to God or a wisdom authority. Jude 12 describes false teachers as self-feeding shepherds. Revelation 2:27 uses *ποιμαίνω* for ruling with an iron rod, a royal or messianic allusion rather than a pastoral office.

#### Indirect Flock Analogies and Literal Occupations

These passages name no shepherd, but flock imagery implies leaderlessness or failed leadership: Numbers 27:17, 1 Kings 22:17, 2 Chronicles 18:16, Ezekiel 34:5, Zechariah 10:2, Matthew 9:36, and Mark 6:34.

Literal occupations are excluded because they do not themselves make a leadership claim: Exodus 3:1, 1 Samuel 16:11, Luke 2:8, and 1 Corinthians 9:7.

## The Good Shepherd

Jesus said:

> **I am the good shepherd**. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. He who is a hired hand and not a shepherd, who does not own the sheep, sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees, and the wolf snatches them and scatters them. He flees because he is a hired hand and cares nothing for the sheep. **I am the good shepherd**.
>
> **I know my own and my own know me**, just as **the Father knows me and I know the Father**; and I lay down my life for the sheep.
>
> And I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also, and they will listen to my voice. So there will be one flock, **one shepherd**.
>
> — John 10:11-16 (ESV)

> My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. **My Father, Who has given them** to me, **is greater than all**, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father’s hand. I and the Father are one.”
>
> — John 10:27-30 (ESV)

In addition, John 10 is often compared with Ezekiel 34 where God seeks and rescues His sheep (Ezekiel 34:11-16) and appoints “my servant David” as their one shepherd (Ezekiel 34:23; Ezekiel 37:24). The argument is that Jesus’ one-shepherd language echoes this setting. His life-giving and preserving role (John 10:27-30), John 10:30, and the charge in John 10:33 provide cumulative material beyond a shared title.

However:

* John gives **no explicit quotation formula for Ezekiel**.
* **Ezekiel distinguishes God from “My servant David”**, the one shepherd.
* In John 10:18, Jesus says he **received this command** from his Father. No God receives commands from anyone else.
* In John 10:29, the Father **gives** the sheep. This means the Father is distinct and is the owner. Jesus is the "shepherd".
* In [John 10:28-30](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/10-28.htm), Jesus gives the sheep eternal life and preserves them in his hand; the Father gave them to him and preserves them in His hand. [John 10:30 in Greek](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/10-30.htm) has distinct first-person and third-person subjects, with neuter *ἕν* (“one”), not masculine *εἷς* (“one [person]”). John also uses *ἕν* for believers’ unity in [John 17:11](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/17-11.htm) and [17:21-23](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/17-21.htm). These observations support unity in the stated work of preserving the sheep, not identification of Father and Son as one person; they do not by themselves settle every Christological question.
* The accusation, “you … make yourself God” (John 10:33), reports Jesus’ opponents’ response, not the narrator’s conclusion. Jesus answers with Psalm 82:6 and calls himself the one whom the Father **sanctified** and **sent**, [“the Son of God”](../../index.md) (John 10:34-36).
* A shared predicate does not prove identity: “God is shepherd; Jesus is shepherd” no more proves identity than “God is king; David is king.”

## Conclusion

The shepherd title cannot prove Jesus’ divinity, because [Scripture applies the leadership image to human leaders](#human-leaders-called-shepherds) as well as to God.
