# Jesus Claims to Be the Son of God

The title "Son of God" is central to understanding the identity of Jesus Christ.
For many, this title immediately suggests divinity and equality with God.
However, in the ancient world, its meaning could vary significantly depending on
whether it was heard through Jewish or Pagan ears. This article explores how
Jesus' claim to be the Son of God was understood by his contemporaries and what
it reveals about his nature.

Jesus is identified as the "Son of God" in the Gospels. For example:

> The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.
> — Mark 1:1 (ESV)
>
> And I have seen and have borne witness that this (Jesus) is the Son of God.
> — John 1:34 (ESV)
>
> Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not
> written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus
> is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in
> his name. — John 20:31 (ESV)

## Historical Understandings

The pagan cultures of the first century often believed that the children of gods
were themselves divine. This perspective was common among the Greeks and Romans.
For them, a "son of God" was a demigod or a divine being. The Roman Empire, for
instance, considered the Emperor to be a god and his successor to be the next
divine ruler.

However, the author of the book of Hebrews wrote to a Jewish audience. This
author used the phrase "the Son of God" multiple times (Hebrews 4:14, 6:6, 7:3,
10:29). To a Hebrew believer, this title did not necessarily imply a claim to
divinity. Instead, it was a messianic title that signified a special
relationship with God.

## The Relationship of Essence

Theological discussions often focus on "essence," which refers to the
fundamental nature or "what" a person is. Some argue that being the Son of God
means Jesus shares the same divine essence as the Father. However, the Bible
frequently applies this title to beings who are clearly not God.

The title "Son of God" is often used to describe a relationship rather than an
ontological nature. For example, Adam is called a "son of God" because he was
created directly by God (Luke 3:38). Israel is called God's "firstborn son"
(Exodus 4:22). Believers are also described as sons and daughters of God
(Matthew 5:9; Galatians 4:4-7; Romans 8:14, 16-17; Ephesians 1:5;
2 Corinthians 6:18).

In these cases, the title highlights a relationship of adoption, creation, or
election. It does not imply that these individuals are made of "divine
substance." Jesus was not the first to speak of God as a Father to His people:

> I said, "How I would set you among My sons, and give you a pleasant land, a
> heritage most beautiful of all nations." And I thought you would call Me,
> "My Father," and would not turn from following Me. Surely, as a treacherous
> wife leaves her husband, so have you been treacherous to Me, O house of
> Israel, declares the LORD. — Jeremiah 3:19-20 (ESV)
>
> Have we not all one Father? Has not one God created us? — Malachi 2:10 (ESV)

## Defending Against Accusations of Equality

Critics often point to specific passages where Jesus' enemies accused him of
"making himself equal with God" by calling God his Father.

### The Response in John 5:18-19

In John 5:18, the Jewish leaders sought to kill Jesus because he was "calling
God his own Father, making himself equal with God." However, it is important to
note that this was an accusation made by his enemies, not a statement by the
narrator or Jesus himself.

Jesus' immediate response in verse 19 refutes the idea of equality in authority
or power. He states, "the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what
he sees the Father doing." An equal does not depend entirely on another for his
ability to act. By emphasizing his total dependence on the Father, Jesus
clarifies that his sonship is one of functional submission rather than
ontological equality.

### The Response in John 10:30-36

Similarly, in John 10:30, Jesus says, "I and the Father are one." His audience
interpreted this as a claim to be God and prepared to stone him. Jesus defended
himself by quoting Psalm 82:6, where human judges are called "gods" and "sons of
the Most High."

Jesus argued that if God called human representatives "gods" because the word of
God came to them, it was not blasphemy for the one whom the Father consecrated
and sent into the world to say, "I am the Son of God" (John 10:34-36). This
defense shows that Jesus used the title in a way consistent with Jewish
tradition. He saw himself as the unique representative of God, not as God
Himself.

## Comparison with the Son of Man

Jesus frequently referred to himself as the "Son of Man." This title appears
dozens of times in the Gospels (Matthew 8:20, 9:6, 10:23, 11:19, 12:8,40, 13:41,
16:13,27-28, 17:9,12,22, 18:11, 19:28, 20:18,28, 24:27,30,37,39,44, 25:13,31,
26:2,24,45,64; Mark 2:10,28, 8:31,38, 9:9,12,31, 10:33,45, 13:26, 14:21,41,62;
Luke 5:24, 6:5, 7:34, 9:22,26,44,56,58, 11:30, 12:8,10,40, 17:22,24,30, 18:8,31,
19:10, 21:27,36, 22:22,48,69, 24:7; John 1:51, 3:13-14, 5:27, 6:27,53,62, 8:28,
12:23,34, 13:31; Acts 7:56; Revelation 1:13, 14:14).

### Old Testament Contrast

In the Old Testament, "son of man" typically emphasizes human mortality and
distinction from God. For example, God says, "God is not man, that he should
lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind" (Numbers 23:19). The term
is also used extensively in Ezekiel to address the prophet as a mere mortal.

### New Testament Application

In the New Testament, Jesus uses "the Son of Man" as a specific messianic title.
It points to the figure in Daniel 7:13-14 who receives dominion from the Ancient
of Days. While this figure is exalted, he remains distinct from God.
Hebrews 2:6 quotes Psalm 8:4-6 to show that this title refers to humanity's role
in God's creation, a role perfectly fulfilled by Jesus.

## Conclusion

The title "Son of God" signifies a functional relationship with the Father
rather than an ontological essence
([The Relationship of Essence](#the-relationship-of-essence)).
While pagan cultures often associated such titles with divine nature, the Jewish
context and the writings of the New Testament present it as a messianic
designation ([Historical Understandings](#historical-understandings)). Jesus' own
defenses against accusations of equality further clarify that his sonship
involves submission to God's authority
([Defending Against Accusations of Equality](#defending-against-accusations-of-equality)).
Ultimately, the contrast between the titles "Son of God" and "Son of Man"
affirms his identity as the prophesied human representative of God
([Comparison with the Son of Man](#comparison-with-the-son-of-man)).
