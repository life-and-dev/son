# Jesus Claims to Be the Son of God

The title "Son of God" is one of the most prominent labels applied to Jesus in
the New Testament. While many modern readers immediately associate this term
with ontological divinity, its meaning in the first century was far more
nuanced and varied. By examining how this title was understood by Jewish and
Greco-Roman audiences, one can better appreciate the specific message the
Gospel writers intended to convey.

Jesus is identified as the "Son of God" throughout the Gospels. For example:

> The beginning of the gospel of **Jesus Christ, the Son of God**. — Mark 1:1
> (ESV)
>
> And I have seen and have borne witness that this *(Jesus)* is **the Son of
> God**. — John 1:34 (ESV)
>
> Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are
> not written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that
> **Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God**, and that by believing you may have
> life in his name. — John 20:31 (ESV)

## Pagan Understanding

Some argue that because Jesus is called "the Son of God," he must be divine.
This reasoning often stems from the fact that pagan cultures during the time
of Jesus believed the children of gods were themselves gods. In such contexts,
referring to someone as a "son of god" implied divinity. The Greeks were
familiar with demi-gods who appeared human but possessed divine nature.
Similarly, the Roman Empire promoted the belief that the Caesar was a god.
Consequently, each son of a Caesar was viewed as the next deity in succession.

However, the author of the book of Hebrews also used the phrase "the Son of God"
multiple times (Hebrews 4:14, 6:6, 7:3, 10:29). Since this book was written
for a Hebrew audience rather than a pagan one, the title must be understood
through a Jewish lens. To a Hebrew believer, the term did not inherently
signify a leader claiming to be ontologically divine.

## Same Essence

Some people incorrectly assume that a person can only be called a "son of God"
if he is made of the same essence as God.

The title "son of God" has nothing to do with a person's essence, but with his
relationship with his Father who is God. Therefore, God has many
["sons of God"](../../sons-of-god.md).

For example, if a white European family adopts a black African child, they do
not have to change his essence to be able to adopt him as a son in their
family.

[The term "son of God"](#new-testament) is also
[applied to people other than Jesus](../../sons-of-god.md)
(Matthew 5:9; Galatians 4:4-7; Romans 8:14,16-17; Ephesians 1:5;
2 Corinthians 6:18). This never refers to their essence. Instead, it
highlights their relationship with their Father God.

Jesus was not the first "son" who called God "my Father":

> I said, "How I would **set you among my sons**, and give you a pleasant land,
> a heritage most beautiful of all nations." And I thought you would call me,
> **"My Father,"** and would not turn from following me. Surely, as a
> treacherous wife leaves her husband, so have you been treacherous to me, O
> house of Israel, declares
> [the LORD](../../shema.md#god-in-the-old-testament). — Jeremiah 3:19-20 (ESV)
>
> **Have we not all one Father?** Has not one God created us? — Malachi 2:10
> (ESV)

## The Son of God and Son of Man

Numerous scriptures refer to Jesus as the "son of man"
(Matthew 8:20; 9:6; 10:23; 11:19; 12:8,40; 13:41; 16:13,27,28; 17:9,12,22;
18:11; 19:28; 20:18,28; 24:27,30,37,39,44; 25:13,31; 26:2,24,45,64;
Mark 2:10,28; 8:31,38; 9:9,12,31; 10:33,45; 13:26; 14:21,41,62; Luke 5:24;
6:5; 7:34; 9:22,26,44,56,58; 11:30; 12:8,10,40; 17:22,24,30; 18:8,31; 19:10;
21:27,36; 22:22,48,69; 24:7; John 1:51; 3:13,14; 5:27; 6:27,53,62; 8:28;
12:23,34; 13:31; Acts 7:56; Revelation 1:13; 14:14).

Trinitarians are quick to point out that this is proof that Jesus was both 100%
God and 100% human. However, this conclusion cannot be made without first
understanding the meaning of these two concepts.

### Old Testament

In the Hebrew Old Testament:

* "The Son of God" is never mentioned, except in
  [certain translations of Daniel 3:25](../../sons-of-god.md#daniel-325).
* "The son of man" is not used as a title. Instead, the phrase describes humans
  and highlights that God is not a human. Examples include Numbers 23:19;
  Job 25:6, 35:8; Psalm 8:4, 80:17, 144:3, 146:3; Isaiah 56:2;
  Jeremiah 49:18,33, 50:40; Ezekiel 2:1,3,6,8, 3:1,3,4,10,25; Daniel 8:17.

### New Testament

In the Greek New Testament:

* [The Son of God](../../sons-of-god.md) refers to someone considered to be
  God's son. This title does not inherently denote essence, divinity, or power.
  * It can refer to [beings created directly by God](../../sons-of-god.md),
    such as Adam or angels. In the case of Jesus, he was conceived in the womb
    of the virgin Mary by the [Holy Spirit](../../trinity/holy-spirit.md)
    without a human father.
  * It can also refer to those adopted by God. When God adopted Israel or
    believers as [sons of God](../../sons-of-god.md), they did not become
    divine beings.
* ["The son of man"](../../son-of-man/human.md) is a unique title for Jesus.
  An exception is [Hebrews 2:6-8](../../son-of-man/limitations.md), which
  quotes Psalm 8:4-6. Unlike the general Hebrew meaning, this title is specific
  to Jesus. It would be unnecessary for Jesus to prove he is human since his
  humanity was evident to all.
  * The title may refer to the
    [prophesied seed of Adam](../../son-of-man.md).
  * It is ultimately used in the context of the prophesied
    [Christ](../../son-of-man.md).

## Defense Against Ontological Claims

Critics often argue that Jesus' claim to be the "Son of God" was understood by
his contemporaries as a claim to be God himself. They frequently cite
John 10:33, where the Jews accused him of blasphemy because he, being a man,
made himself God. However, Jesus' own defense in the subsequent verses
(John 10:34-36) clarifies his position. He quoted Psalm 82:6, where human
judges are called "gods," to demonstrate that the title "Son of God" does not
necessitate ontological divinity. If those to whom the word of God came could
be called "gods," then his own use of the title was even more appropriate
given his sanctification and mission.

## Conclusion

The title "Son of God" as applied to Jesus emphasizes his unique relationship
with the Father and his role as the promised Messiah. It does not inherently
denote a divine nature, as the term was used in both
[pagan](#pagan-understanding) and [Jewish](#same-essence) contexts to refer to
beings or individuals with special status but not necessarily God's own
essence. Furthermore, Jesus' use of the title
[Son of Man](#the-son-of-god-and-son-of-man) highlights his fulfillment of
[Old Testament](#old-testament) and [New Testament](#new-testament)
prophecies regarding the Christ.
