# Jesus Claims to Be the Son of God

The title "Son of God" is one of the most prominent designations for Jesus in the
New Testament. While modern readers often equate this title with divinity, its
historical and biblical context suggests a more nuanced meaning. Exploring the
various ways this term was understood in the first century provides insight into
how the original audience would have perceived Jesus' identity.

The Gospels frequently identify Jesus as the Son of God. For instance, the
Gospel of Mark opens with this declaration:

> The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. — Mark 1:1 (ESV)

John the Baptist also provided eyewitness testimony to this identity:

> And I have seen and have borne witness that this is the Son of God.
> — John 1:34 (ESV)

Furthermore, the Gospel of John states that its entire purpose is to lead
readers to this specific belief:

> Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not
> written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that
> Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life
> in his name. — John 20:31 (ESV)

## Pagan Understanding

In the pagan cultures of the first century, the phrase "son of god" often
carried divine or semi-divine connotations. Greek and Roman mythology was filled
with stories of demigods born from the union of a god and a human. These beings
were typically viewed as possessing divine essence and extraordinary powers.
The Roman Empire even applied the title *Divi filius* (Son of the Divine) to
emperors like Augustus, suggesting a unique and superior status.

However, the author of the book of Hebrews, writing to a Jewish audience, used
the phrase "the Son of God" several times (Hebrews 4:14; 6:6; 7:3; 10:29). To a
Hebrew believer, this title did not inherently signify a being of divine
essence. It instead referred to a leader with a special relationship to God.

## Relationship Over Essence

A common misconception is that the title "Son of God" implies being made of the
same essence as God. In biblical terms, the title often focuses on a
relationship with the Father rather than ontological nature. God is described as
having many "sons" who are not considered divine in the same way He is.

For example, adoption into a family establishes a legal and relational bond as a
son without changing the child's physical DNA or ethnicity. Similarly, the term
"sons of God" is applied to various groups and individuals in Scripture to
highlight their relationship with God. This includes the peacemakers
(Matthew 5:9) and all Christian believers who are adopted into God's family
(Romans 8:14,16-17; 1 Corinthians 6:18; Galatians 4:4-7; Ephesians 1:5).

Jesus was not the only one in the Hebrew tradition to refer to God as "Father."
The Old Testament portrays God expressing a desire for His people to recognize
Him in this way:

> “I said, 'How I would set you among my sons, and give you a pleasant land, a
> heritage most beautiful of all nations.' And I thought you would call me,
> 'My Father,' and would not turn from following me. Surely, as a treacherous
> wife leaves her husband, so have you been treacherous to me, O house of
> Israel,” declares the LORD. — Jeremiah 3:19-20 (ESV)

The prophet Malachi also emphasized this common relationship:

> Have we not all one Father? Has not one God created us? — Malachi 2:10 (ESV)

## The Son of God and the Son of Man

Jesus often referred to himself as the "Son of Man," a title that appears
frequently across the Gospels. Trinitarian perspectives often view the dual
titles of "Son of God" and "Son of Man" as evidence that Jesus was both fully
God and fully man. However, a deeper look at the Old and New Testament meanings
of these terms suggests another perspective.

### Old Testament Context

In the Hebrew Old Testament, the title "the Son of God" is absent. Some
translations of Daniel 3:25 use the phrase. Yet, scholars note that the Aramaic
*bar elahin* is more accurately translated as "a son of the gods" or "a divine
being," referring to an angel.

The phrase "son of man" (*ben adam*) is commonly used in the Old Testament to
describe humans. It is often used in contrast to God's divine nature.
For example:

> God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his
> mind. — Numbers 23:19 (ESV)

### New Testament Application

In the Greek New Testament, "the Son of God" refers to a person considered God's
son. This could be by direct creation, like Adam or angels, or by adoption. In
the case of Jesus, he was conceived through the Holy Spirit.

The title "the Son of Man" takes on a unique and messianic meaning in the New
Testament when applied to Jesus. While the Hebrew phrase simply meant "human,"
Jesus' use of the title often points to the prophesied figure from Daniel 7 who
would receive an eternal kingdom. This title emphasizes his role as the Christ
who would save mankind.

The New Testament also uses "son of man" in quotations from the Old Testament.
For instance, Hebrews 2:6 quotes Psalm 8:4-6 to describe humanity's role in
creation. While some might interpret this as referring to Jesus, the context of
the Psalm is the status of mankind in general, which Jesus fulfills as the
representative human.

## Conclusion

The title "Son of God" does not necessarily demand a claim of ontological
divinity. In its Jewish context, it signifies a [unique relationship and a
messianic role](#relationship-over-essence). By understanding the distinction
between the [pagan interpretations of divine sonship](#pagan-understanding) and
the [biblical emphasis on relationship and
prophecy](#the-son-of-god-and-the-son-of-man), one can see that Jesus could be
both the Son of God and the Son of Man without being defined as God Himself.
