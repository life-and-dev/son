# Jesus Claims to Be the Son of Man

The title "Son of Man" appears more frequently in the Gospels than any other
designation for Jesus. While it sounds straightforward, its origins in the
Hebrew Scriptures suggest a much deeper significance than simple humanity.
Understanding why Jesus chose this specific title requires looking back at an
ancient vision that shaped the expectations of his first-century audience.

## Daniel's Vision of the Son of Man

The most prominent Old Testament source for this title is found in the book of
Daniel:

> I saw in the night visions, and behold, with the clouds of heaven there came
> one like **a son of man**, and he came to the Ancient of Days
> and was presented before him. — Daniel 7:13 (ESV)

Jesus referred to himself as "the son of man" on numerous occasions throughout
the Gospels. Notable examples include Matthew 8:20, 9:6, 10:23, 11:19, 12:8,40,
13:41, 16:13,27-28, 17:9,12,22, 18:11(KJV), 19:28, 20:18,28, 24:27,30,37,39,44,
25:13(KJV),31, 26:2,24,45(KJV),64; Mark 2:10,28, 8:31,38, 9:9,12,31, 10:33,45,
13:26, 14:21,41,62; Luke 5:24, 6:5, 7:34, 9:22,26,44,56(KJV),58(KJV), 11:30,
12:8,10,40, 17:22,24,30, 18:8,31, 19:10, 21:27,36, 22:22,48,69, 24:7; John 1:51,
3:13-14, 5:27, 6:27,53,62, 8:28, 12:23,34, 13:31. The title also appears in
Acts 7:56 and Revelation 1:13, 14:14.

## Trinitarian Interpretations

[Trinitarians][trinity] often reason that this
[prophecy about Jesus][dan-prophecy] implies several key theological points:

1. Jesus pre-existed in the form that Daniel witnessed in his vision.
2. The identification of Jesus as "the son of man" in the presence of "the
   Ancient of Days" signifies his divinity.
3. When Jesus uses this title, he is specifically highlighting his human nature
   as part of the hypostatic union.

One of the strongest arguments for the divinity of the Son of Man comes from the
verse immediately following Daniel's vision:

> And to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples,
> nations, and languages should serve him; his dominion is an everlasting
> dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be
> destroyed. — Daniel 7:14 (ESV)

The Greek Septuagint (LXX) uses the word *latreuousin* for "serve," a term
typically reserved for the worship of God. This suggests that the Son of Man
receives religious service that belongs to the divine.

## Analysis of the Prophetic Claim

Several observations challenge these conclusions and provide a defense for a
non-ontological interpretation:

1. Prophets like Daniel often witnessed future events as if they were occurring
   in the present. For example, see Daniel's other prophecies. The vision does
   not necessarily require the literal pre-existence of the individual seen.
2. In the vision, the "one like a son of man" is clearly distinct from "the
   Ancient of Days." They are presented as two separate entities rather than
   [the same God][similarity].
3. Regarding the word "serve" in Daniel 7:14, the textual evidence is nuanced.
   While the Old Greek (LXX) uses *latreuousin*, the version by Theodotion,
   which is the basis for many modern translations, uses *douleuousin*. This
   term denotes general service or obedience rather than exclusively divine
   worship. Scholarly discussions often highlight this [textual
   variation][dan-714-variation].
4. First-century Jewish expectations for the Messiah (Christ) centered on a
   [human figure][son-of-man] who would fulfill the prophetic requirements.

## Conclusion

The title "Son of Man" links Jesus directly to the Messianic figure seen in
[Daniel's vision][dan-vision], representing authority and a future kingdom.
While [Trinitarian views][trin-interp] see this as proof of divinity and
pre-existence, an [analysis of the prophecy][analysis] suggests the title
emphasizes a human Messiah who is distinct from God.
