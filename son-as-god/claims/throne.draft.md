# Sitting on the Throne of Glory

The biblical description of Jesus sitting on a glorious throne is often used to
support the argument for his divinity. However, a broader examination of
scripture reveals that sitting on a throne, even the throne of God, is not an
honor exclusive to God alone. This article explores how the Bible uses throne
imagery for Jesus, the apostles, and other figures to understand what it
signifies about their nature and authority.

## The Son of Man on His Throne

Jesus frequently spoke of a future time when he would be seated in a position of
supreme authority.

> When the Son of Man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he
> will sit on his glorious throne. — Matthew 25:31 (ESV)

This verse depicts Jesus in a role of judgment and dominion. While this
indicates a high status, the context of the New Testament often clarifies that
this authority is [delegated to him by the
Father](../../son-of-man/limitations.md#jesus-does-not-have-the-highest-authority).

## Believers Shared in the Throne

A key counter-argument to the idea that sitting on a throne proves divinity is
that Jesus promised his followers a similar honor. If sitting on a throne made
one God, then the apostles and all overcomers would also be God.

> Jesus said to them, “Truly, I say to you, in the new world, when the Son of
> Man will sit on his glorious throne, **you who have followed me will also sit
> on twelve thrones**, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.”
> — Matthew 19:28 (ESV)

And

> The one who conquers, I will grant him to **sit with me on my throne**, as I
> also conquered and sat down with my Father on his throne.
> — Revelation 3:21 (ESV)

This indicates that the throne is a symbol of shared authority and reward for
faithfulness rather than an indicator of divine essence. Believers are made to
be a kingdom and priests to God, yet they remain distinct from God (Revelation
1:6).

## The Elders and the Lamb

In the vision of the heavenly throne room, others are also seated on thrones.

> Around the throne were twenty-four thrones, and seated on the thrones were
> twenty-four elders, clothed in white garments, with golden crowns on their
> heads. — Revelation 4:4 (ESV)

These elders are distinct from God, who is seated on the central throne. They
acknowledge God's unique sovereignty by casting their crowns before Him.

> ... the twenty-four elders fall down before him who is seated on the throne
> and worship him who lives forever and ever. They cast their crowns before the
> throne, saying, "Worthy are you, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor
> and power..." — Revelation 4:10-11 (ESV)

Even the Lamb, representing Jesus, is seen in relation to the throne but is
distinguished from the "One seated on the throne" (Revelation 5:6-7). This
distinction is consistent throughout the book of Revelation, where [Jesus is the
faithful witness](../../son-of-man/prophecies.md) and the Lamb who was slain,
while God is the one who lives forever and ever.

## The Throne of the LORD in the Old Testament

The concept of a human sitting on "the throne of the LORD" is not unique to the
New Testament. In the Old Testament, the earthly kings of Israel were said to
sit on God's throne as His representatives.

> Then Solomon sat on the **throne of the LORD** as king in place of David his
> father. And he prospered, and all Israel obeyed him.
> — 1 Chronicles 29:23 (ESV)

Solomon was not God, yet he sat on the throne of the LORD. This demonstrates
that the "throne of the LORD" refers to the seat of authority over God's kingdom
on earth, which God permits human agents to occupy.

## Addressing the Divine Claim

Critics of this view argue that Jesus' throne is uniquely divine because he is
worshiped while on it. However, the worship described in heaven often
distinguishes between the one on the throne (God) and the Lamb. While both
receive honor, they are honored for different reasons: God for His creation and
eternal existence (Revelation 4:11), and the Lamb for his sacrifice (Revelation
5:9). Furthermore, the word for worship often used in these contexts can refer
to the [homage paid to a superior or a king](../../serve.draft.md).

## Conclusion

The evidence from both the Old and New Testaments suggests that sitting on a
throne, even one described as God's throne, signifies [delegated
authority](#the-throne-of-the-lord-in-the-old-testament) and [shared
honor](#believers-shared-in-the-throne) rather than inherent divinity. Jesus
occupies the throne because he conquered and was exalted by the Father, just as
he promises that [believers will share in his
throne](#believers-shared-in-the-throne) for their own faithfulness.
