# Is Jesus Eternal?

Revelation’s titles for Jesus are weighty, but they must be read alongside its distinction between God and the risen Messiah.

> “I am the **first and the last**, and the living one. I **died**, and behold I am alive forevermore.” — Revelation 1:17-18 (ESV)

Trinitarians reasonably connect this title with Isaiah 44:6, where the LORD alone is first and last. The Unitarian case is cumulative: Revelation identifies Jesus as the one who died, God as his God and Father, and Jesus as firstborn from the dead (Revelation 1:5-6; 3:12). God’s exaltation of the Messiah can explain the shared royal language without making the Messiah the LORD. See also [Lord](lord.draft.md#philippians-29-11) and [Jesus Was Resurrected](jesus-was-resurrected.draft.md#jesus-resurrection).

*The title is significant evidence for Trinitarian divine-identity claims, but its meaning remains disputed; it is not a standalone statement that Jesus is eternal.*

## Contextual Distinction

The parallel with Isaiah is strongest when the same title is treated as the same identity. Revelation itself supplies a different marker: Jesus is the one who “died” and whom God raised, whereas YHVH is the living God who gives life (Revelation 1:17-18; Acts 2:24,32). A shared royal title can express the Messiah’s final, God-given authority without erasing this distinction. Revelation repeatedly calls God the one “who is and who was and who is to come” and calls Jesus the faithful witness and firstborn from the dead (Revelation 1:4-5).

# The Alpha and Omega

“Alpha and Omega” occurs in Revelation 1:8; 21:6; and 22:13. Revelation 1:8 explicitly identifies its speaker as “the Lord God” and “the Almighty.” Revelation 22:12-13 is debated because the speaker changes around the passage and Jesus is named in 22:16.

Trinitarians therefore see the title, together with “First and Last,” as divine-identity language for Jesus. The Unitarian response should not rely on Alpha and Omega being Greek rather than Hebrew: John’s Greek allusion can intentionally evoke Isaiah’s LORD language. Instead, it notes Revelation’s continuing distinction between God and the Lamb, including Jesus’ reference to “my God” (Revelation 3:12).

*The title is important but its attribution in every occurrence is disputed; Revelation’s God-and-Lamb distinction remains part of the cumulative case.*

# The Aleph-Tav

Some believe the Aleph-Tav means "Jesus" because:
* John of Patmos revealed that [Jesus is the "first and last"](#the-first-and-the-last) and they reason this is a Greek transliteration of the first and last letters of the Hebrew alphabet.
* The symbols look like the Christian cross to some which they assume refers to Jesus' "crucifixion".

## Why Jesus?

This is important to some people, because Jesus name is nowhere to be found in the Old Testament, yet to support the pre-existence doctrine, Jesus need to be found somewhere in the Old Testament.

However, one need to be very careful with these claims as it completely changes the meaning of many scriptures. 

Those who believe Jesus is God would also quote Genesis 1:1 and state that the "God אֵ֥ת" was in the beginning the Creator, which they reason means Jesus was the Creator.

The Arians and Jehovah Witnesses use the Aleph-Tav as proof in Genesis 1:1 to proof that God first created was the "Aleph-Tav" (Jesus), then the heaven, then the earth.

According to the Hebrew text of Genesis 1:1:

> בְּרֵאשִׁ֖ית בָּרָ֣א אֱלֹהִ֑ים **אֵ֥ת** הַשָּׁמַ֖יִם וְאֵ֥ת הָאָֽרֶץ׃

directly translates to the English:

[first / beginning] [create] [God] **אֵ֥ת** [heaven / sky] [earth / land]

## Why Aleph-Tav is not Jesus

The Hebrew direct-object marker *’et* occurs with many objects and persons, including livestock, animals, idols, cities, Adam, Eve, Cain, Noah, Abraham’s family, Israel, and Israel’s enemies. Its occurrence therefore does not identify its object as Jesus.

Adam is one of these persons (Genesis 1:27; 2:7-8,15). Paul contrasts the first Adam with Christ, whom he calls the “last Adam” (1 Corinthians 15:45). That contrast distinguishes their roles without denying Adam-Christ typology.

Although these examples are incomplete, they show that *’et* is an ordinary grammatical marker. Its presence alone does not establish that it means Jesus or refers to Jesus.

# Begotten son

John calls Jesus the "only begotten Son":

> The only **begotten** son, who is in the bosom of the Father, He has declared him. -- John 1:18 (NKJV)

The Greek term [*monogenēs*](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/g3439/kjv/tr/0-1/) has been understood in more than one way. Older English translations often render it "only begotten", while many modern translations render it "only" or "one and only". The word by itself does not settle whether the Son had a beginning.

The strongest Trinitarian view is that the Son is eternally begotten. In this view, "begotten" describes the Son's eternal relationship to the Father, not an event before which he did not exist. This historic doctrine seeks to preserve both the Father's unique relation to the Son and the Son's eternal existence.

That explanation avoids treating "begotten" as a temporal beginning, but it depends on the doctrine of eternal generation. John 1:18 does not explicitly state that the begetting is eternal. Matthew and Paul instead speak directly of Jesus' human birth:

> **Now the birth of Jesus Christ was as follows**: After His mother Mary was betrothed to Joseph, before they came together, she was found with child of the Holy Spirit. -- Matthew 1:18 (NKJV)

> But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth His son, **born of woman**... -- Galatians 4:4 (ESV)

These passages plainly affirm that Jesus was born of Mary. They do not themselves establish eternal generation or a prior heavenly existence. Matthew does not mention pre-existence or [reincarnation of Jesus](/god/son/essence/as-god/incarnation.md).
