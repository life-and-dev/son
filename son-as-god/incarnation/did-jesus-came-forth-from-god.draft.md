## The One God and His Messiah

The Old Testament identifies the LORD as the one God of Israel. “Hear, O Israel: **The LORD** our God, **the LORD is one**” (Deuteronomy 6:4, ESV). Isaiah likewise records the LORD’s exclusive claim: “I am the LORD, and there is no other, besides me there is no God” (Isaiah 45:5, ESV). These declarations establish the biblical starting point. God Almighty is not one member of a class of gods.

The same Scriptures distinguish the LORD from the Messiah whom he appoints. “The **LORD** says to **my Lord**: ‘Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool’” (Psalm 110:1, ESV). The Messiah is honoured at the LORD’s right hand and rules by the LORD’s grant. He is not portrayed as the LORD who gives him that place. Isaiah also describes God’s chosen and upheld servant as “my servant” (Isaiah 42:1, ESV).

*This distinction supplies a positive framework for reading the New Testament.* It does not deny Jesus’ unique authority or glory. It identifies him as the human king and servant through whom the one God acts. Personal distinction, by itself, would not disprove a claim of deity. The Old Testament’s God-and-Messiah pattern, however, makes a distinction of roles and source central to the biblical account.

## Jesus and the God Who Sent Him

The apostles retain this pattern. Peter calls Jesus “a man attested to you by God” and says that “God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified” (Acts 2:22,36, ESV). Paul writes, “For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). Jesus’ lordship is real, but these statements identify the one God as the one who attests, raises, exalts, and appoints Jesus.

John uses the same distinction. Jesus says that the Father is “the **only true God**” and identifies himself as the one whom the Father sent (John 17:3, ESV). After his resurrection, Jesus says, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (John 20:17, ESV). *These texts positively identify the Father as Jesus’ God and as the source of Jesus’ mission.* They therefore weigh against identifying Jesus with God Almighty.

This is not merely an argument from omitted words. The New Testament repeatedly presents authority as given to Jesus: the Father gives him works, teaching, judgment, disciples, and authority (John 5:19-27; 17:2). God then exalts the obedient Jesus and gives him the name above every name (Philippians 2:8-11). *The recurring direction is from God to his Messiah, not from one equal divine person to another.*

Revelation confirms this distinction without being its sole basis. The speaker who is “the Almighty” identifies himself as “the Lord God” (Revelation 1:8). The risen Jesus still speaks of “my God” (Revelation 3:12). *The titles and relations remain differentiated even in the book’s visions of Jesus’ glory.*

## The Movement in John 16:28

> “I **came from** the Father and have **come into** the world, and now I am **leaving** the world and **going to** the Father.” — John 16:28 (ESV)

Jesus gives one connected sequence: origin with the Father, entry into the world, departure from the world, and return to the Father. The final movement is not a return to ordinary earthly life. In the surrounding discourse, Jesus completes work given by the Father and goes to the Father (John 14:12,28; 17:4-5).

The natural reading is personal pre-existence: Jesus existed with the Father before entering the world and, after his earthly mission, returned to the Father. A Unitarian reading should admit that force. Yet the verse still distinguishes Jesus from the Father as the source and destination of his movement. John 17:3 calls the Father the only true God, and John 20:17 calls the Father Jesus’ God. *Personal pre-existence would be significant, but it is not identical with deity or identity as God Almighty.*

## Mission Language and Its Limits

John joins Jesus’ coming with the Father’s sending: “I **came from** God and I am here. I **came** not of my own accord, but he sent me” (John 8:42, ESV). In John 16:28, however, sending language does not erase the full origin, world-entry, departure, and return sequence. That sequence is why personal pre-existence is the most natural reading.

The warranted Unitarian inference is narrower. John presents the Father as the one who sends, gives authority, receives the Son, and remains the only true God (John 17:1-3; 20:17). Jesus’ personal pre-existence, if granted, would describe his relation to God before his human life; it would not by itself establish that he shares the Father’s identity as God Almighty. ## Conclusion

John 16:28 presents Jesus’ movement from the Father into the world and back to the Father. [The Old Testament](#the-one-god-and-his-messiah) identifies the LORD as the one God and distinguishes his Messiah; [the New Testament](#jesus-and-the-god-who-sent-him) presents Jesus as the man whom God sends, authorises, and exalts. [A literal pre-existence reading](#the-movement-in-john-1628) remains possible, but it does not establish that Jesus is God Almighty or the Father. [John’s mission language](#mission-language-and-its-limits) reinforces the Father’s initiative without settling every pre-existence question.

*The warranted conclusion is that Jesus came from God as God’s unique Son and Messiah, but John 16:28 does not identify him as God Almighty or God the Father.* Its sequence gives the personal-pre-existence reading real force; mission parallels only show why the wording is not self-interpreting.
