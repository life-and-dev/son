## The Biblical Starting Point

### The LORD and His Messiah

The Old Testament identifies the LORD as the one God. “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is **one**” (Deuteronomy 6:4). The LORD says, “I am the LORD; that is my name; my **glory** I give to no other” (Isaiah 42:8). Yet the same Scriptures promise a human, anointed ruler. The LORD says to David’s lord, “Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool” (Psalm 110:1). The one at the LORD’s right hand receives authority from the LORD. He is not thereby the LORD who grants it.

Isaiah calls this promised figure the LORD’s **servant**, one whom the LORD upholds and in whom he delights (Isaiah 42:1; 52:13; 53:11). God’s Messiah is therefore a distinct, commissioned person. *Personal distinction by itself does not disprove deity; the point is that the Scriptures positively describe the Messiah as the LORD’s servant and ruler by appointment.*

### Jesus’ God and Authority

John keeps that distinction in view. Jesus says, “my Father … is the one of whom you say, ‘He is **our God**’” (John 8:54). After his resurrection he says, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (John 20:17). He also calls the Father “the only true God” and himself the one sent by that God (John 17:3).

The apostles likewise proclaim that “God has made him both **Lord** and Christ” (Acts 2:36), and that “the God of our fathers raised Jesus” (Acts 5:30). These are affirmative claims about Jesus’ exalted messianic office. They identify God as the one who sends, raises, and appoints Jesus. They supply the controlling distinction when a difficult saying of Jesus is considered.

## Abraham and the Messiah’s Day

God promised Abraham that “in your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed” (Genesis 22:18; see also Genesis 12:3). Paul identifies that promised offspring as Christ (Galatians 3:16). Abraham’s hope was therefore fixed on the future blessing God would bring through the Messiah.

That promise frames John 8. Jesus says, “Your father Abraham rejoiced that he would see my **day**. He saw it and was glad” (John 8:56). His opponents answer from ordinary chronology: “You are not yet fifty years old, and have you seen Abraham?” (John 8:57). Jesus’ answer must be heard within this argument about Abraham, God’s promise, and the Messiah’s day. *A promised-Messiah reading is an inference from that context, not a lexical definition of the Greek words in verse 58.*

## John 8:58 in Context

> “Truly, truly, I say to you, **before Abraham was, I am**.” — John 8:58 (ESV)

### The Strongest Pre-existence Reading

The strongest Trinitarian reading should be taken seriously. The question in John 8:57 is how Jesus could relate to Abraham, who lived centuries earlier. Verse 58 contrasts Abraham’s coming to be with Jesus’ **I am**. Read as a direct answer, it naturally claims that Jesus personally existed before Abraham. Some also hear an allusion to God’s self-revelation in Exodus 3:14. On that reading, the words and the attempted stoning in verse 59 point to divine identity, not merely messianic status.

A contextual Unitarian reading begins with the exchange about Abraham seeing Jesus’ day, the promised offspring, and Jesus’ relation to the Father whom he calls “our God” (John 8:48-57). It can understand Jesus as speaking from the priority and certainty of God’s messianic purpose. That preserves John’s Father-Son distinction and the Abraham-promise context. But it is a less-direct theological reconstruction than the personal-pre-existence reading: it must explain why Jesus answers a chronological question with **egō eimi** rather than language explicitly naming promise or foreordination. The contextual reading therefore remains possible, not equally straightforward grammar.

### **Before Abraham Was** / **I Am**

The [THGNT display of John 8:58](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=THGNT%7Creference=John.8.58) reads *prin Habraam genesthai egō eimi* (πρὶν Ἁβραὰμ γενέσθαι ἐγώ εἰμι): “before Abraham came to be, I am.” The contrast is real: Abraham **came to be** (*genesthai*), whereas Jesus says **I am** (*egō eimi*). That contrast gives the personal-pre-existence reading its natural force. Grammar does not by itself identify Jesus as God Almighty, but a foreordination reading is not an equally direct grammatical sense; it is a contextual theological account of how the Messiah can be prior in God’s purpose.

The expression *egō eimi* is commonly translated “I am” or “I am he,” depending on context. John uses it when Jesus identifies himself to the Samaritan woman (John 4:26) and when the man healed by Jesus identifies himself (John 9:9). Those uses do not remove John 8:58’s unusual temporal contrast. They show only that the words themselves are not an exclusive divine name. STEP is used here as a readable primary-text display, not as linguistic authority.

### Exodus 3:14 and the LXX

Exodus 3:14 identifies the speaker as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the LORD who sends Moses (Exodus 3:6,14-15). The [LXX display of Exodus 3:14](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=LXX%7Creference=Exod.3.14) reads *Egō eimi ho ōn* (Ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ὤν) and later *Ho ōn* (Ὁ ὢν). It is commonly rendered, **non-ESV wording**, “I am the One who is.” John 8:58 has *egō eimi*, but not *ho ōn*.

The wording differs, so John 8:58 is not a verbal repetition of the Septuagint phrase. But an allusion need not be verbatim to be an allusion. The difference therefore does not rule out a proposed Exodus echo. Neither does the shorter phrase prove that Jesus has claimed the identity of the LORD. STEP is used here as a readable primary-text display, not as linguistic authority. The case for an allusion must rest on John’s wider context as well as on the words themselves.

### The Reaction and the Full Context

After Jesus speaks, his opponents pick up stones (John 8:59). Their reaction shows that they heard an extraordinary and offensive claim. John does not state their reason in this verse. Earlier in the same exchange they call Jesus a Samaritan and say that he has a demon (John 8:48,52). Their hostile response cannot by itself settle what Jesus actually claimed.

John later reports an explicit charge: “you, being a man, make yourself **God**” (John 10:33). Jesus answers by distinguishing “the Father” from “him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world,” and calls himself God’s **Son** (John 10:36). That later scene confirms that John can present accusation, response, and messianic sonship without simply equating Jesus with the Father. It supports reading John 8 within John’s repeated Father-Son distinction.

## What Pre-existence Could and Could Not Establish

John 8:58 can be read as personal pre-existence. A reader may make that concession without conceding that Jesus is God Almighty. Pre-existence, if established, would say that Jesus existed before Abraham. It would not by itself say that he is uncreated, self-existent, the one God, or the Father whom Jesus calls “my God.” Those further claims require their own evidence.

The contextual alternative is that Jesus speaks of the Messiah’s priority in God’s promise and purpose. Scripture uses such pre-creation language for what God has prepared: believers were chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world, and grace was given in Christ Jesus before the ages began (Ephesians 1:4; 2 Timothy 1:9). Peter says Christ was foreknown before the foundation of the world and made manifest in the last times (1 Peter 1:20). These texts do not make believers personally alive before creation. They show that God’s purpose can be spoken of as real before historical fulfilment.

*John 8:58 does not explicitly explain that Jesus exists only in this purposive sense.* Its immediate focus on Abraham’s promised offspring makes that sense coherent, while the Gospel’s repeated distinction between God and his Messiah makes it fitting. The verse therefore does not warrant identifying Jesus as God Almighty or as the Father. The positive biblical case is that the LORD is the one God, and Jesus is his promised, sent, raised, and exalted Messiah.

## Conclusion

The Old Testament presents the LORD as the one God and the Messiah as his appointed servant and ruler. [John’s own Father-Son distinction](#jesus-god-and-authority) remains necessary for reading John 8:58. The saying can support personal pre-existence, but it does not identify Jesus as God Almighty, and the [Abraham-promise context](#abraham-and-the-messiahs-day) supports a messianic-contextual alternative.

*John 8:58 is unusually forceful evidence for personal pre-existence; its grammar does not prove foreordination, and its wording alone does not prove identity with the God of Exodus.* Exodus 3:14 names the LORD who sends Moses, while its Greek wording includes **ho ōn**, absent from John 8:58.
