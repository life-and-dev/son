# Does God Have Blood?

Blood belongs to mortal embodied life: “the life of the flesh is in the blood” (Leviticus 17:11, ESV). Scripture says that God is spirit (John 4:24), is immortal (1 Timothy 1:17), and is not a man (Numbers 23:19). Jesus, by contrast, says after his resurrection that a spirit does not have flesh and bones as he has (Luke 24:39). The ordinary scriptural answer is therefore no: God Almighty does not have blood, while the genuinely human Jesus did.

Acts 20:28 appears to challenge that answer because many translations say that God obtained the church “with his own blood.” The verse deserves careful treatment. It contains both a manuscript variant and a grammatical ambiguity, and neither should be presented as if scholars or manuscripts remove every doubt.

## The wording of Acts 20:28

> “Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to care for the church of **God**, which he obtained with his own blood.” — Acts 20:28 (ESV)

The strongest Trinitarian reading is direct: **God** is the nearest possessor, so “his own blood” means God’s blood. A two-natures theology does not usually claim that the Father bled or that the divine nature consists of flesh. It says that the one person of God the Son truly shed blood according to the human nature he assumed. On that account, properties of Christ’s humanity may be spoken of the divine person.

That response is coherent within Trinitarian doctrine. Acts 20:28 itself, however, does not mention an incarnation, a divine Son, two natures, or the communication of properties. Those ideas are brought to the text to explain its unusual wording. The verse’s actual textual and grammatical details permit another reading.

## “God” or “Lord”?

Ancient witnesses differ between “the church of **God**” and “the church of the **Lord**”; later witnesses also preserve a combined reading. The [NET Bible textual note on Acts 20:28](https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Act&chapter=20&verse=28) describes the external evidence as evenly balanced and prefers **God** on internal grounds. This means that “God” has serious manuscript support and may well be original. It also means that the variation is real and should not be hidden.

The “Lord” reading would naturally direct the blood language to Jesus. The “God” reading still does not require God Almighty to possess blood, because the following phrase has its own ambiguity.

## “His own blood” or “the blood of his own one”?

The Greek construction can be read in two ways: God acquired the church “through his own blood,” or God acquired it “through the blood of his own [one].” In the second reading, the supplied “one,” often completed as “Son,” is a contextual completion, not a word guaranteed by the Greek text. The [NET note](https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Act&chapter=20&verse=28) records both options and adopts “the blood of his own Son” in its translation.

This ellipsis is a legitimate possibility, not a certainty. Romans 8:32 supplies a close conceptual parallel by saying that God “did not spare **his own Son** but gave him up for us all” (ESV). On that reading, God obtained the church through the blood of one who uniquely belongs to him.

Even the ESV’s surface wording can be understood as compressed agency language: the blood God provided and accepted for his saving work is called “his own” because it is the blood of his own Messiah. Possession need not mean that blood is a constituent of God’s immortal being.

## God and Jesus in Acts

Acts repeatedly identifies who died and who raised him. Jesus is “a man attested to you by God”; lawless men crucify and kill him; “God raised him up” (Acts 2:22–24, ESV). God then makes this Jesus “both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36). Peter later says, “The God of our fathers raised Jesus, whom you killed by hanging him on a tree” and exalted him as Savior (Acts 5:30–31, ESV). God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and power (Acts 10:38).

These are not merely statements that Jesus differs from the Father. Mainstream Trinity already affirms that difference. They form a positive agency pattern: the one God empowers, raises, exalts, and saves through the human Messiah. Within Acts, “his own [Son]” therefore has an available referent, and the church’s purchase can be God’s act accomplished through Jesus’ blood.

Other New Testament writers speak more explicitly. God put Christ forward “as a propitiation by his blood” (Romans 3:25, ESV). Christ “offered himself without blemish to God” (Hebrews 9:14, ESV). In Revelation the slain Lamb’s blood ransoms people “for God” (Revelation 5:9, ESV). The blood belongs to Christ; the saving plan, acceptance, and redeemed people belong to God.

## The strongest objection

A Trinitarian can answer that this distinction is fully compatible with the incarnation: the Father raises the Son, while the Son is also divine; the person who sheds human blood may therefore be called God. The “church of God” reading plus “his own blood” is genuine evidence for that position and should not be dismissed as an obvious mistranslation.

The limitation is equally important. To make the verse prove that God has blood, one must select **God** over a genuine **Lord** variant, select “his own blood” over a recognized ellipsis, and then interpret the resulting expression through a two-natures model not stated in the passage. That cumulative case is possible, but it is not textually or grammatically unavoidable. The broad New Testament pattern names Jesus’ blood as the human offering made to God.

## A bounded conclusion

Acts 20:28 may originally say “church of God,” and its shortest reading may be rendered “his own blood.” Neither concession establishes that God Almighty has blood. A live manuscript variant reads “Lord,” and a recognised grammatical reading supplies “his own [Son].” More decisively, Acts distinguishes the man who died from the God who raised and exalted him, while Hebrews and Revelation present Christ’s blood as an offering to God and a ransom for God.

*The best-supported Unitarian conclusion is that God obtained the church through the real human blood of his own Messiah.* The God/Lord manuscript question and the **tou idiou** ambiguity are separate issues; God’s incorporeality does not prove either reading.
