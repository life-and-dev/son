# Did God Almighty Become Jesus?

John does not use the word *incarnation*, but John 1:14 gives the doctrine its classic wording: “And the **Word became flesh** and dwelt among us” (ESV). The central question is what John means by **the Word** and by its becoming flesh. Does an eternal divine person change into a human being, or does God’s preexistent word, wisdom, and saving purpose come to embodied expression in the man Jesus?

*The second reading better preserves the Bible’s repeated distinction between the one God and the Messiah whom he sends, empowers, raises, and exalts.* This is more than arguing that Jesus is not the Father, since mainstream Trinitarian doctrine already distinguishes Father and Son. The positive Unitarian claim is that Jesus is God’s genuinely human Son and definitive agent—the one in whom God’s word becomes a human life—not God Almighty himself.

## God’s word before John

John opens with language rooted in Israel’s Scriptures: “In the beginning was the **Word**, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God” (John 1:1, ESV). Before deciding that **the Word** is a second divine person, the Old Testament pattern deserves priority.

God creates by speaking: “By the **word of the LORD** the heavens were made” (Psalm 33:6, ESV; see Genesis 1:3). His word can be sent to heal (Psalm 107:20), and it accomplishes the purpose for which he sends it (Isaiah 55:10–11). Proverbs presents God’s wisdom speaking, calling, dwelling with prudence, and standing beside God before creation (Proverbs 8:1, 12, 22–31). This is vivid personification: God’s own wisdom is portrayed as a speaker and worker without requiring another Almighty alongside him.

Ezekiel supplies a related prophetic pattern. God commands the prophet to speak his word to dry bones; God’s breath then gives them life (Ezekiel 37:4–14). The word is active because God acts through it. Against this scriptural background, John can present God’s word as existing “in the beginning,” sharing God’s own divine character, and mediating creation without first identifying a second person named Jesus.

This background does not prove that John’s Word is impersonal. Nor does the Greek word *logos* by itself decide whether personal preexistence is intended. *It does show that a non-Trinitarian reading is not created merely to avoid John’s language:* Scripture already speaks of God’s word and wisdom as preexistent, active, and personally portrayed.

## “The Word became flesh”

John 1:14 does not say, “God Almighty became Jesus,” “the Father became the Son,” or “the Word took on a second nature.” It says that **the Word became flesh**. In biblical usage, “flesh” can describe mortal human life (Isaiah 40:6; John 17:2). John’s claim reaches its climax in a real human being: God’s creative speech, wisdom, light, and saving purpose are now expressed in the life of Jesus.

Luke describes the same beginning from another angle. Gabriel does not tell Mary that an eternal person will pass through her. He says that the Holy Spirit will come upon her and the power of the Most High will overshadow her; “**therefore** the child to be born will be called holy—the Son of God” (Luke 1:35, ESV). Luke thus grounds the Son’s human life and title in God’s miraculous action. Jesus later says that his teaching is not his own but belongs to the one who sent him (John 7:16), and that the Father who dwells in him does his works (John 14:10). These statements fit the Word becoming flesh as God’s self-expression taking complete human form in his Messiah.

Hebrews also distinguishes the God who speaks from the Son through whom he now speaks: “Long ago, at many times and in many ways, **God spoke** to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days **he has spoken to us by his Son**” (Hebrews 1:1–2, ESV). Hebrews immediately gives the Son the highest representative language—radiance of God’s glory and exact imprint of his nature (Hebrews 1:3)—yet an imprint represents its source. Revelation finally calls the exalted Jesus “The **Word of God**” (Revelation 19:13, ESV). That title confirms that Jesus fully embodies and executes God’s word; it does not identify him as the God whose word he bears.

## The strongest personal-preexistence reading

The Trinitarian reading has substantial textual grounds. John says that the Word was “with God” and “was God,” that all things came into being through him, and that the Word became flesh (John 1:1–3, 14). John then speaks of the Son’s glory with the Father before the world existed (John 17:5). Read together, these statements can be understood as an eternal divine person entering human existence. Trinitarian theology does not normally claim that the Father became Jesus or that the divine nature turned into blood and tissue. It says that the eternal Son remained divine while assuming a complete human nature.

That account is coherent on its own terms, and Jesus’ distinction from the Father does not by itself disprove it. Yet it depends on reading later personal identity back into every occurrence of **the Word** in the prologue and on using the later formula of two natures to reconcile texts that present Jesus as dependent on God. John 1 never states that formula.

The prologue itself repeatedly names God as the source. John the Baptist is “sent from God” (John 1:6). Believers become God’s children by God’s will (John 1:12–13). Grace and truth come through Jesus Christ (John 1:17), and the Son makes the unseen God known (John 1:18). Jesus is therefore not a rival source beside God; he is the unique human disclosure of the God no one has seen.

John’s Gospel keeps that relation explicit. Jesus is a man who has told the truth that he heard from God (John 8:40). The Son can do nothing from himself but acts according to the Father (John 5:19, 30). He calls the Father “the only true God” and himself the one whom that God sent (John 17:3). After the resurrection, he speaks of “my God and your God” (John 20:17). These are positive reasons to understand John 1 through agency and embodiment, not merely isolated statements that Father and Son differ.

## A bounded conclusion

John 1 teaches that God’s Word was present in the beginning and that all creation came through it. That is pre-existence of God’s own Word, wisdom, and purpose. The disputed question is whether John thereby identifies that Word with Jesus as a personally existing second divine person before his birth. The Old Testament’s word-and-wisdom pattern, Luke’s account of Jesus’ conception, Hebrews’ distinction between God and the Son through whom God speaks, and John’s own agency language provide a coherent alternative.

*On balance, “the Word became flesh” is best read as God’s eternal Word, wisdom, and purpose becoming embodied in Jesus, God’s human Messiah.* This conclusion does not pretend that one Greek term settles the issue or that every personal-pre-existence text is undisputed. It establishes that John can proclaim Jesus as God’s definitive self-revelation without identifying him as God Almighty.
