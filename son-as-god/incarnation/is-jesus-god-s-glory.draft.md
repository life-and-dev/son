# Is Jesus God’s Glory?

Scripture gives Jesus a uniquely high place. It calls him the radiance of God’s glory, God’s exact imprint, God’s image, the exalted Son of Man, and the Lord at God’s right hand. Those descriptions deserve their full force. The question is whether they identify Jesus as God Almighty or as the Father, or whether they describe God’s perfect and appointed Messiah.

## The LORD’s Glory and His Servant

The Old Testament begins with the LORD’s own claim to glory. In Isaiah 42, the LORD introduces his chosen servant and then guards his unique name and glory:

> “Behold my **servant**, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my Spirit upon him; he will bring forth justice to the nations.” — Isaiah 42:1 (ESV)
>
> “I am the LORD; that is my name; **my glory I give to no other**, nor my praise to carved idols.” — Isaiah 42:8 (ESV)

Isaiah 42:8 occurs in a polemic against idols. The speaker names himself as the LORD, says that he gives his glory to no other, and refuses to give his praise to carved idols. *In context, this supports the broader theological inference that the LORD tolerates no rival claimant to his sovereign glory or worship.* Yet the same passage distinguishes the LORD from the servant whom he upholds, chooses, and equips with his Spirit. The servant’s commission therefore does not erase the LORD’s unique identity.

Daniel presents the same distinction in an exalted messianic scene. The one like a son of man approaches the Ancient of Days and receives authority from him:

> “And to him was **given dominion and glory and a kingdom**, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him; his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be destroyed.” — Daniel 7:14 (ESV)

*The Old Testament pattern permits the LORD to invest his appointed king with comprehensive, enduring rule and glory while remaining distinct from that king. Receiving divine glory and rule is therefore not, by itself, an identification with the God who gives them.*

Psalm 110 makes this royal distinction explicit:

> “The LORD says to my Lord: ‘Sit at my **right hand**, until I make your enemies your footstool.’” — Psalm 110:1 (ESV)

The Messiah’s place at the LORD’s right hand is the highest delegated honour. It is not the same position as the LORD who speaks and grants that place.

## Shared and Received Glory in John 17

Jesus’ prayer uses both shared and received glory. Its language is exceptionally high and should not be made weak:

> “Father, the hour has come; **glorify your Son** that the Son may glorify you, since you have **given him authority over all flesh**, to give eternal life to all whom you have given him. And this is eternal life, that they know you, the **only true God**, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” — John 17:1-3 (ESV)
>
> “And now, Father, **glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had with you before the world existed**.” — John 17:5 (ESV)

A Trinitarian reading reasonably sees John 17:5 as evidence of the Son’s personal pre-existence and shared divine glory before creation. That reading takes the words “with you” and “before the world existed” in their most direct sense. A Unitarian argument should not deny that this is serious evidence for a pre-human relation between Jesus and the Father.

The prayer nevertheless names the Father as “the only true God” and Jesus as the one whom that God sent. Jesus asks the Father to glorify him, and says that the Father gave him authority. *Even if John 17:5 is read as real pre-human glory, the text still distinguishes the Father, who is the only true God, from Jesus, the sent Son who receives authority and asks for glory.*

The later prayer makes the shared character of this glory clearer:

> “The **glory that you have given me I have given to them**, that they may be one even as we are one.” — John 17:22 (ESV)

This verse does not make the disciples God Almighty or members of a divine essence. It shows that glory given by the Father may be communicated through Jesus to his people for unity and mission. A Trinitarian may distinguish this communicable glory from the Son’s eternal divine glory. That distinction is possible, but John 17 itself does not label the two glories as different kinds. *The repeated language of given glory means that sharing glory cannot by itself prove that its recipient is the Father or God Almighty.*

## Image and Perfect Representation

The New Testament calls Christ God’s image and locates the knowledge of God’s glory in him:

> “He is the **image of the invisible God**, the firstborn of all creation.” — Colossians 1:15 (ESV)
>
> “For God, who said, ‘Let light shine out of darkness,’ has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the **glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ**.” — 2 Corinthians 4:6 (ESV)

An image is not an empty copy. In these texts, Jesus perfectly makes the invisible God known. The word “image” also preserves a relation: God is the one who is invisible and disclosed, and Christ is the one in whose face that glory is known. *This is a representation claim of the highest order. It says that encountering Jesus is God’s chosen and faithful self-disclosure; it does not require that Jesus be the God whom he images.*

This pattern has Old Testament roots. Humanity was made in God’s image to represent God’s rule in the world (Genesis 1:26-28). Jesus fulfils that vocation without sin:

> “For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet **without sin**.” — Hebrews 4:15 (ESV)

Colossians also grounds Christ’s unsurpassed place in the fullness God caused to dwell in him and in the reconciliation God accomplishes through him:

> “For in him all the **fullness of God was pleased to dwell**, and through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, making peace by the blood of his cross.” — Colossians 1:19-20 (ESV)

*These texts present Jesus as the sinless, definitive representative through whom God reconciles creation.* The comparison with humanity does not make his status ordinary. It explains why image language can be both strong and relational: the perfect representative shows the character and authority of the one represented.

Jesus himself spoke in this representative pattern:

> “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” — John 14:9 (ESV)

The statement is a full claim that the Father is made known in Jesus. In the same conversation Jesus says, “I am going to the Father” and “the Father is greater than I” (John 14:12,28). *John’s presentation therefore supports both truths together: Jesus is the decisive revelation of the Father, and Jesus is not the Father he reveals.*

## Hebrews 1:3: Radiance and Exact Imprint

Hebrews gives the strongest compact description of Jesus as the disclosure of God:

> “He is the **radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature**, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power. After making purification for sins, he **sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high**.” — Hebrews 1:3 (ESV)

The strongest Trinitarian reading treats “radiance” and “exact imprint” as ontological wording. On that reading, the Son eternally shares the divine nature, not merely a task or a moral resemblance. “Exact imprint” is especially forceful. It does not permit a picture of Jesus as an inaccurate, partial, or merely human spokesman. The phrase says that the Son precisely bears and reveals God’s nature.

*A Unitarian reading need not reduce this language to a loose metaphor. Jesus is the exact, living manifestation of God’s glory and character, and the unique agent through whom God now speaks and acts. Yet an exact imprint is still the imprint of another, and radiance is radiance of another’s glory. The terms describe perfect correspondence and revelation while retaining the relation between the God revealed and the Son who reveals him.*

The final clause provides the narrative setting. Having made purification, the Son sat at the right hand of “the Majesty on high.” This fulfils the LORD’s address to the Messiah in Psalm 110:1. Hebrews then says that the Son was exalted above angels:

> “having become as much superior to angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent than theirs.” — Hebrews 1:4 (ESV)

The verb “having become” need not settle what the Son was before his exaltation. It does show that Hebrews can describe his supreme status in terms of inheritance and appointment. *The chapter’s strong representation language and its right-hand, inherited-status language fit a distinction between God Almighty and his uniquely exalted Son.*

Hebrews 1:8-9 is also a serious objection. Addressing the Son, it quotes Psalm 45:

> “But of the Son he says, ‘Your throne, O **God**, is forever and ever, the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom. You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness; therefore **God, your God**, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions.’” — Hebrews 1:8-9 (ESV)

The passage applies exalted royal language to the Son, including “O God.” It must not be dismissed. But the same quotation immediately speaks of “God, your God” anointing the Son. Psalm 45 originally celebrates God’s anointed king, whose rule represents God’s own rule. *In Hebrews, the quotation supports the Son’s incomparable royal authority while still placing God above him as the one who anoints him.*

## Direct Answer and Positive Thesis

Isaiah’s confession of the LORD’s unique deity stands behind later Christian claims about Jesus:

> “Turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth! For **I am God, and there is no other**. By myself I have sworn; from my mouth has gone out in righteousness a word that shall not return: ‘To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance.’” — Isaiah 45:22-23 (ESV)

Paul describes universal homage to Jesus in language that recalls this passage:

> “Therefore **God has highly exalted him** and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that **Jesus Christ is Lord**, to the glory of **God the Father**.” — Philippians 2:9-11 (ESV)

A Trinitarian can reasonably argue that applying Isaiah’s LORD-language to Jesus identifies Jesus within the identity of the one God. This is among the weightiest arguments for Trinitarian theology. The passage also states, however, that God highly exalted Jesus, bestowed the name, and receives glory as “God the Father.” *The Unitarian reading sees the Father’s act as the fulfilment of Daniel 7:14 and Psalm 110:1: God grants his Messiah the authority through which all creation honours God’s rule.*

Jesus’ genuine humanity is not, by itself, a disproof of a two-natures reading. A Trinitarian may affirm that the eternal Son became truly human while remaining divine. The case here is different. It rests on the recurring scriptural relation in which Jesus directly calls the Father “the only true God” (John 17:3), while Jesus is God’s sent Son, image, servant, Lord, and exalted king.

*The positive thesis is that Jesus is God’s unsurpassably perfect image and representative. He bears God’s glory without rivalry, reveals God’s nature without defect, receives universal authority from God, and brings glory back to God the Father. This honours the extraordinary language about Christ while preserving the distinction the texts repeatedly make between God Almighty and his Messiah.*

## Conclusion

*Scripture preserves the LORD’s unique glory while exalting Jesus as his chosen and enthroned Messiah. Its [Old Testament pattern](#the-lords-glory-and-his-servant), [shared and received glory](#shared-and-received-glory-in-john-17), [image language](#image-and-perfect-representation), and [Hebrews 1 description](#hebrews-13-radiance-and-exact-imprint) support a distinction between God Almighty and Jesus.*

John 17:5 naturally supports personal pre-existence, though pre-existence alone does not identify Jesus as the one God; shared glory must be read with the Father who gives, glorifies, and remains Jesus’ God.
