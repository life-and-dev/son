## Was Jesus Glorified Before the World Existed?

John 17:5 places Jesus’ requested glory before the world existed. The verse deserves its strongest reading, yet the prayer and the wider Scriptures also identify the Father as the one true God and Jesus as his glorified Messiah.

### The LORD’s Glory and His Messiah

The Old Testament identifies the LORD as the one God whose purpose cannot fail. He declares “the end from the beginning” and says, “My counsel shall stand” (Isaiah 46:9-10, ESV). His word accomplishes the purpose for which he sends it (Isaiah 55:10-11). *These passages show that what God has settled can be spoken of with certainty; they do not by themselves describe a person’s personal life before birth.*

The LORD also gives honour and rule to his appointed servant without making that servant the LORD. Isaiah introduces the LORD’s chosen servant, upheld by the LORD and endowed with his Spirit (Isaiah 42:1). In the same context the LORD says, “my glory I give to no other” (Isaiah 42:8, ESV). Daniel then sees “one like a son of man” receive dominion, glory, and a kingdom from the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:13-14, ESV). The giver and recipient remain distinct.

This pattern matters for John 17. Scripture can call glory God’s own while also portraying God as giving glory, authority, and kingdom to his Messiah. *A bestowed share in glory is therefore not, by itself, an identity claim that the recipient is God Almighty.*

### John 17:5 in Its Prayer

> “And now, Father, glorify me in your own presence with the **glory that I had with you before the world existed**.” — John 17:5 (ESV)

The most direct reading takes Jesus’ words as personal pre-existence. On that reading, before creation Jesus was with the Father and possessed glory in the Father’s presence. The sentence naturally supports more than a merely future reward. It must not be dismissed by saying that every reference to a pre-world reality is only a plan in God’s mind.

That reading still does not identify Jesus as God Almighty or as the Father. The glory is requested from the Father, and Jesus is **with** the Father. Trinitarian theology itself recognises that this personal distinction does not disprove deity. The question is therefore narrower: does shared pre-world glory establish that Jesus is the one God whom Scripture identifies as the Father? John’s prayer answers that question by distinguishing them.

At its opening Jesus addresses the Father as “the **only true God**” and identifies himself as the one whom the Father sent (John 17:3, ESV). He asks the Father to glorify him (John 17:1,5). Later he calls the glory his Father **gave** him and says that this gift rests on the Father’s love before the foundation of the world (John 17:24). These statements present the Father as giver, sender, and only true God, and Jesus as the one sent and glorified.

The same prayer also speaks of bestowed glory: “The **glory that you have given me I have given to them**” (John 17:22, ESV). The disciples do not become God Almighty by receiving glory. *The prayer therefore establishes that glory can be shared by God’s gift without erasing the distinction between God and those he glorifies.*

### What Foreordination Can and Cannot Explain

A second reading understands the requested glory as secure in God’s purpose before creation and now to be openly granted. Scripture supplies real parallels for God’s prior purpose. Before Jeremiah was formed, the LORD knew him and appointed him a prophet (Jeremiah 1:5). Believers were chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world (Ephesians 1:4). Christ was foreknown before the foundation of the world and made manifest later (1 Peter 1:20). None of those statements requires Jeremiah, the believers, or the manifested Christ to have been living personally before the world.

John 17 itself makes that category relevant. Jesus’ followers receive the glory given to him (John 17:22), and the Father loved Jesus before the foundation of the world (John 17:24). God’s saving purpose includes people and glory before their historical fulfilment. *This makes a foreordination reading of John 17:5 a possible contextual reading.*

It is not a lexical rule that makes **“I had with you”** mean only “you planned for me.” The first-person wording remains the main difficulty for that reading, and a reader may reasonably judge that it indicates Jesus’ personal pre-existence. The foreordination parallels show only that pre-world language can concern God’s settled purpose. They do not settle John 17:5 apart from the prayer’s giver-and-recipient pattern.

### The Father and the Exalted Son

The positive biblical case rests on the identities and relationships Scripture states. The LORD is the unique God, Creator, and Saviour (Isaiah 43:10-11; 44:24; 45:5-6). In Jesus’ prayer, the Father is “the **only true God**,” while Jesus is the Christ sent by that God (John 17:3, ESV). After his resurrection Jesus still says, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to **my God** and your God” (John 20:17, ESV). This is not merely a difference of persons. It identifies the Father as Jesus’ God.

Jesus is the Messiah whom God authorises and exalts. He says that the Father gave him authority (John 5:26-27; 17:2), and Peter proclaims that “God has made him both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36, ESV). The Father has already given him glory, authority, and a place at his right hand. *These are affirmations of Jesus’ unique, exalted status, not reductions of him to an ordinary man.* Yet their repeated direction is clear: God acts toward and through his Messiah.

Revelation confirms this distinction by speaking of “the Lord God the Almighty” and “the Lamb” together, and by calling the Lamb’s Father “his God” (Revelation 21:22; 3:12, ESV). The confirmatory language fits the earlier witness of Isaiah, Daniel, John, and Acts. Jesus’ glory is real, and Scripture presents it as glory given by the Father. Scripture gives God Almighty’s identity to the Father, the God Jesus calls “my God.”

### Conclusion

John 17:5 can reasonably be read as Jesus’ personal pre-existence, while a foreordination reading remains possible but limited by the verse’s first-person wording. Neither conclusion identifies Jesus as God Almighty. [The Old Testament’s giver-and-Messiah pattern](#the-lords-glory-and-his-messiah), [the prayer’s identification of the Father](#john-175-in-its-prayer), and [Jesus’ received authority and glory](#the-father-and-the-exalted-son) present the Father as the only true God and Jesus as his exalted, sent Messiah.
