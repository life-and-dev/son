# Praying in the Name of Jesus

The strongest Trinitarian reading of John 14:13-14 takes Jesus’ promise, “Whatever you ask in my name, this I will do,” as an invitation to pray directly to him. The textual variant in verse 14 and the direct wording mean that this reading deserves consideration.

The immediate setting still directs the disciples to the Father through the Son. Jesus says that he will act “that the Father may be glorified in the Son” (John 14:13), and later states plainly, “If you ask the Father anything in my name, he will give it to you” (John 16:23). Asking in Jesus’ name means approaching the Father on the basis of Jesus’ God-given authority and mission.

This does not forbid a believer from addressing the exalted Christ in an exceptional vision or appeal. It does preserve the regular pattern taught by Jesus: the Father is the one addressed, Jesus is the way to the Father (John 14:6), and the Holy Spirit is God’s empowering presence. Honour given to the Son should lead to the Father, not identify the Son as the Father.

## Contextual Distinction

Direct appeals to the risen Jesus occur and should not be denied. Stephen, while seeing Jesus in heaven, says, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit” (Acts 7:59); John closes Revelation with “Come, Lord Jesus” (Revelation 22:20). The first is made in a vision and the second is a direct eschatological petition, so neither should be reduced to ordinary conversation. These appeals show that the exalted Son may be addressed; by themselves, they do not identify him as God. The ordinary pattern Jesus teaches is prayer to the Father in his name, and Paul names one God and one mediator, the man Christ Jesus (John 16:23; First Timothy 2:5). Access through the Son gives him unique honour without making the mediator the one God.

# Jesus is our mediator

Jesus is our mediator, according to Paul:

> For there is one [God](/god), and there is one mediator between God and men, the man [Christ Jesus](/god/son/christ) -- First Timothy 2:5 (ESV)

Some assume that Jesus is a divine being who channels all our prayers to God as a mediator. They would often say that we need to pray to Jesus and that he will somehow filter our prayer requests or present them in such a way that they would be acceptable to God. This is not a biblical teaching. Jesus is not hindering our prayers, nor is he some sort of communication channel to God.

Instead, we need to understand that a "[mediator](/bible/concepts/mediator)" is:

> one who intervenes between two parties to restore peace, make a covenant, or ratify an agreement. In the New Testament, it is primarily used to describe Jesus Christ as the mediator between God and humanity, highlighting His role in reconciling sinful humans to a holy God through His sacrificial death and resurrection. -- [Strong's Lexicon](https://biblehub.com/greek/3316.htm)

# Fellowship with Jesus

Paul writes that believers are called into fellowship with God’s Son (1 Corinthians 1:9), and John speaks of fellowship with both the Father and the Son (1 John 1:3). This fellowship is present, not merely future.

Direct appeals to the risen Jesus are genuine biblical practice. Stephen says, “Lord Jesus, **receive my spirit**” (Acts 7:59), and Revelation closes, “**Come, Lord Jesus!**” (Revelation 22:20). These texts should not be denied or reduced to ordinary conversation.

The theological question is different from the fact of direct address. Jesus’ regular teaching gives the ordinary prayer pattern as asking the Father in his name (John 16:23), and Paul calls Jesus the mediator between the one God and humanity (1 Timothy 2:5). Unitarians therefore affirm that believers may directly address the exalted Christ while maintaining prayer to the Father through Christ as the normal pattern. Trinitarians may regard the direct appeals and fellowship language as evidence of devotion fitting God’s Son. Neither conclusion follows from fellowship language alone.

> “Whoever receives you receives me, and whoever receives me receives him who sent me.” — Matthew 10:40 (ESV)

Fellowship with Christ also has communal expression: receiving his people is receiving him, and receiving him leads to the one who sent him. *This supports active fellowship with Christ without collapsing Christ into the Father.*
