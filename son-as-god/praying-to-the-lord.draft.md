# Praying in the Name of Jesus

Christians must distinguish the regular pattern Jesus taught from biblical instances of direct appeal to the risen Messiah. This article considers whether such appeals are permitted, what prayer and fellowship mean, and how disciples follow Jesus now.

## Contextual Distinction

Prayer is intentional religious address. It may include request, thanks, praise, confession, or intercession. Its definition does not settle whether every recipient is legitimate. A religious petition to the risen Jesus cannot be dismissed merely by calling it direct address, yet an everyday request to another person is not necessarily prayer. Prayer also does not automatically establish that its recipient is God.

Jesus gives the clearest regular pattern: “Pray then like this: ‘Our **Father** in heaven’” (Matthew 6:9). Private prayer should avoid display and seek the Father who sees in secret (Matthew 6:6-9). He later says, “Whatever you ask of the **Father** in **my name**, he will give it to you” and adds that the Father himself loves the disciples (John 16:23-27). The Father hears directly; Jesus is not a required relay for passing words onward.

Acts 4:24-30 gives a corporate apostolic example. Gathered believers address God as Creator and Sovereign, identify Jesus as God’s holy servant, and ask for boldness and signs through Jesus’ name. This strengthens the Father-directed pattern while giving Jesus’ name a decisive place.

To ask in Jesus’ name is to approach the Father in the authority, representation, and allegiance of his Messiah. It is neither a magic formula nor an automatic rule that every request bearing the phrase must be addressed to Jesus. This Father-directed pattern deserves priority, while the texts below test whether it excludes every direct petition to the risen Son.

### A Serious Objection

John 16:23 also says, “In that day you will ask **nothing of me**.” The surrounding questions in John 16:17-19,30 support a distinction between asking Jesus questions and asking the Father for what is needed. A rigid Greek rule that *erōtaō* only means question and *aiteō* only means request cannot carry the argument, because *erōtaō* denotes request in John 16:26. The command to pray to the Father therefore does not, by itself, prohibit every Son-directed petition.

God-only service in Matthew 4:10 remains a serious objection. Direct appeal requires positive textual support. Jesus’ authority does not establish that he hears every silent petition, but the absence of a described mechanism does not establish that this is impossible. Permission rests on the relevant texts, not speculation about what God could enable.

## Evidence for Direct Appeal

John 14:14 in the ESV reads, “If you ask **me** anything in my name, I will do it.” Some manuscripts omit “me”, so no unsupported ranking should be asserted. Where the reading includes “me”, Jesus is the recipient of the request. Trinitarians commonly take this as invocation of the Son, without identifying him as the Father. The text itself presents Jesus as acting so that the Father is glorified in the Son (John 14:13).

Stephen’s words are an actual petition: “Lord Jesus, **receive my spirit**” and “Lord, **do not hold this sin against them**” (Acts 7:59-60). He spoke while seeing Jesus in heaven. That setting makes the passage neither a universal command for every prayer nor a visions-only prohibition. It is positive evidence that the risen Jesus may receive a petition.

Paul addresses the church as those “who in every place **call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ**” (1 Corinthians 1:2). This naturally supports invocation, though a confession of allegiance is an alternative reading. Paul’s wish that “our God and Father himself, and our Lord Jesus, direct our way” (1 Thessalonians 3:11) names both Father and Lord Jesus in a communal devotional context. Its third-person form is less unambiguous than Stephen’s direct second-person petition, and it does not mandate that every prayer address Jesus. In 2 Corinthians 12:8-9, the Lord addressed is plausibly Jesus because the answer speaks of Christ’s power, but verse 8 does not name him. Revelation 22:20, “**Come, Lord Jesus!**”, may corroborate direct appeal but is not needed as the sole evidence.

### Invocation, Honour, and Identity

Romans 10:9-13 draws Joel 2:32 into confession of Jesus as Lord. Trinitarians fairly regard this application of a LORD salvation text to Jesus as a strong divine-identity argument, without claiming that the Son is the Father. An alternative reads the Father as saving through his exalted Messiah. The text’s application and the ontological conclusion are related but distinct; invocation alone neither proves nor disproves deity.

Deuteronomy 6:13 and Matthew 4:10 make exclusive service to God a serious boundary. Worship is not merely sacrifice, and not every petition is automatically identical to worship of the Father. John 5:23 commands honour for the Son as for the Father. One reading sees shared divine nature; the adopted reading sees God-commanded honour for the unique Messiah, whose authority comes from God. [Nature](../nature.md#incompatible-attributes) and [Jesus Serves God](../nature.md#jesus-serves-god) address this distinction further. Matthew 7:21 and John 14:15,24 connect obedience to Jesus with the Father’s will and word. Incarnate-mission and human-Messiah readings account for this relation differently; neither should be asserted as Trinity fact.

*The combined evidence substantially supports permission for direct appeal beyond a vision, but does not state a separate routine that every believer must maintain.*

## Jesus is our mediator

Paul distinguishes [God](/god) from [Jesus](/god/son/christ): “For there is **one God**, and there is **one mediator** between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). Jesus reconciles humanity to the one God. Romans 8:34 adds that the risen Christ intercedes. Access and intercession through Christ do not make him an automatic prayer filter.

A [mediator](/bible/concepts/mediator) is one who acts between parties to bring reconciliation or establish an agreement. This careful summary reflects [Strong’s Lexicon](https://biblehub.com/greek/3316.htm). The mediator’s role alone neither mandates nor forbids direct address. It must not be used to deny access to the Father through Christ, nor to assert that every prayer must first be addressed or routed to Jesus.

## Fellowship with Jesus

Fellowship translates *koinōnia*: shared participation, belonging, relationship, and partnership. Believers are called “into the **fellowship** of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord” (1 Corinthians 1:9), and John writes of fellowship “with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ” (1 John 1:3). This is present fellowship, not only a future meeting, socialising, or studying a dead author.

Jesus’ vine and branches teaching describes abiding in him through dependence, obedience, and fruitfulness (John 15:1-10). Fellowship does not require audible conversation and does not independently prove a duty to pray to Jesus. It includes personal participation in the life and work of the risen Messiah, not mere community memory. Jesus says, “Whoever **receives** you receives me, and whoever receives me receives him who sent me” (Matthew 10:40). This gives fellowship a communal expression, without replacing the personal Christ with the congregation.

The bread and cup are participation in Christ’s body and blood, and the one bread expresses shared unity (1 Corinthians 10:16-17). This complements the Lord’s Supper proclamation of his death (1 Corinthians 11:23-26). God’s Spirit makes this present relationship practical. Jesus promises that the Father will give another Helper, and that he and the Father will make their home with the one who loves and keeps his word (John 14:16-23). This promises no audible answers and gives no licence to treat every inner impression as Jesus’ words.

## Present Discipleship

Following Jesus today is concrete. His received authority grounds the commission to learn and keep his teaching (Matthew 28:18-20; 1 John 2:6). Disciples obey his commands to love one another, forgive, and love enemies (John 13:34; Matthew 5:44; 1 John 2:3-6). They serve as he served in washing feet (John 13:14-15), follow his example in suffering (1 Peter 2:21), and keep his commands as he kept his Father’s commands (John 15:10).

Believers are conformed to the image of God’s Son (Romans 8:29), while God distributes differing gifts within one body (1 Corinthians 12). This does not make later leaders who claim to speak for Jesus unquestionable. It also does not deny legitimate delegated leadership; all such leadership remains subject to Jesus’ teaching.

Philippians 3:21 promises transformed bodies, and 2 Peter 1:4 speaks of participation in divine nature. Such likeness and shared participation do not erase Jesus’ unique office and authority or make believers additional gods. These texts alone do not decide every claim about Jesus’ eternal nature.

Disciples pray to the Father in Jesus’ name, while recognising the biblical support for direct appeals to the risen Jesus. They share worship, care, and the Lord’s Supper, which proclaims his death until he comes (1 Corinthians 11:23-26).

This discipleship is relationship in action. God’s Spirit empowers obedience; it does not replace Jesus’ teaching or guarantee private messages from him.

## Conclusion

Christians should follow Jesus’ explicit pattern of praying to the Father in his name, strengthened by [the contextual evidence](#contextual-distinction). Access and intercession remain through [Jesus the mediator](#jesus-is-our-mediator), not through a mandatory prayer filter.

The risen Jesus receives petitions in Scripture. [The evidence](#evidence-for-direct-appeal), including invocation and honour texts, supports permission for direct appeal, though its extension beyond visions remains disputed. Such prayer is permissible, not mandatory; Scripture states no requirement to divide prayers or keep a separate Son-addressed routine.

[Fellowship with Jesus](#fellowship-with-jesus) is present shared participation, and [discipleship](#present-discipleship) expresses it through obedience, service, and life together. The evidence does not settle Jesus’ ontology.
