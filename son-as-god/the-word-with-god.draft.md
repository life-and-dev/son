# The Word With God

John 1:1-18 is a central text in the debate over Jesus’ identity. Both its high language and its distinctions must remain in view.

> “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was **with God**, and the Word **was God**.” — John 1:1 (ESV)

The classic Trinitarian reading identifies the personal Word with the pre-human Son: *pros ton theon* marks relation with God, *dia autou* presents creation through the Word, and the Word becomes flesh in Jesus. The final anarthrous *theos* is not automatically “a god”; many scholars understand it qualitatively, and qualitative language does not automatically exclude full deity. A Unitarian reading argues from Israel’s Word and Wisdom traditions, John’s Father-Son distinction, and Jesus’ identification of the Father as the only true God (Psalm 33:6,9; John 17:3). It must account for the personal language and creation claim rather than merely rename the Word. See [The Son as God](the-son-as-god.draft.md).

*John 1 strongly supports the classic reading, while a contextual Unitarian reading remains possible only if it explains the prologue’s grammar, personification, and later identification of Jesus without oversimplification.*

## Reading John 1:1-18

The Greek distinguishes the Word from God in *pros ton theon*, “with God.” The phrase naturally supports relation to God; in this context it is strong evidence for the classic personal-Logos reading, though a Unitarian may argue that God’s Word is personified as in biblical Wisdom traditions.

The final clause, *kai theos ēn ho logos*, has an anarthrous predicate noun before the verb. Its lack of article does not require “a god,” and it does not settle the issue by itself as merely indefinite. The widely recognised qualitative reading describes what the Word was like. Trinitarians reasonably contend that this affirms full deity; Unitarians contend that it describes God’s own self-expression rather than a second person who is God. Neither conclusion follows from article usage alone.

> “All things were made **through him**, and without him was not any thing made that was made.” — John 1:3 (ESV)

*Dia autou* presents creation as occurring through the Word. Trinitarians read this as the Son’s agency in creation. Unitarians read it with Genesis 1 and Psalm 33:6,9, where God creates by His word, without denying that John’s personal pronouns make the alternative demanding. The verb *egeneto* in John 1:3 marks things coming into being; it does not say that the Word came into being.

John 1:14 says that the Word “became flesh.” The classic reading sees the personal Word entering human life in Jesus. The Unitarian reading sees God’s Word, purpose, and self-revelation embodied in the man Jesus. The context, including John 1:18 and John 17:3, continues to distinguish God, the Father, from His Son.

The textual reading at John 1:18 is also relevant. “The only God” is supported by early Greek witnesses and is reflected in the ESV; “only Son” is also a well-attested reading reflected in the KJV. This is a manuscript question distinct from translation and interpretation. Both readings present Jesus as the unique revealer at the Father’s side; the former supplies important evidence for the Trinitarian case.

*John 1 cannot be reduced either to “Jesus is the Father” or to a mechanical “a god” translation. The Unitarian conclusion depends on the Gospel’s repeated Father-Son distinction and must answer the prologue’s personal and creative language.*

## Grammar and Article Usage

The Greek article is not a simple equivalent of English “the.” The anarthrous *theos* in John 1:1c cannot be made indefinite solely because it lacks an article. Its pre-verbal position is commonly discussed as qualitative, but grammar does not independently settle the Word’s personal identity or the later doctrine of the Trinity.

## Creation Through the Word

John 1:3 uses *dia autou*, “through him.” The classic reading identifies the Word as the Son acting in creation. The Unitarian alternative reads the phrase through Israel’s scriptural picture of God creating by His word. Both must respect John’s personal language and the Old Testament’s insistence that the LORD is Creator (Genesis 1:1-3; Psalm 33:6,9; Isaiah 44:24).

## The Word Became Flesh

John 1:14 is not a statement that the Father became the Son. Trinitarians understand it as the personal Word taking human nature. Unitarians understand it as God’s self-expression becoming fully embodied in the human Messiah. Both readings distinguish God from the Jesus whom the Gospel presents as God’s Son and sent one.

## John 1:18 and Textual Evidence

“Only God” and “only Son” are distinct textual readings, not merely competing translations of identical Greek. The ESV follows the former; the KJV follows the latter. The variant must be separated from the interpretive question: either reading presents Jesus as the unique revealer of the unseen Father, while “only God” is especially important to the Trinitarian case.

## Limits of the Unitarian Reading

A Unitarian reading may not claim that “with God” makes the Word impersonal, that anarthrous *theos* means “a god,” or that personification is automatic. The Trinitarian reading is historically classic and textually substantial.

The positive Unitarian case is that John consistently distinguishes the Father from Jesus, calls the Father the only true God, and can describe God’s creative Word in terms drawn from Israel’s Scriptures. Its adequacy depends on explaining the Word’s personal language and embodiment in Jesus. See [Reading John 1:1-18](#reading-john-11-18).

## Contextual Distinction

The pre-existence reading sees the Word as Jesus before birth and takes “the Word was God” as a direct deity statement. Any Unitarian reading must still account for John’s exalted language. John also identifies the one true God as the Father and identifies Jesus as the one sent by that God (John 17:1,3; 17:18). The prologue should therefore be read with the Gospel’s repeated God-and-Son distinction, rather than making every statement about God’s Word an assertion that Jesus is YHVH.

# The Word of God in Revelation

Revelation 19:13 calls the conquering rider “The Word of God.” Most readers identify the rider with Jesus from the surrounding titles and imagery. This use supports the Trinitarian identification of Jesus with John’s Logos, although it does not by itself define the metaphysical relation between Jesus and God.

A Unitarian can understand the title as Jesus’ perfect representation and execution of God’s word. That reading must acknowledge that the title is more than a bare claim that Jesus follows instructions. *The title supports a cumulative debate; it does not settle John 1 alone.*
