## The Word and John 1:18

John 1:1 says that the Word was with God and that the Word was God; John 1:14 says that the Word became flesh. The Trinitarian reading understands the Word as the eternal divine Son who became incarnate. It accounts for both the Word’s distinction from God and the Gospel’s highest language about the Word.

A Unitarian reading proposes that the Word is God’s self-expression, purpose, or wisdom, embodied in the human Messiah. That proposal is an *interpretive inference*, not an automatic result of the word *theos*. The predicate *theos* in John 1:1c is anarthrous, but that grammatical feature alone does not mean “a god”, “lesser deity”, or a settled theological conclusion. Context must decide.

John 1:18 has an important manuscript variant. The critical Greek texts record readings commonly translated “only God” and “only Son”. The **Nestle-Aland, Novum Testamentum Graece, 28th edition** apparatus and the **United Bible Societies’ Greek New Testament, 5th revised edition** apparatus document this variant. A manuscript variant concerns what wording John wrote; an English rendering is a translation decision; either reading’s theological force is an interpretation. Both readings present Jesus as the unique revealer of the unseen Father. Neither reading identifies Jesus as the Father or by itself defines God as three persons.

### Thomas in John’s Ending

Thomas answers the risen Jesus, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28). He addresses Jesus, not another addressee or heaven. The Trinitarian reading therefore treats this direct confession as a climactic declaration of Jesus’ deity. It is serious evidence within John’s exalted presentation of the Son.

The Unitarian reply must not evade the address. It reads Thomas’s words beside Jesus’ “my God” in John 20:17 and the Gospel’s distinction between the Father who sends and the Son who is sent. *Calling Jesus “my God” need not identify him with the Father or establish later Trinitarian doctrine, but it is stronger evidence than a merely honorific title.* John 20:31 names Jesus as the Christ and Son of God; it does not automatically exclude high Christology. [Thomas’s Witness](thomas-s-witness.draft.md#two-readings-of-the-confession) weighs both readings.

## Conclusion

John’s Word language and textual variant require [careful contextual reading](#the-word-and-john-118), and Thomas gives a [direct confession to Jesus](thomas-s-witness.draft.md#thomass-direct-confession). *A Unitarian conclusion remains possible only if it accounts for that high language alongside the Father-Son distinction; neither John 20:31 nor grammar alone settles the issue.*
