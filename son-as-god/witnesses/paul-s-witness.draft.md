## Creation and Pre-Existence

The creation language raises a major objection. If “all things” came through Jesus, and if Jesus existed before his human birth, many conclude that he belongs on God’s side of the Creator-creature distinction. A Unitarian reading need not deny either point in order to reject that conclusion. Personal pre-existence and participation in God’s creative work would make Jesus unique; neither expression explicitly says that Jesus is the Father.

> “For **from him and through him and to him are all things**. To him be glory forever. Amen.”  
> Romans 11:36 (ESV)

In Romans 11:36, Paul says that all things are from, through, and to God. In 1 Corinthians 8:6, he says that all things are **from** the Father and **through** Jesus. Many Trinitarian interpreters see this as a deliberate reworking of the Shema: the one God and one Lord together receive the terms by which Paul describes all things. That reading deserves weight, especially because “Lord” echoes the LORD of Deuteronomy 6:4.

The Unitarian reading stresses Paul’s explicit distinction: “one God, the Father” is the ultimate source, and “one Lord, Jesus Christ” is the one through whom God’s purpose is carried out. *The preposition “through” alone does not settle whether Paul means original creation, new creation, or both in every passage. The reworked-Shema reading makes this a stronger Trinitarian text than a bare agency formula, while the source-and-agent distinction remains real evidence for the Unitarian case.*

## The Son’s Enduring Subjection

Paul’s resurrection argument says that Christ reigns until every enemy is defeated, then delivers the kingdom to God the Father.

> “Then comes the end, when he delivers the kingdom to God the Father after destroying every rule and every authority and power.”  
> 1 Corinthians 15:24 (ESV)

> “When all things are subjected to him, then the Son himself will also be **subjected** to him who put all things in subjection under him, that **God may be all in all**.”  
> 1 Corinthians 15:28 (ESV)

The text explicitly places the Son’s subjection at “the end” and identifies the recipient as God the Father. *That Paul teaches a permanent inequality of divine nature is an inference from this final-order language, not a statement the passage makes in those terms. The Unitarian reading treats the final subjection as evidence that the Father remains ultimate; the Trinitarian reading can treat it as the Son’s ordered economic role without denying shared divine identity.*

### The Economic-Subordination Objection

A Trinitarian reply distinguishes an economic role from ontology: the Son may be voluntarily subordinate in salvation while remaining equally God in nature. That distinction is a theological account of the passage; 1 Corinthians 15:24-28 does not use later terms such as “economic” or “ontology”. The text itself gives a final relation of God and Son and identifies the recipient of the kingdom as “God the Father”.

Paul also writes that “the head of Christ is God” in 1 Corinthians 11:3. *These texts directly establish personal distinction and ordered relation. That they establish permanent divine inequality is the Unitarian inference; that the order is compatible with equal divine nature is the Trinitarian inference. Neither conclusion should be presented as the text’s unstated wording.*

## Philippians 2 and the Exalted Messiah

Philippians 2:6-11 is often presented as Paul’s clearest statement that Jesus is God. The passage moves from Christ’s self-emptying and obedient death to God’s act of exalting him.

> “Though he was in the **form of God**, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men.”  
> Philippians 2:6-7 (ESV)

> “Therefore **God has highly exalted him** and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus **every knee should bow**, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of **God the Father**.”  
> Philippians 2:9-11 (ESV)

The pre-existent reading takes “form of God”, equality with God, and Christ’s taking human likeness to describe voluntary incarnation. It is a coherent Trinitarian reading and should not be excluded by appeal to Jesus’ humanity, service, or obedience. The Adamic reading sees an Adam-Christ contrast: unlike Adam, Christ refuses self-advantage and obeys. It can explain the passage’s ethical movement, though interpreters dispute whether it explains all the pre-existence language.

*Under either reading, Paul distinguishes Christ from God and says that God exalted him. Under the pre-existent reading, those facts need not disprove shared divine identity; under the Adamic reading, they support an exalted human Messiah. The passage therefore supplies strong evidence, but does not settle the question by one phrase alone.*

### The Isaiah 45 Objection

Isaiah 45:23 says, “To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance.” Paul’s application of knee-bending and confession to Jesus is a powerful reason to give Jesus the highest honour. The Trinitarian reading argues that applying the Father’s exclusive Isaiah language to Jesus places Jesus within the divine identity.

Philippians also specifies that God bestows the name, every tongue confesses Jesus as Lord, and the result is the glory of God the Father. *A Unitarian reading can understand the Father’s universal rule exercised through his appointed Lord, but it must account for Paul’s striking reuse of Isaiah’s language. The text distinguishes Father and Son; whether that distinction excludes shared deity is the disputed inference.*

## The Spirit in Paul’s Confession

Paul speaks of the Spirit with reverence and ascribes God’s work to the Spirit. He does not, however, identify the Spirit as a second or third God alongside the Father.

> “There are varieties of gifts, but the same **Spirit**; and there are varieties of service, but the same **Lord**; and there are varieties of activities, but it is the same **God** who empowers them all in everyone.”  
> 1 Corinthians 12:4-6 (ESV)

This passage distinguishes Spirit, Lord, and God in the distribution of gifts. It names only “God” as the one who empowers all. *The distinction therefore cannot establish that the Holy Spirit is a separate God, just as the distinction between God and Lord does not make Jesus the one God.*

## Conclusion

Paul distinguishes the Father and Jesus in [creation language](#creation-and-pre-existence) and in [the Son’s final subjection](#the-sons-enduring-subjection). Yet 1 Corinthians 8:6 may rework the Shema, and [Philippians 2](#philippians-2-and-the-exalted-messiah) applies Isaiah’s homage language to Jesus. *The evidence supports a serious Unitarian source-and-agent reading, but the Trinitarian reading retains substantial force.* See [Peter’s Witness](peter-s-witness.draft.md#what-the-grammar-establishes).
