# The Sons of Korah’s Witness: Psalm 45 and Jesus

Psalm 45 gives a Davidic king striking honour, including an address translated “O God.” Hebrews 1 applies that psalm to Jesus. *This article argues that the psalm’s royal setting, its complete wording, and the wider Scriptural distinction between the Father and Jesus support a Unitarian reading: Jesus is the exalted Messiah and Lord, not God himself.*

## Psalm 45 in Its Royal Setting

### A Song for the King

The heading identifies Psalm 45 as “A Maskil of the Sons of Korah; a love song.” Its opening speaker says that he addresses his verses to **the king**. The psalm then praises a royal bridegroom, his beauty, his sword, his throne, and his bride. *Those features establish the psalm’s original setting as a royal wedding song, before Hebrews later uses it of Jesus.*

> My heart overflows with a pleasing theme; I address my verses to **the king**; my tongue is like the pen of a ready scribe.  
> Psalm 45:1 (ESV)

> Gird your sword on your thigh, O mighty one, in your splendor and majesty!  
> Psalm 45:3 (ESV)

### The Address “O God”

Psalm 45:6 contains the Hebrew word `elohim`, which ESV translates **“O God”** in its address to the king. The next verse immediately distinguishes the addressee from **“God, your God,”** who anoints him. The same passage therefore gives the king an extraordinary God-language address while presenting him as the one whom God anoints.

> Your throne, O **God**, is forever and ever. The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of uprightness; you have loved righteousness and hated wickedness. Therefore **God, your God**, has **anointed** you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions.  
> Psalm 45:6-7 (ESV)

*The immediate context permits reading the address as elevated royal language for God’s appointed king. It does not require that the king be the one God, because the same wording says that God is his God and that God anoints him.*

Some interpreters propose a different construal of the Hebrew, often represented by “God is your throne.” *That translation possibility is disputed and is not required for this case.* ESV’s vocative rendering should be taken seriously. *The stronger contextual point is the paired statement “God, your God,” not a claim that the alternative rendering removes the difficulty.*

## The Wider Scriptural Pattern

### One God, the Father

Paul distinguishes “one God, the Father” from “one Lord, Jesus Christ.” *This distinction does not make Jesus insignificant. It identifies the one God as the Father while identifying Jesus as the one Lord through whom God’s purposes are carried out.*

> Yet for us there is one **God, the Father**, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one **Lord, Jesus Christ**, through whom are all things and through whom we exist.  
> 1 Corinthians 8:6 (ESV)

Jesus likewise addresses the Father as “the only true God” and identifies himself as the one sent by that God.

> And this is eternal life, that they know you, the only true **God**, and **Jesus Christ whom you have sent**.  
> John 17:3 (ESV)

*Read alongside Psalm 45, these statements supply a stable distinction: God is the Father, and Jesus is God’s commissioned Messiah and Lord.*

### The Exalted, Anointed Lord

After the resurrection, Peter says that God made the crucified Jesus both Lord and Christ. *This is consistent with Psalm 45’s picture of an anointed royal recipient of honour.*

> Let all the house of Israel therefore know for certain that **God has made him** both **Lord** and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified.  
> Acts 2:36 (ESV)

Jesus also speaks of the Father as “my God.” *The repeated relation of God to Jesus coheres with Psalm 45’s “God, your God” and resists identifying Jesus with the God who appoints and anoints him.*

> Jesus said to her, “Do not cling to me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father; but go to my brothers and say to them, ‘I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to **my God** and your God.’”  
> John 20:17 (ESV)

*This argument concerns Jesus’ identity and does not depend on presenting the Holy Spirit as a separate God. The cited passages distinguish the Father and Jesus without adding a second God.*

## Hebrews 1 Reuses the Psalm

As later confirmation, Hebrews 1:8-9 quotes Psalm 45:6-7 for the Son. Its wording is exalted, and the author uses it while showing the Son’s superiority to angels. A fair reading must retain both parts of the citation: the Son is addressed as **God**, and “God, your God” anoints him.

> But of the **Son** he says,
>
> “Your throne, O **God**, is forever and ever,
> the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom.
> You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness;
> therefore **God, your God**, has **anointed** you
> with the oil of gladness beyond your companions.”  
> Hebrews 1:8-9 (ESV)

Hebrews 1:10-12 then quotes Psalm 102:25-27. On the strong contextual and syntactic reading, the address to the Son introduced in Hebrews 1:8 continues through verses 10-12; Psalm 102’s creator-and-endurer language therefore supplies major high-Christology evidence. That continuation is widely defended, but it is an interpretive judgement about the quotation’s syntax and addressee, not an undisputed fact stated by Hebrews in a separate formula. *Psalm 45:6-9 preserves an anointed-Son distinction; a Unitarian reading must also reckon with the strong reading that Hebrews applies Psalm 102’s LORD language to the Son.*

### The Strongest Trinitarian Reading

*The strongest Trinitarian reading treats “O God” in Hebrews 1:8 as the author’s direct address to the Son as God. The immediate contrast between the Son and angels makes this weighty. Hebrews calls the Son “the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature” (Hebrews 1:3), then applies Psalm 102’s creator-and-endurer language to him in Hebrews 1:10-12. A distinction between the Son and his God need not, on this reading, deny the Son’s divine identity.*

*The Unitarian case cannot fairly deny that Jesus is addressed as `theos` in Psalm 45 as quoted in Hebrews 1, or that Hebrews 1:10-12 intensifies the argument. Its case rests instead on the royal setting, the complete citation, and the recurring distinction between the anointed Son and God.*

### Direct Reply from the Complete Citation

*Hebrews quotes the complete royal statement: the Son is addressed as “O God”, yet “God, your God” anoints him. Within that citation, the Son is not the anointing God but the anointed king.* This preserves the distinction present in Psalm 45 rather than treating verse 8 as its only interpretive datum.

*That distinction does not by itself disprove deity, and it does not answer Hebrews 1:10-12. The warranted Unitarian conclusion is narrower: Psalm 45 and Hebrews 1 establish extraordinarily high language for the Son while still stating his relation to God. Whether Hebrews includes the Son in the divine identity remains disputed and cannot be settled by omitting either side of the evidence.*

## Conclusion

Psalm 45 addresses a royal king as “O God” and also names [“God, your God” as his anointer](#the-address-o-god). Hebrews reapplies that full citation to Jesus, then adds [Psalm 102’s creator language](#hebrews-1-reuses-the-psalm). *The royal context and Father-Son distinction support a Unitarian reading, but the direct `theos` address and Hebrews 1:10-12 remain major Trinitarian evidence.* Compare [Paul’s Witness](paul-s-witness.draft.md#creation-and-pre-existence).
