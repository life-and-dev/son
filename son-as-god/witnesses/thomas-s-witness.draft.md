# Thomas’s Witness

Thomas addresses the risen Jesus as “my Lord and my God.” That direct confession deserves neither evasion nor a conclusion beyond John’s context. The surrounding passage identifies Jesus’ God and the faith John seeks to create.

## Thomas’s Direct Confession

John records two closely connected statements. First, the risen Jesus distinguishes God from himself.

> “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to **my God and your God**.”  
> John 20:17 (ESV)

Then Thomas responds when Jesus appears.

> “Thomas answered him, ‘**My Lord and my God!**’”  
> John 20:28 (ESV)

The narrative says Thomas answered *him*. The confession addresses Jesus; it is not an exclamation directed to heaven. The direct address is a textual fact. It establishes neither a complete ontology nor the later doctrine of the Trinity by itself, but a fair Unitarian reading must retain its emphatic force.

### Israel’s Scriptural Categories

> “The **LORD** says to my **Lord**: ‘Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.’”  
> Psalm 110:1 (ESV)

Jesus applies this text to the Messiah (Mark 12:35-37). *Within this scriptural pattern, “Lord” can name God’s exalted Messiah without making that Messiah identical with the LORD.* The point does not settle Thomas’s use of “God,” but it supplies the narrative’s category of delegated rule.

### Two Readings of the Confession

The Trinitarian reading takes both titles as predicates of Jesus and treats the saying as the Gospel’s climactic confession of his deity. It is strengthened by the direct address, the resurrection setting, and John’s elevated language about the Word. An incarnation and two-natures account can distinguish Jesus from the Father without reducing the force of Thomas’s address.

The Unitarian reply accepts that Thomas calls Jesus “my God”. *A divine address to God’s supreme authorised representative need not mean identity with the Father or establish later Trinitarian doctrine. Yet that representative reading is an interpretation that must account for the unusual direct title, not a way to weaken it.* John 20:17 keeps the Father as Jesus’ God; the issue is what Thomas’s title means within John’s whole narrative.

### The Narrator’s Purpose

> “But these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the **Christ**, the **Son of God**, and that by believing you may have life in his name.”  
> John 20:31 (ESV)

John’s purpose follows Thomas’s confession. It identifies the faith John seeks to produce: faith that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God. The statement does not provide a complete later ontology, but neither does it automatically exclude high Christology or reinterpret Thomas’s direct address as less than it says. *Read with John 20:17, it supports a Unitarian distinction between the Father and Jesus; read with John 1 and 20:28, it also leaves the Trinitarian reading a substantial case.*

## Conclusion

Thomas directly addresses Jesus in [his confession](#thomass-direct-confession); no two-addressee or exclamation reading fits John’s narration. [Psalm 110’s Messiah](#israels-scriptural-categories) and Jesus’ “my God” establish distinction, while [John’s purpose](#the-narrators-purpose) does not settle the ontology. *The Unitarian reading is possible, but must explain this direct divine address; the Trinitarian reading has genuine textual weight.* Compare [John’s Witness](john-s-witness.draft.md#thomas-in-johns-ending).
