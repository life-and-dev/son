# Jesus Denies Being God

Many people believe that Jesus is God. However, a careful reading of the Gospels reveals several instances where Jesus' own words seem to draw a clear distinction between himself and God the Father. Did Jesus actually deny being God? Let's examine the scriptural evidence to see what he said about his own identity.

## Direct Denials

> So He said to him, “Why do you call me good? No one is good but **One, that is, God**. — Matthew 19:17 (NKJV); Mark 10:18; Luke 18:19

> So Jesus said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing...
>
> How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from **the only God**? 
> 
> Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father.
>
> — John 5:19,44-45 (ESV)

> Jesus answered, If I honour myself, my honour is nothing: it is my Father that honoureth me; of whom ye say, that **He is your God.** — John 8:54 (KJV)

> Jesus spoke these words, lifted up His eyes to heaven, and said: “Father, the hour has come. Glorify Your Son, that Your Son also may glorify You, as You have given him authority over all flesh, that he should give eternal life to as many as You have given him. And this is eternal life, that they may know You, **the only true God**, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent. — John 17:1-3 (NKJV)

## Implicit Denials

> But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, **nor the Son**, but **only the Father**. — Mark 13:32 (ESV)

By identifying himself as "[the Son](../index.md)" and the Father as a separate being who knows something he doesn't, Jesus implies a distinction between himself and the Father.

> Jesus answered, “I do not have a demon, but **I honor my Father**, and you dishonor me. Yet **I do not seek my own glory; there is One who seeks it, and He is the judge**. — John 8:49-50 (ESV)

Here again, Jesus draws a clear distinction between himself and his Father, whom he identifies as the ultimate judge.

## Counter-arguments

Some theologians explain these passages through the doctrine of *kenosis*, a Greek term meaning "to empty." This concept, drawn from Philippians 2:7, suggests that when Jesus became human, he voluntarily limited or "emptied himself" of his divine prerogatives, such as omniscience.

From this perspective:
*   When Jesus says he doesn't know the day or the hour (Mark 13:32), he is speaking from his human nature, having temporarily set aside his divine omniscience.
*   When he refers to the Father as "the only true God" (John 17:3), he is speaking in his role as a human, subordinate to the Father during his earthly ministry.

This view maintains that Jesus did not cease to be God, but chose not to access the full extent of his divine attributes while on earth.

However, critics of this view point out that the Bible never explicitly states that Jesus had two natures operating independently, or that he "set aside" his divine attributes. They argue that this interpretation is a later theological construct developed to resolve contradictions created by the doctrine of the Trinity.

## Conclusion

The scriptural evidence presented shows that Jesus made statements that both [directly](#direct-denials) and [implicitly](#implicit-denials) distinguished himself from God. His words in the Gospels consistently point to a relationship where the Father is the "only true God" and he is the Son sent by the Father. While some theological frameworks like [kenosis](#counter-arguments) offer explanations for these statements, a straightforward reading of Jesus' words suggests a clear distinction between himself and God.
