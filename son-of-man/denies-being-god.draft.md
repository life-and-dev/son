# Jesus Denies Being God

The nature of Jesus's divinity has been a central topic of theological debate for centuries. While many Christian doctrines affirm his godhead, a closer look at the Gospels reveals several instances where Jesus's own words appear to challenge this belief. This article examines the scriptural accounts where Jesus speaks about his relationship with God the Father, offering a deeper perspective on his identity.

## Direct Denies

> So He said to him, “Why do you call me good? No one is good but **One, that is, God**. — Matthew 19:17 (NKJV); Mark 10:18; Luke 18:19

> So Jesus said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing...
>
> How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from **the only God**? 
> 
> Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father.
>
> — John 5:19,44-45 (ESV)

> Jesus answered, If I honour myself, my honour is nothing: it is my Father that honoureth me; of whom ye say, that **He is your God.** — John 8:54 (KJV)

> Jesus spoke these words, lifted up His eyes to heaven, and said: “Father, the hour has come. Glorify Your Son, that Your Son also may glorify You, as You have given him authority over all flesh, that he should give eternal life to as many as You have given him. And this is eternal life, that they may know You, **the only true God**, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent. — John 17:1-3 (NKJV)

## Implicit Denies

> But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, **nor the Son**, but **only the Father**. — Mark 13:32 (ESV)

By referring to himself as ["the Son,"](../index.md) Jesus distinguishes his role from that of the Father, suggesting a separate identity.

> Jesus answered, “I do not have a demon, but **I honor my Father**, and you dishonor me. Yet **I do not seek my own glory; there is One who seeks it, and He is the judge**. — John 8:49-50 (ESV)

Here, Jesus draws a clear distinction between himself and the Father, which suggests he did not consider himself to be the Father.

## Scholarly Interpretations

It is important to consider how these passages are understood within mainstream Trinitarian theology. From this perspective, Jesus's statements are not seen as denials of his divinity but as affirmations of the distinction between the persons of the Trinity. For example, when Jesus says, "The Son can do nothing of his own accord," it is interpreted as reflecting the perfect unity and functional subordination of the Son to the Father, not a difference in essence.

Similarly, the declaration in John 17:3, referring to the Father as "the only true God," is understood as distinguishing the Father's role, not as excluding the Son from the Godhead. The verse in Mark 13:32, where Jesus states he does not know the hour, is often explained by the concept of *kenosis*, where Christ voluntarily limited his divine knowledge during his earthly ministry. These interpretations seek to harmonize the cited verses with other scriptures that affirm Jesus's divinity, such as John 1:1 and John 20:28.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, the Gospels present a nuanced picture of Jesus's identity. The scriptures record several [direct statements](#direct-denies) where Jesus appears to differentiate himself from God. Furthermore, there are [implicit instances](#implicit-denies) where his words suggest a subordinate relationship to the Father. While these passages can be interpreted as Jesus denying he is God, mainstream Christian theology offers [alternative interpretations](#scholarly-interpretations) that align with the doctrine of the Trinity. These passages are crucial for a comprehensive understanding of Christology and continue to be a subject of deep theological study.
