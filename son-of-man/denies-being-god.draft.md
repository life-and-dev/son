# Jesus Denies Being God

Many passages in the Gospels record instances where Jesus appears to deny being God. This article explores those statements in detail to provide a clearer and deeper understanding of his true identity.

## Direct Denials

> So He said to him, “Why do you call me good? No one is good but **One, that is, God**. — Matthew 19:17 (NKJV); Mark 10:18; Luke 18:19

Here, Jesus makes a clear distinction between himself and God, attributing goodness solely to God.

> So Jesus said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing...
>
> How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from **the only God**? 
> 
> Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father.
>
> — John 5:19,44-45 (ESV)

In this passage, Jesus positions himself as subordinate to the Father, able to act only in accordance with what he sees the Father doing. He further reinforces this by referring to 'the only God' as a separate being from himself.

> Jesus answered, If I honour myself, my honour is nothing: it is my Father that honoureth me; of whom ye say, that **He is your God.** — John 8:54 (KJV)

Jesus again distinguishes himself from God, stating that his honor comes from his Father, whom the people he is addressing consider their God.

> Jesus spoke these words, lifted up His eyes to heaven, and said: “Father, the hour has come. Glorify Your Son, that Your Son also may glorify You, as You have given him authority over all flesh, that he should give eternal life to as many as You have given him. And this is eternal life, that they may know You, **the only true God**, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent. — John 17:1-3 (NKJV)

In this prayer, Jesus addresses the Father as "the only true God" and refers to himself as "Jesus Christ whom You have sent," clearly defining two distinct beings.

## Implicit Denials

> But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, **nor the Son**, but **only the Father**. — Mark 13:32 (ESV)

Jesus admits a limitation to his own knowledge, something that would not be true if he were the omniscient God. By stating that only the Father knows the hour, he places himself in a position subordinate to the Father.

> Jesus answered, “I do not have a demon, but **I honor my Father**, and you dishonor me. Yet **I do not seek my own glory; there is One who seeks it, and He is the judge**. — John 8:49-50 (ESV)

Once more, Jesus distinguishes himself from the Father, stating that he honors the Father and does not seek his own glory. This reinforces the idea of two separate beings with a hierarchical relationship.

## Addressing Counter-Arguments

Those who believe Jesus is God often argue that in these passages, Jesus is speaking from his human nature, not his divine nature. This is often referred to as the [doctrine of *kenosis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenosis), where Jesus voluntarily emptied himself of some of his divine attributes. For example, when Jesus states that he does not know the day or the hour (Mark 13:32), it is suggested that he is speaking from the perspective of his limited human consciousness.

Similarly, in John 17:3, where Jesus refers to the Father as "the only true God," it is argued that this does not exclude Jesus from also being God. The reasoning is that the Father is the only true God, but Jesus is also of the same divine essence as the Father.

However, these interpretations require reading concepts into the text that are not explicitly stated. The Bible does not describe Jesus as having two separate consciousnesses or natures that speak independently of each other. When Jesus speaks, he speaks as a single, unified person. The simplest and most direct reading of these passages is that Jesus is presenting himself as a being distinct from, and subordinate to, God the Father.

## Conclusion

The passages reviewed in this article present a consistent theme of Jesus distinguishing himself from God.
- In his [direct denials](#direct-denials), he points to God as the only one who is good and the only true God.
- In his [implicit denials](#implicit-denials), he acknowledges limitations in his own knowledge and glorifies the Father.
- While [counter-arguments](#addressing-counter-arguments) exist, they often rely on complex theological frameworks that are not explicitly found in the biblical text.

These statements are crucial for understanding the nature of Jesus and his relationship with God the Father.
