# Jesus Denies Being God

The Gospels portray a complex picture of Jesus's identity. While many passages are cited to affirm his divinity, others present a different perspective. This article explores several key statements from Jesus that appear to challenge the traditional understanding of his relationship with God, inviting a closer examination of his own words on the matter.

The Gospels record several instances where Jesus appears to deny being God.

## Direct Denials

> So He said to him, “Why do you call me good? No one is good but **One, that is, God**. — Matthew 19:17 (NKJV); Mark 10:18; Luke 18:19

and

> So Jesus said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing...
>
> How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from **the only God**? 
> 
> Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father.
>
> — John 5:19,44-45 (ESV)

and

> Jesus answered, “If I honour myself, my honour is nothing: it is my Father that honoureth me; of whom ye say, that **He is your God.**” — John 8:54 (KJV)

and

> Jesus spoke these words, lifted up His eyes to heaven, and said: “Father, the hour has come. Glorify Your Son, that Your Son also may glorify You, as You have given him authority over all flesh, that he should give eternal life to as many as You have given him. And this is eternal life, that they may know You, **the only true God**, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent. — John 17:1-3 (NKJV)

## Implicit Denials

> But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, **nor the Son**, but **only the Father**. — Mark 13:32 (ESV)

[Jesus called himself "the Son"](../index.md), therefore he implied that he is not our Father God.

and

> Jesus answered, “I do not have a demon, but **I honor my Father**, and you dishonor me. Yet **I do not seek my own glory; there is One who seeks it, and He is the judge**. — John 8:49-50 (ESV)

Jesus made a clear distinction between himself and the Father. This implies that Jesus is not the Father.

## Critiques

The passages cited above are often interpreted differently within Trinitarian theology, which holds that God exists as three co-equal and co-eternal persons: the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ), and the Holy Spirit. From this perspective, Jesus's statements are not seen as denials of his divinity but as reflections of his role and his human nature.

*   **On "the only true God" (John 17:3) and "the only God" (John 5:44):** Trinitarians argue that these statements emphasize the monotheistic nature of God in contrast to the polytheism of the surrounding cultures. They do not exclude the Son from the Godhead but rather affirm that the Father and the Son together constitute the one true God. The phrase "and Jesus Christ whom You have sent" in John 17:3 is seen as inclusive, defining the complete object of saving knowledge.

*   **On "Why do you call me good?" (Matthew 19:17):** This is often interpreted not as a denial of his own goodness, but as a rhetorical question to make the young man consider the source of goodness. Jesus is essentially asking, "Do you recognize that the 'goodness' you see in me is divine?" thereby prompting a deeper understanding of his identity.

*   **On "nor the Son" knows the hour (Mark 13:32):** This is perhaps the most debated passage. The common explanation is the doctrine of *kenosis*, which posits that Jesus voluntarily emptied himself of certain divine prerogatives during his earthly ministry. In his humanity, he chose to not know the day or the hour, submitting to the Father's will. This is seen as a limitation of his human consciousness, not a limitation of his divine nature.

## Conclusion

The question of Jesus's divinity is central to Christian theology, and his own words are at the heart of the debate. The passages examined in this article highlight a recurring theme in the Gospels where Jesus appears to subordinate himself to the Father, both directly and implicitly.

*   He makes [direct statements](#direct-denials) that distinguish himself from God, referring to the Father as "the only God" and "the only true God."
*   He also makes [implicit denials](#implicit-denials) of his own omniscience and authority, pointing to the Father as the ultimate source.

While Trinitarian theology offers explanations for these passages, as discussed in the ["Critiques"](#critiques) section, the plain reading of these verses continues to fuel discussion about the precise nature of Jesus's relationship with God. Ultimately, these statements remain a crucial element in the ongoing scholarly and devotional exploration of one of history's most influential figures.
