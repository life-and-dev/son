# God Does Not Have Flesh and Blood

Scripture ordinarily distinguishes God, who is spirit and immortal, from mortal “flesh and blood.” This supports a human-Messiah reading of Jesus, but the argument must answer the classical claim that the eternal Son truly assumed human nature without turning the divine nature into flesh.

## God’s Invisible Life and Jesus’ Body

The Old Testament ordinarily distinguishes the Creator from embodied and mortal creatures. Moses is told, “you cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live” (Exodus 33:20, ESV). Isaiah declares, “The LORD is the **everlasting God**” (Isaiah 40:28, ESV). Jesus says, “God is **spirit**” (John 4:24, ESV). These texts do not mean that God can never reveal Himself through visions or signs. They distinguish His unseen, immortal life from creaturely life.

The risen Jesus makes a bodily distinction equally plain: “a spirit does not have **flesh and bones** as you see that I have” (Luke 24:39, ESV). He is seen, touched, eats, suffers, dies, and is raised. Hebrews says that he shared “flesh and blood” so that through death he might defeat the devil (Hebrews 2:14-15). This is positive evidence for Jesus’ full humanity, not merely an appearance of humanity.

The texts should not be overstated. They do not say that God cannot act in, with, or through a human being. Their force is that Jesus’ actual bodily life is not itself a description of God’s invisible life.

## Incarnation and the Unitarian Conclusion

The strongest Trinitarian reply is not that God’s divine nature became material. It is that the eternal Son assumed a complete human nature while remaining what he eternally was. John says, “the Word became flesh” (John 1:14, ESV); Hebrews joins the Son’s high status with his real sharing of flesh and blood (Hebrews 1:3; 2:14-18). On this account, hunger, weakness, death, service, and prayer belong to the incarnate Son’s human life. They do not automatically disprove two natures.

The Unitarian case asks what these passages explicitly identify. God is spirit and unseen; Jesus has flesh and bones. Peter calls Jesus “a **man** attested to you by God” and says God raised him (Acts 2:22,24, ESV). Paul distinguishes “one God” from “the **man** Christ Jesus” who mediates between God and humanity (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). This recurring source-and-agent pattern makes the distinction substantive, rather than a temporary detail of Jesus’ earthly mission.

John 1:14 remains important, but the phrase itself does not state the later two-natures doctrine. That doctrine arises from a wider Trinitarian synthesis. *The warranted Unitarian conclusion is that Scripture identifies God as unseen spirit and Jesus as God’s embodied human Messiah; incarnation is a coherent Trinitarian explanation, but it is not the only reading of this evidence.* See [Jesus’ humanity](human.md#jesus-is-human) and [Jesus’ distinction from God](distinct.md#jesus-interacted-with-god).
