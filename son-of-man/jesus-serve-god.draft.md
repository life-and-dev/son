# Jesus Serves God

Jesus worships, obeys, and serves the one he calls God. These texts positively identify his messianic relation to the Father, though service alone does not refute the classical claim that the incarnate Son obeys according to his assumed human nature.

## The Obedient Servant

Jesus describes his mission as service: “the Son of Man came not to be served but to **serve**, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45, ESV). In Gethsemane he prays, “not what I will, but what you will” (Mark 14:36, ESV). These are not incidental scenes. They identify obedience to God’s will as central to his saving vocation.

This vocation has Old Testament foundations. The LORD’s servant is vindicated after bearing the sins of many (Isaiah 52:13-53:12). When Philip explains that passage, he proclaims Jesus (Acts 8:32-35). Acts repeatedly calls Jesus God’s “holy servant” (Acts 3:13,26; 4:27,30). Jesus therefore fulfils a recognisable biblical category: God’s chosen, suffering, and vindicated servant-Messiah.

This is positive Unitarian evidence. Jesus consciously worships, prays to, obeys, and serves the God who sent him. His service is unique and saving, but unique service does not require identity with the God served.

## Fasting, Dependence, and Temptation

Jesus’ fasting further displays his real human dependence and obedience. “Then **Jesus was led up by the Spirit** into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. And after **fasting** forty days and forty nights, he was hungry” (Matthew 4:1-2, ESV). The narrative joins the Spirit’s leading, voluntary fasting, hunger, and temptation.

For the Unitarian case, this is evidence that Jesus lives before God as the obedient Messiah, not simply that he performs a religious example. It coheres with his prayer that the Father’s will be done (Mark 14:36). For the Trinitarian case, genuine hunger and temptation belong to the Son’s assumed humanity. Hebrews says that he was tempted as humans are, yet without sin (Hebrews 4:15). Thus fasting is neither a simple disproof of incarnation nor an incidental detail; it is part of the Gospel portrayal of Jesus’ faithful human obedience.

## Two Natures and the Unitarian Conclusion

The strongest Trinitarian reply holds that the Son’s obedience belongs to his incarnate mission. Philippians 2:6-8 is often read as pre-existent divine self-humbling: one existing in God’s form takes a servant’s form and becomes obedient unto death. On this reading, service does not show that the Son lacks divine nature; it shows voluntary humility in the humanity he assumed. Hebrews likewise says that Jesus learned obedience through suffering and became the source of eternal salvation (Hebrews 5:7-10).

An Adamic reading of Philippians 2 is also serious. It reads Christ as the obedient human counterpart to Adam rather than as a pre-existent person descending from heaven. Both readings must account for the poem’s stated sequence: “Therefore God has **highly exalted** him and **bestowed** on him the name that is above every name” (Philippians 2:9, ESV). Exaltation establishes extraordinary God-given lordship; it does not by itself decide the question of pre-existence.

The Unitarian reading therefore does not infer “not God” from service alone. It draws its conclusion from service together with Jesus’ prayers, God’s commissioning, and God’s exaltation of him. *The warranted Unitarian conclusion is that Jesus serves God as the promised Servant-Messiah and is uniquely exalted by God; Trinitarian incarnation remains a possible synthesis, but these texts directly maintain a God-Messiah distinction.* See [Jesus serving God](serve-god.md#jesus-serves-god) and [Jesus’ humanity](human.md#jesus-is-human).
