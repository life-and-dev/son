# Jesus Was a Blood Sacrifice

Jesus’ death is central to Christian salvation. Christians, including Unitarians and Trinitarians, agree that he gave himself for sinners, that his blood is connected to the new covenant, and that God raised him from the dead. They differ over how his identity relates to that saving efficacy. Israel’s sacrificial background matters: Leviticus 17:11 assigns atoning significance to blood because God gives it upon the altar, and Isaiah 53 portrays the servant’s suffering within God’s redemptive purpose.

## Nicene Account: the Incarnate Son's Unique Saving Efficacy

The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed confesses one Lord Jesus Christ as “true God from true God,” who “came down from heaven,” “was incarnate ... and became human,” was crucified, rose, and ascended. This account does not say God merely appeared to suffer. It says the eternal Son truly assumed human life and offered that life in obedience.

On this reading, the incarnate Son’s divine identity gives his death singular saving significance. John identifies the Word as God and says the Word became flesh (John 1:1,14). Hebrews calls the Son the exact imprint of God’s nature while saying that he shared flesh and blood, suffered, and helps those tempted (Hebrews 1:3, 2:14-18). Trinitarians therefore speak of the divine Son’s saving death **in** the human nature he assumed, not of an impassible divine nature ceasing to be divine.

Some defenders reason that sin against an infinite God requires an infinitely valuable sacrifice, or that a finite human could not atone. Those are theological inferences, not biblical rules stated in those terms. The texts themselves establish the Son’s incarnation, obedient death, resurrection, priesthood, and reign.

## Project Account: God's Covenant Through the Obedient Human Messiah

The Unitarian account emphasises God’s saving initiative through the genuinely human Messiah. Scripture calls Jesus the one mediator between God and humankind, “the **man** Christ Jesus,” who gave himself as a ransom for all (1 Timothy 2:3-6). As death came through Adam, resurrection comes through Christ, also described as a man (1 Corinthians 15:21-22). This is a positive representative pattern, not a claim that Jesus was ordinary. See [Jesus’ humanity](human.md#jesus-is-human).

Jesus names his death covenantal: his blood is “the blood of the covenant” poured out for forgiveness (Matthew 26:26-28). He says the Son of Man came to serve and give his life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45). These sayings echo the Servant’s representative suffering in Isaiah 53 and locate the cross in God’s covenantal provision rather than a bargain with the devil.

Hebrews adds priesthood. Jesus shared human weakness without sin, became high priest, entered through his own blood, and mediates the new covenant (Hebrews 4:14-16, 9:11-15,24-28). His obedient death fulfils God’s purpose: through the obedience of the one man many are made righteous (Romans 5:18-19; Philippians 2:8).

God then raised and exalted Jesus. Peter says God raised the crucified Jesus and made him Lord and Christ (Acts 2:23-24,32-36); Paul says God highly exalted the obedient Jesus (Philippians 2:9-11). This account explains the sacrifice by God’s provision, Christ’s faithful human mediation, and God’s vindication.

## Shared Confession and Further Reading

Both accounts affirm that Jesus’ death was real, saving, covenantal, and inseparable from resurrection. They differ on whether saving efficacy requires the incarnate Son’s divine identity or is explained by God’s work through the obedient human Messiah. Neither side should treat “only an infinite sacrifice can save” or “a finite human cannot save” as explicit biblical rules.

*The warranted conclusion is that Scripture presents God’s supplied, sinless Messiah as sacrificial ransom, priest, and risen Lord; the texts supporting this confession do not by themselves settle the metaphysical account of his person.* See [Jesus’ humanity](human.md#jesus-is-human), [Jesus serving God](serve-god.md#jesus-serves-god), and [the covenant of Christ](../../../kingdom/covenants/christ.md).

## Does Atonement Require a Divine Victim?

The strongest Trinitarian argument is that Christ’s divine identity gives his sacrifice incomparable value. The New Testament calls his offering once for all and sufficient (Hebrews 9:26-28, 10:10-14), calls him righteous (1 John 2:1-2), and Acts 20:28 speaks of the church’s purchase through blood. In Trinitarian interpretation, this coheres with the Word becoming flesh (John 1:1,14).

Acts 20:28 requires three separate judgements. **Textual variant:** NA28 prints *tēn ekklēsian tou theou* (“the church of God”); manuscripts also read “the church of the Lord” or “the church of the Lord and God.” **Translation:** with the NA28 wording, *dia tou haimatos tou idiou* may be rendered “with his own blood,” as in ESV, or understood idiomatically as “with the blood of his own [Son].” **Interpretation:** “God’s own blood” is read by Trinitarians in light of incarnation, while the “own Son” rendering is compatible with the Father giving the Son’s blood. The NET Bible textual note on Acts 20:28 discusses both the variant readings and this translation question. No one of these decisions should be treated as though it settled the others.

The Unitarian reply is not that Christ’s death was merely a finite human act. Scripture grounds its sufficiency in God’s saving purpose and appointment. God presented Christ as the atoning sacrifice and raised him (Romans 3:24-26, 4:24-25). It calls him the human mediator who gave himself as ransom and connects his representative obedience with Adam’s disobedience (1 Timothy 2:5-6; Romans 5:18-19). Neither ransom nor priestly texts explicitly says that his blood saves because he is God.
