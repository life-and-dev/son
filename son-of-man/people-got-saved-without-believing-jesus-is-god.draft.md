# Peter's sermon

Peter’s Pentecost sermon identifies the God of Israel as actor throughout. God attests Jesus as “a **man**” by miracles God does through him, raises him, and exalts him to God’s right hand (Acts 2:22-24,32-33). Peter uses Psalm 110:1 to distinguish “the LORD” from David’s “Lord,” then concludes that God made the crucified Jesus “both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:34-36).

The strongest Trinitarian reading joins Joel 2:32, cited in Acts 2:21, with the risen Jesus’ title “Lord” and with salvation in Jesus’ name. On this reading, Peter applies language belonging to the LORD to Jesus and thus signals more than delegated status. This is a serious challenge to a merely functional reading.

Yet Peter’s immediate exposition also grounds Jesus’ lordship in God’s resurrection and exaltation. The sermon calls hearers to repent, be baptised in Jesus’ name, and receive forgiveness (Acts 2:38). It does not make a metaphysical formula about Jesus’ deity an explicit condition of forgiveness. That omission cannot prove what every apostolic witness believed, because sermons address partial audiences and purposes. It can show only what this sermon expressly requires.

# Paul's sermon in Antioch

In Antioch, Paul begins with “the God of this people Israel,” who chose the fathers, gave Israel David, and fulfilled His promise (Acts 13:17,22-23). Jesus appears within that promise as David’s descendant: “from this man’s offspring God has brought to Israel a Saviour, Jesus” (Acts 13:23). Paul grounds the good news in God’s raising of Jesus and offers forgiveness and justification through him (Acts 13:30,38-39).

A Trinitarian may reply that the Old Testament calls the LORD the only Saviour, so Jesus as Saviour participates in the LORD’s saving identity. The counter-reading preserves Paul’s source-and-agent wording: God brings the Davidic Saviour and raises him, while Jesus is the promised descendant through whom God saves. “Saviour” can refer both to God as ultimate deliverer and to His appointed means (compare Judges 3:9). The title alone does not settle identity.

Paul calls hearers to believe God’s fulfilled promise concerning His raised Messiah (Acts 13:32-33,38-39). This sermon gives Jesus a decisive saving office without stating belief that Jesus is God as its condition of forgiveness.

# Paul's sermon in Areopagus

At the Areopagus, Paul identifies God as Creator. God made the world and all people, gives life and breath, commands all people to repent, and will judge the world in righteousness (Acts 17:24-27,30-31). Paul identifies the judge as “a **man** whom he has **appointed**,” giving assurance by saying God raised him from the dead.

Trinitarians rightly observe that the wider New Testament gives Jesus universal judgement and calls for honour to the Son (John 5:22-23). Acts 17 itself, however, explains the judge’s authority by appointment. It presents God as universal judge and Jesus as His risen human representative. Appointment does not establish that the appointed man is God, but neither does it make his authority insignificant.

*These sermons support the conclusion that apostolic evangelism proclaimed repentance, God’s raising of Jesus, and his saving lordship without always stating a doctrine of Christ’s deity as an entry condition. They do not prove silence about that doctrine everywhere or exhaust apostolic Christology.* See [Jesus’ humanity](human.md#jesus-is-human) and [Jesus’ distinction from God](distinct.md#jesus-interacted-with-god).
