## Strongest Trinitarian Reading and Reply

A strong Trinitarian reading gives full weight to passages that describe the Word as God and Jesus as sharing glory with the Father, including John 1:1 and John 17:5. It also notes Thomas’s confession, “My Lord and my God,” in John 20:28. Philippians 2:6-11 is commonly read as portraying pre-existent divine status, voluntary humiliation, and universal homage. On this reading, Scripture’s centre is the incarnate Son, not merely a human representative. Jesus’ humanity, service, and dependence on the Father do not by themselves rule out incarnation or two natures.

The positive Unitarian case is that the Bible’s covenant story repeatedly identifies the God of Israel as the Father and presents Jesus as His promised human Messiah. Peter calls Jesus “a man attested to you by God” and says that God raised him (Acts 2:22,24). Paul names “one God, the Father, from whom are all things” and “one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things” (1 Corinthians 8:6). Many Trinitarians see this coordinated “from” and “through” language as a reworked Shema that includes Jesus within God’s identity. Unitarians see differentiated titles and roles: the Father is the one God and ultimate source, while the one Lord is God’s appointed agent. Both readings must explain the deliberate echo of Israel’s confession in Deuteronomy 6:4.

Philippians 2 also has more than one serious reading. The pre-existence reading takes “form of God” and “emptied himself” as descent before incarnation. An Adamic reading sees Jesus refusing wrongful grasping and humbling himself as the obedient human counterpart to Adam. The poem’s indisputable narrative is obedience unto death followed by God’s action: “Therefore God has **highly exalted** him and **bestowed** on him the name that is above every name” (Philippians 2:9, ESV). That sequence strongly supports God’s vindication of Messiah, while it does not alone settle the pre-existence question.

The Spirit belongs in this biblical pattern as God’s active presence. Luke 1:35 parallels the Holy Spirit with “the power of the Most High,” and Acts 1:8 connects receiving the Holy Spirit with receiving power. These observations do not deny the force of Trinitarian texts or make the Spirit impersonal by definition.
