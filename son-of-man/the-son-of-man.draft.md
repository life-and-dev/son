# Jesus compared to Adam

Paul builds his resurrection argument by comparing Adam and Christ as human representatives:

> “For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead.” — 1 Corinthians 15:21 (ESV)

> “The first man Adam became a living being”; the last Adam became a life-giving spirit. — 1 Corinthians 15:45 (ESV)

> “The first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven.” — 1 Corinthians 15:47 (ESV)

Adam’s disobedience brought death to those identified with him. Christ’s obedience and resurrection bring life to those identified with him (Romans 5:12-19; 1 Corinthians 15:21-22,45-49). Paul therefore calls Jesus “the **man** Christ Jesus” in describing his mediation (1 Timothy 2:5-6).

The strongest Trinitarian reading takes “the second man is from heaven” as evidence that the Son existed in heaven before becoming human, alongside John 1:1,14. A contextual alternative reads Paul’s contrast as earthly, mortal Adam against the risen Christ’s heavenly, life-giving status. Paul says believers will bear the image of “the man of heaven” (1 Corinthians 15:49). Neither reading may claim that the phrase alone proves its whole account of Christ’s origin.

The comparison does not make Jesus ordinary or deny his unique origin, sinlessness, authority, and resurrection. It identifies him as God’s appointed human representative. God raised the man Jesus and appointed him judge (Acts 17:31); through the obedience of this one man, many are made righteous (Romans 5:18-19).

# Jesus is not God

Scripture does not use the sentence “Jesus is not God.” The Unitarian case rests instead on repeated distinctions between the one God and the Messiah He sent. Jesus calls the Father “the only true God” and identifies himself as the one sent by that God (John 17:3). After resurrection, he says, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (John 20:17). Paul distinguishes “one God, the Father” from “one Lord, Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:6).

Trinitarians reply that these texts distinguish persons, not natures: the incarnate Son can call the Father his God while sharing divine nature. They point to Thomas’s confession, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28), the Word being God before becoming flesh (John 1:1,14), and Daniel 7:13-14. In Daniel, one like a son of man comes with the clouds, receives everlasting dominion, and is served by all peoples. Jesus’ use of “Son of Man,” especially with cloud-coming imagery (Mark 14:62), is therefore stronger than a generic claim to humanity. It may invoke Daniel’s exalted heavenly figure.

A Unitarian can affirm Daniel 7’s extraordinary exaltation while noting its distinction: the human-like figure receives dominion from the Ancient of Days. The chapter’s interpretation also relates the kingdom to “the saints of the Most High” (Daniel 7:18,27). Daniel establishes delegated, universal, everlasting rule; whether it identifies the recipient with God remains disputed.

Paul also says, “Therefore God has **highly exalted** him and **bestowed** on him the name that is above every name” (Philippians 2:9, ESV). *The warranted conclusion is that Jesus is the unique, exalted Son of Man and God’s Messiah. The repeated God-Messiah distinction supports the Unitarian reading, but Daniel 7 and Johannine texts require a serious Trinitarian reply.* See [Jesus’ humanity](human.md#jesus-is-human) and [Jesus’ distinction from God](distinct.md#daniel-prophesied-jesuss-distinction).
