# Arianism

This draft expands the [development overview](../development.md) by considering Arianism precisely. It neither makes every non-Nicene reading Arian nor attributes a universal archangel claim to Arius.

## Definition

Arianism broadly identifies the Son as pre-existent but created, not coeternal or consubstantial with the Father. The term covers a historical controversy and related theological positions, not one exhaustively documented system. [Britannica's account](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arianism) is a useful secondary definition, but it cannot by itself establish every detail of Arius's theology. The claim that the Son is an archangel belongs to some later interpretations, not a universal definition of Arianism.

## Biblical Tests

### Old Testament

Isaiah 43:10, 44:6, and 45:5 identify the LORD uniquely as **God**. These texts challenge any proposal of a second deity alongside the Almighty. They do not explicitly address a pre-existent Son, so neither side should claim that they alone resolve the later controversy. They establish the monotheistic starting point described in [the Shema](../../shema.md).

### New Testament

John 1 and Colossians 1 are the strongest texts for a pre-existent Son. John says that the Word was “with **God**” and that the Word “was **God**,” then speaks of creation through him. Colossians calls Christ “the **firstborn** of all creation,” then speaks of all things being created through and for him. Arian readings may take “firstborn” as priority within creation and “through” as delegated agency. Nicene readings take the same context as excluding creaturehood because all created things came through the Son. Grammar, context, and theological premises matter; neither reading follows merely from isolating one title.

First Timothy 2:5 calls Jesus Christ the “**man**” who mediates between God and humanity. It positively supports Jesus' humanity, but humanity alone does not disprove pre-existence. Acts 2:22 likewise calls him “a **man** attested to you by God.” The defended reading holds that these clear descriptions fit the human Son sent by the Father, but it should not claim they formally refute every pre-existence proposal.

## Premises and Inferences

An Arian conclusion requires an inference: “**firstborn**” means first created, or “**through him**” means God first created the Son and then used him. Those interpretations are arguable; they are not formal consequences of the words alone. Conversely, reading John 1 as eternal equality requires further premises about “the Word was **God**,” personhood, and the relation of Word to Jesus.

The Father-alone reading refuses the created lesser-deity alternative as unnecessary. It identifies the Father as the one Almighty God, Jesus as His human Christ, and the Spirit as God's active interaction. This is distinct from saying that Arianism is internally incoherent. A pre-existent created agent can be stated consistently; its biblical warrant is the disputed question.

The category must also distinguish creation from commission. Scripture often describes God working through an appointed agent, but agency alone does not reveal whether that agent existed before birth or was the first creature. Likewise, a unique role in creation would not by itself decide whether “God” names the agent's nature, authority, or representation. The historical Arian answer joins several premises; no single proof text states the whole system.

## Strongest Defence and Reply

Its strongest defence is that it takes seriously the Son's dependence, begetting language, and the Father as the one unbegotten God. It offers a real distinction without making two equal ultimates.

Within the defended Father-alone reading, the reply is that a supreme creature adds a heavenly being which the explicit human-Messiah texts do not require. This is an objection to biblical warrant, not a formal disproof of pre-existence or a claim that the model is self-contradictory. The Arian account must explain why honourific “god” language does not create a rival true god and why the apostles so often distinguish God from Christ. The defended reading begins with the explicit human Messiah texts and leaves disputed cosmic readings open rather than turning them immediately into an ontology.

## Conclusion

Arianism is defined by a [created pre-existent Son](#definition), not by every low-Christology view. Its strongest texts require interpretation, while [human-Messiah texts](#new-testament) support the Father-alone reading without providing a formal disproof of pre-existence.
