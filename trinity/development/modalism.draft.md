# Modalism

At Jesus' baptism, he stands in the water while the Spirit descends and a voice speaks from heaven. How should readers understand the Father, the Son, and the Spirit in scenes like this? This article expands the [development overview](../development.md) by explaining modalism, examining its historical reception, and testing its central claim against Scripture.

## What Is Modalism?

Modalism holds that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are not three distinct personal subjects but different ways in which one divine personal subject is known or acts. It is sometimes associated with the names Sabellius and Praxeas. The word describes a *one-person* account of the three terms, not simply belief in one God. Christians who distinguish the Father from the Son can also affirm one God.

Some descriptions portray God acting first as Father, then as Son, then as Spirit. That sequence is **not** a fair definition of every version: one subject may be said to act in these ways at the same time. Likewise, an early opponent's description of a teaching is not the teacher's own surviving statement. This article evaluates the one-person claim rather than the sincerity of its adherents.

## Who Holds This View Today?

[The United Pentecostal Church International (UPCI)](https://upci.org/our-beliefs/) is a present-day example of an **Oneness Pentecostal** body. Its statement of belief describes one God revealed as Father, in his Son Jesus Christ, and as the Holy Spirit, and calls Jesus God manifested in flesh. Other Oneness or Apostolic Pentecostal congregations also teach versions of this view. **Not all Pentecostals are Oneness Pentecostals**, and a person using the phrase “one God” does not necessarily hold modalism.

## How Is It Explained Today?

Oneness believers commonly identify the Father with the one God and regard the Holy Spirit as that God's Spirit. They identify Jesus as the one God's manifestation in flesh. On this account, “Son” refers especially to the real human life and birth of Jesus, while “Father” speaks of the divine identity. They therefore explain Jesus' prayers as the genuine humanity of Jesus addressing God, not necessarily as one actor pretending to pray. The [UPCI belief statement](https://upci.org/our-beliefs/) explicitly calls Jesus both God and man.

This account can accommodate the simultaneous voice, Son, and descending Spirit at Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:16-17): one God can speak from heaven and act by his Spirit while present in the incarnate Son. **Simultaneous appearances alone do not disprove modern Oneness belief.** The harder question is whether the Father and Jesus can be the distinct sender and sent one, and the *two* witnesses Jesus names, if they are ultimately the same personal subject.

## Historical Rejection

### Early Christian Writers

Around the early third century, [Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*, chapters 1-2 and 9](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0317.htm), opposed a teaching he associated with Praxeas that collapsed the Father and Son into one personal identity. He argued instead that the Father and Son must be distinguished. [Hippolytus, *Against the Heresy of One Noetus*, chapters 1-3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0521.htm), similarly reported that Noetus identified the Father with the Son and opposed that identification. These are **opponents' accounts**, not surviving writings by Praxeas or Noetus; they establish early rejection of the claim without proving that every modern Oneness explanation matches an ancient opponent's description.

### Creeds and Later Reception

The [Nicene Creed in Catholic use](https://www.usccb.org/prayers/nicene-creed) names the one God as Father, distinguishes Jesus Christ as his Son, and speaks of the Holy Spirit. Its language distinguishes Father and Son, although the creed should not be misrepresented as an explicit, stand-alone condemnation of every form of modalism. The later [Athanasian Creed](https://bookofconcord.org/athanasian-creed/) is more direct: it says *“neither confounding the persons nor dividing the substance”* and distinguishes the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

This rejection remains part of established church teaching, not merely an ancient curiosity. The [Catholic Catechism, paragraphs 253-255](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P17.HTM), teaches that the divine persons are really distinct; the [Westminster Confession of Faith, chapter 2.3](https://opc.org/wcf.html#Chapter_02), received by confessional Presbyterian Protestants, likewise distinguishes three persons in the one Godhead. These sources **do not** establish that every Protestant group accepts every creed or agrees with every church father. Nor does citing them endorse their teaching that the Son and Spirit are coequal divine persons: they are historical witnesses against **one-person modalism**, whereas the biblical case below evaluates the texts on their own terms.

## Biblical Evidence

The Father-alone reading presented here identifies the Father as Almighty God, Jesus as his distinct human Son and Christ, and the Spirit as God's active presence and power. It does **not** infer three gods or three equal divine persons from the distinctions in Scripture. Deuteronomy 6:4 and Isaiah 44:6 affirm the **one God**; neither says that the Father and his appointed Son are one personal subject.

The older prophetic pattern distinguishes God, his Spirit, and his servant. In Isaiah 42:1, God says, *“I have put my **Spirit** upon **him**”* (ESV). Matthew 12:17-21 applies that servant passage to Jesus. In the same pattern, Isaiah 61:1 describes the speaker as anointed by the Spirit and sent by the Lord GOD; Jesus applies this passage to his mission in Luke 4:17-21. The New Testament application confirms the messianic reading rather than making the prophetic speaker identical with the sender.

Jesus then appeals to a legal distinction: *“the testimony of **two people** is true”*, naming himself and *“the **Father who sent me**”* (John 8:17-18, ESV). He addresses the Father as *“the only true **God**”* and himself as *“Jesus Christ whom you have **sent**”* (John 17:3, ESV). His appeal to two witnesses bears more weight against a one-subject reading than the baptism scene alone. His prayer *“not as **I will**, but as **you will**”* (Matthew 26:39, ESV) likewise presents a real relationship, not merely three visual signs. The same Father-Son relations are surveyed in [Jesus Interacted With God](../../son-of-man/distinct.md).

**Oneness reply:** The human Son can pray to and testify alongside the divine Father without denying that Jesus' divine identity is the Father. **Response:** That preserves a real human experience, but it must still explain why Jesus invokes the legal standard of *two people* in John 8:17-18 if the Father and Son are only one personal subject. The apostolic distinction is similarly direct: *“there is one **God**, and there is one **mediator** between God and men, **the man Christ Jesus**”* (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). A mediator is distinguished from the God to whom he mediates. Neither argument requires treating God's Spirit as another god.

| John 14:16-17 | Gives the Spirit at Jesus' request | The promised Spirit of truth who remains with the disciples | Asks the Father for the Spirit |
| Acts 2:32-33 | Raises Jesus; promises the Spirit | Received by the exalted Jesus and poured out | Raised and exalted; receives the promised Spirit and pours it out |
| 1 Timothy 2:5 | The one God to whom the mediator relates | Not named | The man Christ Jesus, mediator between God and people |

One subject could produce three simultaneous signs, but the [two witnesses](#biblical-evidence), sending, asking, receiving, and mediating in these passages describe more than changes of appearance. The table's Spirit column records **God's Spirit in action**; the decisive distinction for modalism is that the Father and Son are not the same personal subject.

## Conclusion

The [historical witnesses](#historical-rejection) rejected the confusion of Father and Son, but Scripture supplies the decisive test. The [biblical relationships and comparison](#biblical-evidence) show the Father and his human Son as genuinely distinct while God's Spirit acts among them. **Modalism's one-person claim is unbiblical and disproved by these distinctions.**
