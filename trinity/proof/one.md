# Defining “One”

When does “one” mean 1 individual, and when does it describe people who are united? That distinction affects how readers understand God, the Father, Jesus, and believers.

## Words for One

### Hebrew: *ʾeḥad* and *ʾaḥat*

Hebrew [אֶחָד, *ʾeḥad*](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h259/kjv/wlc/0-1/) is the ordinary masculine numeral for “one”; אַחַת, *ʾaḥat*, is its feminine form. Like English “one”, it can count one individual, identify the same one, express only or exclusive in a fitting setting, or describe people acting together. Its form matches the grammatical gender of the noun it describes. The sentence and its setting show what is being counted or characterised.

For example *echad* is used for the simple numerical one in:

- Genesis 42:11,13 where the brothers refer to themselves as sons of "one" (*echad*) man, and note that "one" (*echad*) of their brothers is no more.
- Exodus 26:6 where the curtains are coupled together so that the tabernacle forms "one" (*echad*) structure.

However, Trinitarians favour the view that the Hebrew word *echad* refers to a composite unity where multiple persons when it reference God. To proof this point they often quote *echad* in Genesis:

> Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one *[echad]* flesh. — Genesis 2:24 (ESV)

[**וְהָיוּ לְבָשָׂר אֶחָד** (*wəhāyū ləḇāśār ʾeḥāḏ*)](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0102.htm), “and they shall become one flesh”. Husband and wife do not become one individual. Marriage joins two distinguishable people into "one flesh". This is real unity between people, sometimes called corporate unity, meaning people acting and belonging together.

Based on this usage, Trinitarians apply the same "unified" meaning to *echad* in theological declarations:

> "Hear, O Israel! [Yahweh](https://ofgod.info/name) our God, [Yahweh](https://ofgod.info/name) is one *[echad]*!" — Deuteronomy 6:4 (LSB)

[**יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ יְהוָה אֶחָד**](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0506.htm) also translated as “the LORD our God, the LORD is one” or “the LORD alone”. The command that follows tells Israel to fear and serve the LORD rather than other gods, and warns against following them. This is monotheism: *belief in 1 God*, not several gods agreeing with one another.

The same book of Isaiah makes the exclusion plain: [“there is no other besides Him”](https://www.sefaria.org/api/texts/Deuteronomy.4.35?context=0) (Deuteronomy 4:35), [“there is no other”](https://www.sefaria.org/api/texts/Deuteronomy.4.39?context=0) in heaven or earth (Deuteronomy 4:39), ["the LORD your God in your midst is a jealous God"](https://www.sefaria.org/api/texts/Deuteronomy.6.13-15?context=0) (Deuteronomy 6:13-15) and [“there is no god beside Me”](https://www.sefaria.org/api/texts/Deuteronomy.32.39?context=0) (Deuteronomy 32:39). These words directly rule out rival gods.

In the the book of Mark, Jesus quoted Deuteronomy 6:4:

> And Jesus answered him, The first of all the commandments is, Hear, O Israel; The Lord our God is one *[heis]* Lord. — Mark 12:29 (KJV)

The Greek [**κύριος ὁ θεὸς ἡμῶν κύριος εἷς ἐστίν**](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DMark%3Achapter%3D12%3Averse%3D29) means “the Lord our God, the Lord is one”. The scribe answers, [**εἷς ἐστιν καὶ οὐκ ἔστιν ἄλλος πλὴν αὐτοῦ**](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DMark%3Achapter%3D12%3Averse%3D32), “he is one, and **there is no other besides Him**” (Mark 12:32).

The scribe added his understanding that “there is no other besides him” which implies that he understood that the "one" of the Shema meant numerical 1 and excludes another God. Jesus did not correct the scribe (Mark 12:29-34). That confession still does not reduce everything Mark says about Jesus to one word. Mark also raises questions about the Messiah's status in Mark 12:35-37 and 14:61-62. The question here is narrower: readers should not confuse the one God without rivals with the unity that can exist among multiple persons.

Some people argue that *echad* in Deuteronomy means that "only" the LORD is God. However, there is a different Hebrew word which specifically means "only" or "alone", called *[bad](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/905.htm)* for example:

> So now, O LORD our God, save us from his hand, that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you alone *[bad](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/905.htm)* are the LORD.” — Isaiah 37:20 (ESV)

### Greek: *heis*, *mia*, and *hen*

The following table compares the Hebrew and Greek terms used in Scripture for "one" or "alone," along with their definitions and Biblical occurrences:

| Language | Word                                         | Definition  | Examples                                     | Usage                         |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------- | ----------- | -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Greek    | [heis](https://biblehub.com/greek/1520.htm)  | numerical 1 | Mark 12:29,32; Galatians 3:20, 1 Timothy 2:5 | Numerical 1 God               |
| Greek    | [monos](https://biblehub.com/greek/3441.htm) | alone       | 1 Timothy 1:17                               | Only God                      |
| Greek    | [hen](https://biblehub.com/greek/1520.htm)   | unity or 1  | John 10:30; John 17                          | Unity of Jesus and the Father |

Greek [εἷς, *heis*](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/heis) is masculine, μία, *mia*, is feminine, and ἕν, *hen*, is neuter. These are grammatical forms of the same numeral, as the [Greek lexical entry](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/g1520/kjv/tr/0-1/) confirms.

#### Numerical 1

[Mark 12:29,32](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=Mark.12.29,32), uses *heis* in the Shema and pairs it with “no other”. [1 Timothy 2:5](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=1Tim.2.5) calls God one and the mediator between God and humanity one; it counts God and the mediator in their respective statements. [Galatians 3:20](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=Gal.3.20) says that God is one in an argument about a mediator, whose precise interpretation is disputed. These uses show that *heis* can express numerical oneness; they do not by themselves resolve every theological question.

#### Alone

Greek also has [μόνος, *monos*](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/g3441/kjv/tr/0-1/), “only” or “alone”. [1 Timothy 1:17](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=1Tim.1.17) calls God the only God. As with Hebrew *bad*, this is explicit exclusive language rather than a different meaning hidden in the numeral.

#### Unity

The neuter form *hen* in John 10:30 and John 17 can suit a statement about one thing or unity. When Jesus said, [**ἐγὼ καὶ ὁ πατὴρ ἕν ἐσμεν**](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=John.10.30), “I and the Father **are** one” (John 10:30) he named two subjects. “are” is plural. The neuter predicate ἕν says that they are one in some respect, not that Jesus and the Father are the same person. Greek grammar distinguishes Jesus from the Father and the surrounding words explain their unity.

The setting is the sheep's safety. No one can take them from Jesus's hand or the Father's hand (John 10:28-30). Their shared work and protection explain why Jesus can speak of unity. His opponents accuse him of blasphemy in John 10:33. Jesus answers by referring to Scripture's use of “gods” in John 10:34-36, then says that the Father sanctified and sent him and that he called himself the Son of God. He points to his works in John 10:37-38 and speaks of mutual indwelling; see the [surrounding Greek passage](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=John.10.28-39). This response does not make the accusation the final explanation of his words, nor does it erase Jesus's exalted authority. It keeps the Father and the Son distinct while showing their close unity in work and protection.

The Trinitarian creed also distinguish the Father and Son as persons. Their claim is that the distinct persons share one divine being or essence, not that God is composite or made of three parts (partialism) or that the Father is the Son (modalism).

The disagreement is therefore not whether Father and Son are the same person, but whether their unity identifies Jesus as sharing the Father's deity. John 10:30 itself states unity, but this unity was never defined as a shared deity. In the context of John 14-17 Jesus made it clear that [this unity means shared life, truth, authority, mission, love, glory and witness](../jesus-relationships.md).

John 17 gives readers Jesus's own comparison. He prays that the disciples **ἵνα ... ἓν ὦσιν** (“that ... they may be one”), “just as we are one”, **καθὼς ἡμεῖς ἕν**, and that they may be perfected **εἰς ἕν** (“into one”) (John 17:11,21-23). The [SBL Greek text provides the full context](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=John.17.11,21-23).

The disciples do not become one person or one God, nor does the disciples *"share a divine nature"*. They remain many people, yet Jesus calls them into unity through the Father's name, His glory, the word, love, mutual indwelling, witness before the world, and sanctification.

John's Gospel, often called Johannine when discussing its language and themes, gives a fuller picture. Jesus **receives** significant honour, authority, life, and judgement in John 5:19-23. Yet He says that he does what he sees the Father doing, can **do nothing from himself**, and **seeks the Father's will** (John 5:30) which implies he has naturually a different will or mind. He names the Father who **sent** him as another witness, making **two witnesses** in John 8:16-18, and says, “the Father is **greater** than I” (John 14:28). Above all, he addresses the Father as “**the only** true God” and identifies himself as the one **sent by the Father** (John 17:3). The [SBL Greek passages are available here](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version=SBLG%7Creference=John.5.19-23,30;John.8.16-18;14.28;17.3).

Classical Trinitarian teaching reads these distinctions alongside Jesus's shared authority and glory, affirming one divine essence and three distinct persons.

## Conclusion

Readers can draw three conclusions from the [words for one](#words-for-one):

1. The Father is the one true God, with no rival beside him.
2. Believers are called to be one united body in love, truth, work, and witness, while remaining many people.
3. The Father and Jesus are united, but they are not the same God under the scriptural case set out here: the Father is the only true God, and Jesus is his sent Son.

“One” must be read in its sentence and setting, not made into a dictionary-only proof. The Scripture passages considered here identify one God, the Father, and distinguish Jesus from him while showing their profound unity. For that reason, the Trinity is rejected.
